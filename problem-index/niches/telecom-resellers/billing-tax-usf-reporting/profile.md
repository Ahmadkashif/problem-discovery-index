# Telecom Billing, Tax & USF Revenue Reporting

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/telecom-resellers|Telecom Resellers]]
**Category:** Highly Automatable
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to classify every billed dollar the way USAC's auditors and state tax administrators will read it (interstate or intrastate, end-user or reseller, taxable or not) and reuse that one classification across the invoice, the tax return and Form 499, and whoever does it once and consistently takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.5B a year in telecom billing and tax software, regulatory consultants and in-house staff time at resellers and VoIP providers (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~2.5% of a ~$60B resale economy (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — invoices and tax calculation are automated; Form 499 preparation, reseller certificates and recovery-fee setting run on spreadsheets and consultants
**Target Buyer:** Controller or Regulatory & Tax Manager at a reseller or VoIP provider
**Automation Potential:** High

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Taxes, fees and surcharges are now 28.8% of an average wireless bill (Tax Foundation, 2026), with the federal USF surcharge at 14.39% on its own. The proposed contribution factor for Q4 2026 is 42.0% of assessable revenue, the second record quarter in a row. A reseller files Form 499-Q four times a year and Form 499-A each April 1, and pays USAC monthly on its projections. It must hold an annual certificate from every reseller customer it exempts, and it files communications taxes and 911 fees across state and local jurisdictions. The rules are codified and the volume is high, which makes the niche automatable. The cost of an inconsistent classification across these filings makes it contested.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Telecom billing platforms (Rev.io and others), communications tax engines (Avalara for Communications, CCH SureTax), USAC's E-File and reseller certificate template, and consultants who prepare filings. The gaps: tax codes and 499 lines mapped separately; 499-Q projections guessed and trued up later; reseller certificates collected by email and left to expire; and recovery fees set without reconciling to what was actually owed.
