# A Quota That Ignores the Item

**Niche:** [[niches/recommerce-platforms/the-intake-processor/profile|The Intake Processor]]
**Industry:** [[industries/recommerce-platforms|Recommerce Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The quota is items per hour regardless of what the items are, so a processor spends the same ninety seconds on a twelve-dollar shirt and a four-hundred-dollar coat, and the pressure falls hardest where the value is.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #revenue-impact #evaluation-metrics #convex-optimization #descriptive-statistics #confidence-intervals #quick-win #workflow-orchestration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to give the person making the platform's most economically consequential judgements the support and the feedback to make them well — and whoever does that takes the quality, because the whole model's economics are decided at that station.

## The Problem
The quota is two hundred and forty items a shift. A plain shirt takes forty seconds and a structured coat with complex condition, a designer label to verify and eight photographs takes four minutes. The processor, measured on the count, hurries the coat — fewer photographs, a quicker grade, an approximate category — because the coat is what is costing them their number. The item where care would have returned the most gets the least, systematically, across every shift. The operation has designed an incentive that inverts its own economics and reports a throughput figure that looks stable.

## Why It's Still Broken
Items per hour is the warehouse's natural metric and is easy to measure and manage. Weighting the quota requires knowing each item's difficulty and value at the moment it is picked up, which felt unavailable. Processors adapt to the metric they are given, which is what metrics do. And the consequence appears as lower realised prices on high-value items, which is attributed to the market.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Weight the quota by the item. Assign a time allowance per item from its category, expected value and complexity, and measure against that rather than against a flat count, which is a straightforward change once the value estimate exists and removes an incentive currently working against the business — this is the fix. Give high-value items explicitly more time and require more of them, since the additional care returns more than it costs and the current structure forbids it. Route complex items to processors with the relevant expertise rather than to whoever is next, which is both faster and better. Measure the operation on realised value per hour rather than on items per hour, which is the metric that aligns with the economics and is computable. Let a processor stop and escalate without it counting against them, since the item they are unsure about is the one worth pausing on. Report grade and listing quality by value band, which will show the current inversion clearly and is the evidence for the change. Balance the shift mix so a processor is not given a run of complex items against a flat target. And involve processors in setting the allowances, since they know how long things take and are the only people who do.

## Who Feels the Pain
Processors hurrying the items that matter most to hit a number; high-value sellers whose goods were processed in ninety seconds; and platforms losing realised value to an incentive they designed.

## Impact If Fixed
A flat count inverts the economics by putting the most pressure where the value is, and the item-weighted allowance removes it. Realised value per hour is computable and is the metric that aligns the operation with the business it serves.
