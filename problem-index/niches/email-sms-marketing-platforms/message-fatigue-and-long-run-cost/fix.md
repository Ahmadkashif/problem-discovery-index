# The Frequency Cap Somebody Chose

**Niche:** [[niches/email-sms-marketing-platforms/message-fatigue-and-long-run-cost/profile|Message Fatigue & Long-Run Cost]]
**Industry:** [[industries/email-sms-marketing-platforms|Email & SMS Marketing Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The frequency cap is three a week because that is what the previous agency used, it applies to every segment identically, and it has never been tested.
**Tags:** #hypothesis-testing #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #quick-win #revenue-impact #survival-analysis #causal-inference
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to price what an extra message costs in the recipient's future willingness to hear from a brand — and whoever measures that changes how much the whole channel sends.

## The Problem
Three messages a week. That number came from a previous agency, or a blog post, or a conversation in the first month of the programme. It applies to the customer who buys monthly and the one who bought once two years ago, to the segment that reads everything and the segment that has not clicked since the sign-up. It has never been tested against anything. It is simultaneously the most consequential parameter in the programme and the least examined, and when revenue targets are tight it gets raised by a conversation rather than by evidence, exactly as it was set.

## Why It's Still Broken
The cap is a setting rather than a parameter, and settings inherited at setup are never revisited — the same pattern that produced the ad load problem next door in retail media. Testing it means deliberately sending less to a cohort, which reduces measurable revenue now for a benefit that appears later. The cost of being wrong is diffuse. And nobody owns the number.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Test it and vary it. Run frequency variation cohorts held long enough to see the effect, which is the fix and is cheap at a small allocation for a platform that can run it across many brands at once. Set frequency per segment rather than globally, since tolerance differs enormously and one cap is simultaneously too high for most and too low for the engaged. Use engagement recency and purchase behaviour to set an individual cap, which is a straightforward personalisation on data already held and outperforms any global number. Report the trade-off at each frequency — revenue, unsubscribes, engagement decay, placement effect — so raising it is a decision with a stated cost rather than a conversation. Measure the effect on subsequent months rather than on the campaign, because the entire cost of the parameter is delayed and campaign-window measurement is structurally incapable of seeing it. Include the placement consequence, since a frequency that drives disengagement degrades delivery for the whole list. Review it on a schedule, as audiences and tolerance change. Give the marketer a recommended cap with its evidence, since they will otherwise inherit the last one. Distinguish promotional from transactional and triggered messages, which are tolerated very differently and are usually capped together. And report actual received frequency rather than the cap, because the cap is a policy and the received load is what the recipient experiences.

## Who Feels the Pain
Recipients over-messaged into unsubscribing; brands raising a number they have never tested when targets are tight; and lifecycle teams whose most consequential parameter is inherited.

## Impact If Fixed
The cap is a setting inherited at setup and never revisited, while being the most consequential parameter in the programme. Per-segment variation cohorts are cheap for a platform running them across many brands, and a stated trade-off turns raising it into a decision.
