# Fix: An Hour of Their Time and Nothing Back

**Niche:** [[niches/talent-assessment-platforms/the-candidate/profile|The Candidate]]
**Industry:** [[industries/talent-assessment-platforms|Talent Assessment Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The assessment says thirty minutes, takes fifty, and ends with a page thanking them for their time.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #compliance #workflow-orchestration #worker-facing #quick-win #automation
**Contested on:** Whether the candidate will be told the truth about the time and given anything at the end.

## The Problem

A candidate is invited to an assessment described as taking about thirty minutes. They set aside forty. It takes fifty-five, because the published estimate was optimistic, because instructions and practice items are not counted, and because the estimate came from the median of a fast population.

They finish late, having rushed the final section or having missed something else they had scheduled. The final screen thanks them. There is no result, no indication of what happens next, and no timeline.

Weeks later a rejection email arrives with no reason, or nothing arrives at all.

Each of these is a small failure and together they are the reason assessment is consistently the worst-rated stage of a hiring process and the reason abandonment is high among exactly the candidates an employer would most want.

## Why It's Still Broken

The time estimate is a marketing number — a shorter one improves invitation acceptance — and nobody is accountable for its accuracy even though the vendor has the exact distribution.

The closing screen was designed as a confirmation, not as a communication, and nobody has revisited it.

And the rejection template belongs to the applicant tracking system rather than to the assessment vendor, so neither party owns the experience of the outcome.

## What a Fix Looks Like

Tell the truth about the time and give something back at the end. All of it is available.

Publish an honest estimate from the actual completion distribution — median and upper quartile, including instructions and practice items. "Most people take 45–60 minutes" is accurate, sets expectations correctly, and is a field lookup.

Show progress meaningfully. Sections completed, sections remaining, and an updating time estimate based on their own pace, so a candidate can decide whether to continue or pause.

Allow a pause and resume where the instrument permits it. Many do not require a single sitting and enforce one anyway.

Say what happens next on the closing screen. Who will contact them, by when, and what the next stage is. This costs nothing and its absence is the most common complaint in every candidate experience survey of this stage.

Give a reason at rejection, including when the assessment was the cause. A stage and a band, not a personalised critique. Several regulatory directions are moving here and doing it voluntarily is cheaper than doing it under requirement.

Provide a route for problems, findable during the assessment and not only afterwards. A technical failure with no way to report it is a rejection for a reason the candidate did not cause.

And report abandonment by stage to the employer, so the cost of a fifty-five-minute assessment described as thirty is visible to the person who chose it.

## Who Feels the Pain

Candidates, giving an unpaid hour on a false estimate and receiving nothing, repeatedly, across every application. Candidates with less schedule flexibility, caring responsibilities or hourly work, for whom the overrun is not a minor annoyance. Employers, losing candidates at this stage and not knowing which ones. And the vendor, whose product is the worst-rated part of their client's process.

## Impact If Fixed

The time estimate becomes true, which is a field lookup and removes the most common complaint. The candidate knows what happens next. Rejections carry a reason. And the employer finds out how many people they are losing at the stage they chose to include.
