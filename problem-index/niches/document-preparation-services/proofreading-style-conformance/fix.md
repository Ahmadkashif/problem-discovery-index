# The Edit That Changed the Meaning

**Niche:** [[niches/document-preparation-services/proofreading-style-conformance/profile|Proofreading & Style-Guide Conformance]]
**Industry:** [[industries/document-preparation-services|Document Preparation Services]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** One well-meant rewording of a defined term or a qualifier in hundreds of accepted edits can change a contract, a grant commitment or a clinical statement, and the client finds it later.
**Tags:** #bert #transformers #evaluation-metrics #compliance #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to bring a document into line with a named style guide while changing nothing the guide doesn't require, and whoever returns a change log the client can accept without rereading the document takes the account.

## The Problem
Proofreaders are asked to correct, not rewrite, but the line blurs: "may" tightened to "will", "approximately" dropped, a defined term replaced with a synonym, a hedge removed from a clinical sentence. Clients accept all changes because re-reading defeats the purpose of hiring an editor, and the meaning change surfaces when it matters.

## Why It's Still Broken
Tracked changes show what changed, not whether the meaning changed. Editors under deadline don't classify their own edits.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Classify every tracked change as mechanical (spelling, punctuation, style rule), stylistic, or potentially meaning-changing: modal verbs, quantities, negations, defined terms, hedges. Group the meaning-risk edits in a short list for the client or a second editor to confirm before acceptance.

## Who Feels the Pain
Clients in legal, regulatory, grant and medical writing; editors whose reputation rests on not introducing errors.

## Impact If Fixed
Clients review a short list instead of the whole document, and the editor's worst errors are caught before they ship.
