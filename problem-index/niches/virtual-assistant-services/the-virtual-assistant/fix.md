# Fix: Nobody Knows What They Are Billed At

**Niche:** [[niches/virtual-assistant-services/the-virtual-assistant/profile|The Virtual Assistant]]
**Industry:** [[industries/virtual-assistant-services|Virtual Assistant Services]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The assistant is paid an hourly rate and has no idea what the client pays for their hour, which removes any basis for a conversation about the split.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #compliance #data-integration #worker-facing #quick-win #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Whether the billing rate will be disclosed to the person whose work is being billed.

## The Problem

An assistant is paid a rate. The client is billed a rate. The assistant does not know the second number, and in many arrangements is contractually discouraged from discussing rates with the client at all.

A margin between the two is a normal agency structure and entirely legitimate — the agency recruits, vets, matches, manages, guarantees replacement, handles payroll across borders and carries the client relationship. What is unusual is that the party providing the labour has no visibility of the number and therefore no way to assess whether the split reflects that value or simply reflects their lack of alternatives.

The information gap compounds the others. Without the billing rate, and without a portable record, an assistant negotiating a raise has nothing to argue from except the length of time they have been there.

## Why It's Still Broken

Disclosure reduces margin, straightforwardly, in a market where the margin is the business. An assistant who knows the billing rate has a starting point for a negotiation that currently has none.

There is also a genuine complication that gets used more broadly than it deserves: the billing rate covers real costs the assistant does not see, and a raw comparison of the two numbers misrepresents the agency's economics. That argues for disclosing the rate alongside an explanation of what it covers, not for disclosing nothing.

And the norm is inherited from staffing generally, where margin opacity is standard, without anyone examining whether it holds up in an arrangement with this much of the relationship resting on trust.

## What a Fix Looks Like

Disclose the rate, or at minimum the structure, and let the market information exist.

The direct version: tell the assistant the billing rate for their placement, with a plain explanation of what the agency's share covers — recruitment, management, guarantee, payroll, the client relationship. Several agencies in adjacent staffing markets do this and report that it improves retention rather than triggering demands, because most people's objection is to being kept in the dark rather than to the existence of a margin.

The partial version: publish the margin range as a policy — the agency's share is typically thirty to fifty percent depending on the service level — without per-placement disclosure. This is a policy statement and it removes most of the resentment at a fraction of the commercial exposure.

Drop the prohibition on discussing rates where it exists. A contractual bar on two people discussing what the arrangement between them costs is corrosive, and in some jurisdictions it is on uncertain legal ground.

Publish a pay scale. Rates by experience, capability and market, with the criteria for moving up them. Most agencies set rates individually and opaquely, which means identical assistants are paid differently based on what they asked for — a pattern that reliably disadvantages the same groups.

And support the market reference rather than opposing it. Assistants already exchange rate information in community groups, imperfectly. An agency that pays well benefits from accurate market information; only one that pays badly is served by the fog.

## Who Feels the Pain

Assistants, negotiating without any information about the thing they are negotiating over, and discovering the billing rate eventually through a client's offhand remark — which is the worst possible way to learn it and reliably ends the placement. Clients, who assume more of what they pay reaches the person doing the work. And agencies, whose retention problem is partly built out of a resentment that disclosure would largely dissolve.

## Impact If Fixed

The person doing the work knows what the work is sold for, which is the minimum basis for a conversation about the split. Rate setting becomes a published scale rather than a private negotiation, which removes the arbitrary differences it currently produces. And the discovery that ends placements — hearing the rate from a client by accident — stops happening.
