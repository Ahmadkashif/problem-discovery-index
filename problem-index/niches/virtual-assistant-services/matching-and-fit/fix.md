# Fix: Nobody Asks How the Executive Actually Works

**Niche:** [[niches/virtual-assistant-services/matching-and-fit/profile|Matching & Placement Fit]]
**Industry:** [[industries/virtual-assistant-services|Virtual Assistant Services]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The intake form asks what tasks to delegate and never asks how the executive briefs, how much autonomy they grant, or what they do when something goes wrong.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #workflow-orchestration #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #quick-win #worker-facing #tacit-knowledge-ml
**Contested on:** Whether ten minutes of the right questions will be added to an intake that currently asks the wrong ones.

## The Problem

A client signs up and completes an intake. It asks which tasks they want handled, how many hours they need, which timezone, which tools they use, and what experience they require.

It does not ask how they brief — in detail or in a sentence. Whether they want the assistant to ask before acting or to act and report. How they prefer to communicate and how often. What they do when something is done wrong. Whether they have delegated before and how it went. What they mean when they say "handle it".

These are the dimensions on which placements fail. Task skill is rarely the problem; a mismatch between an executive who briefs in half-sentences and an assistant who needs specifics is a problem in week one and a replacement in month four.

## Why It's Still Broken

The intake was designed to get to a placement quickly, and the questions it asks are the ones a client can answer without thinking. Asking an executive how they grant autonomy requires them to reflect, which is friction at exactly the point the agency is trying to convert.

The questions are also uncomfortable in a small way. "What do you do when an assistant makes a mistake" invites an honest answer that some executives would rather not give, and the salesperson conducting the intake is not positioned to ask it.

And nobody has established that these dimensions matter, because placement outcomes have never been analysed. The intake asks about tasks because tasks are what the service appears to be about.

## What a Fix Looks Like

Add ten minutes of the right questions, and use the answers.

Ask about briefing style: do you prefer to explain in detail once, or give a short instruction and clarify as you go. Both are legitimate and they need different assistants.

Ask about autonomy: should the assistant act and inform you, or check first. Where is the line. This single question predicts more mismatches than the entire task list.

Ask about errors: what happens when something goes wrong, what would you want the assistant to do, what would frustrate you most. Executives answer this honestly more often than expected and the answer is highly informative.

Ask about communication: how often, through what channel, at what hours, and how quickly you expect a response. Timezone is asked; the expectation attached to it usually is not.

Ask about prior delegation: have you had an assistant before, what worked, what did not. The most predictive question available and frequently omitted.

Then profile the assistant on the same dimensions, so the match can actually be made on them. Most agencies assess assistants on skills and English and not on how they work.

And record the answers as structured fields rather than notes, so that when placement outcomes are eventually analysed there is something to analyse. This is the step that turns ten minutes of questions into an asset.

## Who Feels the Pain

Executives who receive an assistant who cannot work the way they work, and who conclude that delegation does not suit them. Assistants placed into relationships they were never suited to, who are judged as underperforming and replaced. Account managers, who mediate a mismatch nobody screened for. And agencies, paying the replacement cost for a question they did not ask.

## Impact If Fixed

The dimensions that actually determine whether a placement works get asked about before the placement is made. Both sides get profiled on the same axes, which is what makes matching possible at all. And the answers accumulate as structured data, which is what eventually lets the agency learn from its own placements instead of repeating a recruiter's afternoon.
