# Niche Analysis — Security Alarm Integrators

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/security-alarm-integrators|Security Alarm Integrators]]

## Niche Selection

Security alarm integrators sell a promise of response and finance it as recurring monthly revenue. Around that sit four kinds of work: deciding in seconds whether a signal is real, keeping accounts paying long enough to recover their acquisition cost, keeping commercial and fire systems working and compliant, and handling the flood of signals that are not alarms at all. The eight niches below split the industry by what competitors actually fight over. Market sizes are estimates of the revenue or spend each niche governs within the $29.67B of 2022 industry receipts (Census SUSB). They overlap where an audience niche cuts across a function, as small dealers do across residential.

## Why These Niches

Residential monitoring is the largest pool, about half the industry's receipts by estimate, and its defining problem is attrition: ADT's gross revenue attrition reached 13.1% in 2025. Commercial access and video integration is second, and its money is in the recurring service and hosted subscriptions that follow the install. Fire alarm ITM and alarm permitting are the two low-digitized niches. In the first, NFPA 72 tests still travel as PDFs to fire marshals who now demand them through portals. In the second, thousands of local ordinances decide whether police will answer, tracked in spreadsheets if at all. The underserved audiences are the 4,980 small dealers whose book of accounts is their retirement and who never see it valued until they sell, and older adults on personal emergency response systems, served by tools designed for burglary. The two automatable niches are the most mechanical parts of central-station work: verifying camera events at a cost per camera below a guard, and clearing the power-loss, low-battery and communication troubles that make up most station traffic.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. Commercial Access Control & Video Integration is not. Its sentence came out as two contests with different winners. In an office campus, hospital or data centre, access control is part of the client's identity system, and the integrator that keeps cardholders and doors in step with HR and IT wins. In a national retail, bank or restaurant programme, the integrator that proves hundreds of sites healthy at the lowest cost per site wins. The buyers differ (corporate security with the CISO, against loss prevention with facilities). So do the data (identity records against device telemetry and subcontractor tickets) and the clocks (audit cycles against incident-driven renewals). It decomposes into **Access Control & Credential Lifecycle** and **Multi-Site National Account Service Programmes**.

Two close calls are recorded. **Fire Alarm ITM** borders [[niches/facilities-management-firms/statutory-inspection-compliance/profile|Statutory Inspection & Life-Safety Compliance]] under Facilities Management Firms. It is kept here because the contest is the integrator's own: being both inspector and repairer, and converting deficiencies to repairs before the AHJ's deadline. The FM niche governs the owner's portfolio calendar. **Trouble-signal handling** could fold into video monitoring as "central-station automation", but the two have different winners. Trouble handling is won on signals per operator hour under UL 827. Video is won on time to a verified event and cost per camera. Alarm verification itself, the dispatch-or-cancel judgement, is the industry's high-impact problem rather than a niche, because every niche with a central station shares it.

Four candidates were rejected. **DIY self-monitored security** (SimpliSafe, Ring) is real, but its sentence came out as "sell more cameras through retail at a lower price", a generic consumer-electronics contest that fails the filter. Where those brands sell professional monitoring, they are inside Residential. **Door-to-door residential sales** was considered as a low-digitized niche, but its contest is acquisition cost per account. That is the same contest as Residential, from the other side of the ledger. **Elevator emergency phone and alarm line monitoring** was passed here by [[industries/answering-services|Telephone Answering Services]]. It is line-supervision traffic and sits inside Trouble & Supervisory Signal Handling, while its copper replacement belongs to [[niches/telecom-resellers/pots-line-replacement/profile|Legacy POTS Line Replacement]]. **Low-voltage cabling and structured wiring** belongs to [[industries/electrical-contractors|Electrical Contractors]], and lockouts, rekeying and safe work to [[industries/locksmiths|Locksmiths]].

## Why These Pockets

No pocket qualifies, and the operators fail the gate first. A dealer sells installs and monitoring, a central station sells signal handling, and an integrator sells projects and service. Their staff are technicians, operators and salespeople, all production, and none invoices analysis. The central stations' own analytics are already profiled under Security Guard Firms as [[niches/security-guard-firms/alarm-monitoring-analytics/profile|🔍 Alarm Monitoring Central Station Analytics]] (41) and are not profiled again. That pocket names the industry's defining asset: hundreds of millions of alarm events with a disposition attached, which nobody grades against an outcome. It passes the gate only by counting 200–1,000 operations and quality staff across stations. Central stations are operators of NAICS 561621, this industry, so that pocket is misfiled under guards and its headcount is production; it is logged for Stage E rather than re-scored here. The organisations around the operators split two ways. Those holding rich data hold it for someone else, or price it into something other than an invoice. Interactive platforms (35) see 170 million devices on dealers' and subscribers' behalf; Alarm.com's terms reserve aggregate use only to improve its own products, which is a vendor product right rather than a live kill switch, but the insight is a SaaS feature, not an invoice. Insurers (30) join device enrolment to claims and price the result into premium. Dealer programmes (29) and rollups (26) hold the best record of which accounts survive and use it to buy accounts. Those that do sell insight are small. Market research firms (34) invoice their surveys, but their teams are a few dozen people in a market of a few firms, on calendars they set themselves.

The most instructive gate failures are the organisations that hold a missing label. Police alarm units and 911 centres hold the clearance codes that would say whether a dispatched alarm was real, and each is one to a few people. Fire marshals' reporting platforms hold the cross-jurisdiction record of which fire alarm systems fail inspection and how fast deficiencies are fixed. Their staff are reviewers, and nobody analyses that record. City false-alarm billing vendors match dispatches to permits for a share of the fines and employ clerks, not analysts. The standards side is already in the vault. NFPA, which publishes NFPA 72, is indexed under Electrical Contractors as [[niches/electrical-contractors/code-standards-development-bodies/profile|🔍 Electrical Code & Standards Development Bodies]] (54). The product-certification side of UL and its peers is logged there as [[niches/electrical-contractors/electrical-product-certification/profile|🔍 Electrical Product Certification Bodies]] (48, kill switch). Fire protection code consultancies such as Jensen Hughes are logged under Architecture Firms as [[niches/architecture-firms/building-fire-code-consultants/profile|🔍 Building & Fire Code Consultants]] (49). Security design and risk consultancies, which write the specifications integrators bid against, are logged as [[niches/security-guard-firms/security-risk-consulting/profile|🔍 Security Risk & Threat Assessment Consulting]] (45, kill switch). Video analytics vendors that sell AI filtering to remote-guarding stations appear as [[niches/security-guard-firms/video-analytics-vendors/profile|🔍 Video Analytics & Security Technology Vendors]] (39, kill switch). Crime risk data, which commercial integrators use in site assessments, is [[niches/security-guard-firms/crime-risk-data-providers/profile|🔍 Crime Risk Data & Forecasting Providers]] (48). State agencies that license alarm companies are largely the same bodies that license guard firms, logged as [[niches/security-guard-firms/state-security-licensing-boards/profile|🔍 State Security Licensing & Regulatory Boards]] (31), and alarm-failure lawsuits draw on the experts in [[niches/security-guard-firms/negligent-security-litigation-support/profile|🔍 Negligent Security Litigation Support]] (43, kill switch). None of these is profiled twice.

There is no clean near-miss. Interactive platforms (35) and market research firms (34) score highest, held back by an invoice that is really a SaaS fee in one case and by team size, self-set calendars and a market of a few firms in the other. The vault's favourite pattern, a dated judgement whose outcome the producer never grades, sits inside the operators. Every dispatch request is a prediction that a crime is in progress, and AVS-01 now makes it a published score. That is a product to sell to stations and their automation vendors, set out in [[problems/security-alarm-integrators/high-impact|The Operator's Read of an Alarm]] and [[niches/security-alarm-integrators/trouble-signal-handling/build|Outage Clusters and Self-Restoring Troubles Recognised Automatically]]. It is not an insight business the stations could sell.
