# Alarm Permits, False-Alarm Fines & Verified-Response Compliance

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/security-alarm-integrators|Security Alarm Integrators]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to keep each customer's alarm eligible for police response under its own city's ordinance (permitted, under the false-alarm limit and verified to the local standard), and whoever keeps the most accounts dispatchable with the fewest fines takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$400M a year in alarm permit fees, false-alarm fines and the dealer and station staff time spent managing them (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~1% of 561621 receipts (estimate), with an outsized effect on attrition
**Digital Adoption:** Low — ordinances, permit forms and fine notices differ city by city, and dealers track them in spreadsheets or not at all
**Target Buyer:** Compliance manager or VP of monitoring operations at a dealer or central station
**Automation Potential:** High — permit registration, rule tracking, fine monitoring and evidence packaging are rules and documents

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Police response to a burglar alarm is a local privilege, governed by thousands of separate ordinances. Many cities require the alarm user to hold a permit, cap the false alarms allowed per year, fine each one above the cap and suspend response after repeated failures. Some, like Seattle since October 2024, respond only to verified alarms. Billing is often outsourced: Public Safety Corporation's CryWolf and PM AM run permit and fine programmes for cities on a share of collections (Reading, Pa. paid 25–35%; Calexico, Calif. weighed bids of 12.3% and 18%). Dealers and stations must know, account by account, which rules apply and whether the customer is still dispatchable. A fine that reaches the customer unexplained is a leading cause of cancellation. This is distinct from the alarm decision itself, which belongs to the station's verification work.

## Current Tools & Gaps
City portals and vendor sites for permits and fines; the automation platform's account notes; dealer CRMs. The gaps: no registry of ordinance rules by jurisdiction, no link between a station's dispatch history and the city's fine count, and no warning before an account hits suspension.
