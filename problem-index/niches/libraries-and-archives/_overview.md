# Niche Analysis — Libraries & Archives

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/libraries-and-archives|Libraries & Archives]]

## Niche Selection

A library builds a collection through decisions it never records — what to buy, license, renew, keep, withdraw or process next — and runs it on a few concentrated platforms. Around that core sit outside clocks: publishers' renewal dates and licence expiries, funder open-access mandates, grant end dates, federal records rules, and since 2023 a wave of public challenges to what libraries hold. The eight niches below split the industry by what vendors and library teams actually compete over. Market sizes are estimates of software, content-licence, services and labour spend within each niche, not of library budgets as a whole.

## Why These Niches

Library services platforms take the first high-share slot because they are the largest software line in a library's budget and the decision a library makes least often; public digital lending takes the second because it is the fastest-growing part of circulation (over 820 million OverDrive loans in 2025, a figure that includes school lending through its Sora app) and a recurring licence bill rather than a one-off purchase. The two low-digitized niches are where boxes and microfilm still run the work: archival processing, where about half of collections had no online presence in OCLC's 2010 survey, and local history digitisation in small institutions. The two underserved audiences are the library with four staff that every product built for large systems ignores, and the special library inside a law firm or company that manages a large content budget with tools built for no one. The two automatable niches are the most mechanical parts of the work: loading the right catalogue record for an item that already has one, and assembling the same renewal analysis for every e-resource package every year.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. Library Services Platforms is not: writing its contested sentence produced two contests with different winners. In academic libraries the platform is chosen on whether it keeps one correct record of licensed electronic resources and print — entitlements, activations and access across every publisher — a contest Alma leads with FOLIO and OCLC chasing. In public libraries it is chosen on circulation, holds and patron service across a shared consortium catalogue and on whether a migration can be survived, where Polaris, Sierra, Symphony, Evergreen and Koha compete and Alma barely plays. Different buyers, data and winners, so it decomposes into **Academic Unified Resource Management** and **Public Library ILS & Consortium Circulation**.

Three terminal calls were close. **E-Resource Renewal & Usage Analytics** overlaps the academic platform sub-niche; it stays separate because its contest is the renew-or-cancel number, which consortia, usage-analysis tools and subscription agents fight over regardless of which platform holds the licence. **Public Library Digital Lending** could split ebooks from streaming video, but one contest — loans per licence dollar under the publisher's terms — governs both. **Archival Processing & Finding Aids** and **Local History & Newspaper Digitisation** both start from paper; they are kept apart because one is won on description and screening speed at university and state archives and the other on per-page cost and rights terms at small institutions, with different competitors (archival software and contract archivists against imaging vendors and genealogy platforms).

Four candidates were considered and rejected. **Resource sharing and interlibrary loan** has a real contest — filling a patron's request fastest across the most lenders — but it is decided by network membership that OCLC and Clarivate's Rapido already hold, and the routing problem inside a consortium appears under the public ILS sub-niche. **Library programming and outreach** was rejected because its contest reduces to easier registration and promotion, a generic "better UX" statement that fails the filter. **RFID and automated materials handling** is a hardware installation contest won on price and service, belonging to equipment suppliers rather than to library work. **School libraries** belong to the K-12 schools that run them: private-school libraries sit under [[industries/k12-private-schools|K-12 Private Schools]], and public-school libraries are not analysed by any built industry. The colleges-and-universities industry in the queue keeps the university's own research administration and accreditation work.

## Pass 2 — Insight-Layer Discovery

Second, orthogonal sweep. This pass sweeps seven positions in the value chain — aggregator/rollup, payer & intermediary, data & benchmark vendors, association research arms, specialist advisory & valuation, regulatory bodies, and suppliers selling into the industry — looking for a dedicated insight function of 10+ people whose analytical output is the billable deliverable. Cataloguers, digitisation technicians, indexers and processing archivists are production headcount and do not count towards the gate. The libraries themselves fail it: the median public library system had 4.0 paid staff in FY 2022, and even large systems and research libraries produce collections and services, not invoiced analysis. ## Why These Pockets

No pocket qualifies, and the libraries themselves fail the gate. That is the finding, not a gap in the sweep. A library is a buyer and steward of other people's content: it holds rich data on what its community borrows and what its faculty read, but it invoices nobody for analysis, and its most valuable data — circulation tied to people — is exactly what state confidentiality laws and professional ethics keep off limits. The insight functions around it are few and concentrated. Citation indexes, review journals and genealogy platforms each come down to three to five organisations, so Q5 sits at 2; subject abstracting databases are a dozen or so producers (3); OCLC is a market of one. And almost nothing in the value chain runs on a deadline set by a third party: renewal dates are contracts, metrics releases are the vendors' own, and the clocks that do exist — funder mandates, federal records rules — bind the libraries rather than the insight producers.

The strongest near-miss is the research intelligence vendors (46). Web of Science and Scopus sell curated citation indexes and journal metrics whose editorial judgements move journals' fortunes, on a corpus nobody can rebuild — the same shape as the chemistry and patent abstracting services scored at 48 elsewhere, and short of the threshold for the same reasons: no outside clock and three producers. The discipline abstracting-and-indexing databases (42) — CINAHL, PsycInfo, Inspec and their peers — share the shape too; their indexing is their own, but it is increasingly bundled into platform subscriptions and competes with free discovery over open metadata. The full-text aggregation businesses that sell those indexes alongside licensed journals are left out of scope, because the full text belongs to publishers. Library review media (37) are the cleanest insight-as-invoice pocket and run against publishers' on-sale dates, but each outlet has a small staff and freelance reviewers. OCLC (36) holds the richest corpus in the industry and under-uses it for analysis; it is a cooperative selling metadata and software. Only the platform vendors carry a kill switch: they hold library transactions on the libraries' behalf. The consortia that negotiate big deals fail the gate — no consortium licensing team of ten analysts was found.

For an insight engine, this industry is a buyer of decision support, not a seller of insight. The commercial openings sit in Pass 1: the collection decision ledger in the high-impact note, the licence optimiser in public digital lending, and the renewal analytics academic libraries rebuild every year — sold through platforms and consortia, which is where library budgets are pooled.
