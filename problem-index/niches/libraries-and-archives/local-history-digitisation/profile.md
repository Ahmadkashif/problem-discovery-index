# Local History & Newspaper Digitisation

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/libraries-and-archives|Libraries & Archives]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to turn a small institution's newspapers, photographs and records into searchable online collections at a per-page cost a grant can carry, and whoever does it with the fewest strings on the library's rights to its own content takes the collection.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$250M a year in US digitisation of local newspapers, photographs, microfilm and records by public libraries, historical societies and small archives, including grant-funded vendor work (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~1% (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Low — collections sit on microfilm, in bound volumes and in photo drawers; inventories are card files and spreadsheets
**Target Buyer:** Local history librarian or library director at a small or mid-size public library; director of a county historical society; state digital library coordinator
**Automation Potential:** Medium — imaging is mechanical; OCR on historical layouts, rights review and description are the bottlenecks

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
The local history room holds what no one else has: the town newspaper's run, the photo morgue, school yearbooks, church and cemetery records. Digitising them is the most-requested project in many small libraries and the hardest to fund. Three kinds of partner compete for the content: imaging vendors paid from state and federal grants; state digital libraries and their aggregation hubs; and commercial genealogy and newspaper platforms that digitise for free in return for exclusive or time-limited rights to sell access. Audio and video carriers are a separate trade covered under [[niches/recording-studios/archival-tape-transfer/profile|Archival Tape Transfer & Restoration]].

## Current Tools & Gaps
Imaging vendors and in-house scanners, OCR engines, CONTENTdm and Omeka for hosting, state newspaper projects modelled on the Library of Congress's Chronicling America. Gaps: OCR that fails on column layouts and old type; copyright status of each newspaper run unknown; partnership contracts whose exclusivity terms the library does not track; and grant reports due before collections are searchable.
