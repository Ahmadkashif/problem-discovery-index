# OCR Built for Today's Page Layouts

**Niche:** [[niches/libraries-and-archives/local-history-digitisation/profile|Local History & Newspaper Digitisation]]
**Industry:** [[industries/libraries-and-archives|Libraries & Archives]]
**Type:** Buy & Customize (Vertical Adaptation)
**One-liner:** Commercial OCR reads modern invoices and contracts; a 1910 eight-column newspaper page scanned from scratched microfilm defeats it.
**Tags:** #cnns #transformers #semantic-segmentation #transfer-learning #evaluation-metrics #automation
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to turn a small institution's newspapers, photographs and records into searchable online collections at a per-page cost a grant can carry, and whoever does it with the fewest strings on the library's rights to its own content takes the collection.

## The Problem
Digitised newspapers are only useful if they are searchable, and search depends on OCR quality. Small projects accept whatever the scanning vendor's engine produces.

## What Already Exists
ABBYY FineReader and enterprise document AI (Google Document AI, Azure AI Document Intelligence, Amazon Textract), Tesseract, and the workflows of national newspaper programmes.

## The Customization Gap
Historical newspapers need column and article segmentation on dense, irregular layouts; models tuned for period typefaces, broken type and microfilm artefacts; output in the METS/ALTO format newspaper repositories expect; and correction tools that volunteers can use. Generic engines handle none of these well out of the box.

## Target Customer
Local history librarians, state newspaper projects and imaging vendors serving them.

## Impact If Solved
Search that finds a name in a 1910 obituary, which is what most local history users come for.
