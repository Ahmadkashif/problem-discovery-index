# Is Thirty Percent Good

**Niche:** [[niches/game-analytics-vendors/cross-studio-benchmarking/profile|Cross-Studio Benchmarking]]
**Industry:** [[industries/game-analytics-vendors|Game Analytics Vendors]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The studio's day-one retention is thirty percent and nobody in the building can say whether that is good.
**Tags:** #quick-win #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #data-integration #compliance #automation #hypothesis-testing
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to tell a studio whether its numbers are good, which requires seeing comparable games — and whoever turns that corpus into a product takes the account.

## The Problem
Studios operate without reference points. A retention figure, a conversion rate or a session length is reported and nobody knows whether it indicates a problem, a success or an ordinary result for that kind of game. Decisions worth a great deal — whether to keep investing, whether to fix something, whether to launch — are made against numbers found in a conference talk, a blog post of unknown provenance, or the team's previous title.

## Why It's Still Broken
No credible reference exists — a number with no comparison is uninterpretable, and the industry's available comparisons are anecdotes with no methodology attached. The vendor holds the answer and does not publish it. Published figures do not say what they measured or over what population. And nobody has been able to insist.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Assemble the best comparison available and state what it is. Ask the analytics vendor directly for aggregate benchmarks, which is the fix and is a request many will honour and almost nobody makes. Define the metric precisely before comparing, since half the disagreement about benchmarks is definitional. Use the studio's own previous titles as the first comparison, which is imperfect and far better than an unsourced figure. Record the provenance of any external benchmark used, so its weight is known. Compare against the game's own history rather than an absolute where no comparison exists, which at least measures direction reliably. Distinguish platform and acquisition source, as those shift the number more than most differences between games. Note the sample and period behind any figure quoted. Stop citing unsourced industry numbers in decisions, which is a discipline rather than a tool. Push vendors collectively for benchmarks as a contractual expectation, since demand is what creates the product. And say plainly when a number cannot be interpreted rather than interpreting it anyway.

## Who Feels the Pain
Studios investing or abandoning against uninterpretable numbers; analysts asked whether a figure is good; publishers making portfolio decisions on anecdote; and every conversation that cites a number nobody can source.

## Impact If Fixed
A number with no comparison is uninterpretable, and the industry's available comparisons are anecdotes with no methodology. Asking the vendor for aggregates and recording provenance is what replaces the conference-talk figure.
