## The Problem
An operations coordinator built a shift-swap app. Forty people use it. Every week they get six or seven messages: someone cannot find the button, someone's request did not go through, someone deleted a row, someone wants to know what a field means. Each takes a few minutes and arrives while they are doing their actual job. When they are on leave, the messages continue and go unanswered, and people revert to the group chat. Their manager knows the app is useful and does not know it costs its builder several hours a month. The coordinator has started saying no to requests for new apps.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
The category's product thinking ends at creation, and the entire funnel — marketing, onboarding, activation metrics — is built around getting something working for the first time. What happens in month fourteen has no metric and therefore no owner. The load is also invisible by construction, since it arrives as direct messages to an individual and never touches a system that anybody reports on. And the builder is not the buyer, so their experience carries no commercial weight even though their willingness to keep building is what determines whether the platform spreads.

## What to Build
Operability for applications built by people who are not engineers. Documentation generated from the app itself — what each field means, what the automations do, what the views show — since the app is a complete specification and the model can render it in plain language, with the builder editing rather than authoring. In-app help attached to fields and actions, which removes the most common question class outright. Errors explained to the user in terms of what to do, rather than logged for the builder, which is the single change that removes the largest share of the messages. Self-service recovery for the common accidents — restoring a deleted record, resending a failed automation, re-running a stuck step — which the platform can do safely and currently requires the builder. A support path that is not a person: a request queue attached to the app, so questions go somewhere rather than to a colleague's phone, and so the load becomes visible. And an explicit second-owner concept with a handover artefact, so the builder can be on leave. Measuring the load and showing it to management is the piece that makes all of it fundable.

## Target Customer
No-code platform vendors whose expansion depends on builders continuing to build, IT and platform governance functions carrying the consequences when a builder leaves, and the builders themselves.

## Impact If Built
The support burden is the reason experienced builders stop sharing, which caps the platform's spread inside every organisation that adopts it. Errors explained to users and self-service recovery remove most of the volume, and making the load visible is what gets it acknowledged at all.
