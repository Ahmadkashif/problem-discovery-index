# Apprentice Request Letters Nobody Kept

**Niche:** [[niches/renewable-power-producers/tax-credit-qualification-records/profile|Tax-Credit Qualification Records]]
**Industry:** [[industries/renewable-power-producers|Renewable Power Producers]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The good-faith exception that saves a project short of apprentices depends on request letters to registered programmes and their replies, which sit in subcontractors' inboxes and are gone by the time the credits are sold.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #compliance #workflow-orchestration #quick-win #automation
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to evidence every credit dollar that a buyer's diligence and the IRS will test, and whoever has the smallest share of credit value held back or excluded at transfer closing takes the account.

## The Problem
When registered apprenticeship programmes cannot supply apprentices, a contractor that asked in writing and was refused or ignored can rely on the good-faith exception (recalled). Electrical, civil and racking subcontractors send those requests by email and rarely file them with the developer.

## Why It's Still Broken
Nobody owns the requests across subcontractors, the rule's value appears only at diligence, and contractors turn over between projects.

## What a Fix Looks Like
A shared request log: each subcontractor sends requests through it or forwards them to it, replies are captured and dated, and the log maps each request to the craft, period and facility it covers, with reminders before the next request is due.

## Who Feels the Pain
Tax directors defending apprentice percentages; subcontractors asked for letters a year later; buyers pricing the risk.

## Impact If Fixed
A complete request log can turn an apprentice-hour shortfall from a penalty of ~$50 per missing hour (recalled) into no penalty at all, worth ~$100K–$500K on a large solar project short by a few thousand hours (estimate).
