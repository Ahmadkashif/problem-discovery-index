# The Cooperative Contract Nobody Found

**Niche:** [[niches/procurement-spend-platforms/public-sector-procurement/profile|Public Sector Procurement]]
**Industry:** [[industries/procurement-spend-platforms|Procurement & Spend Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** An agency can frequently buy off another public body's competitively awarded contract instead of running its own solicitation, and finding an applicable one requires knowing it exists, so agencies run redundant solicitations for things already competitively priced.
**Tags:** #bert #word-embeddings #k-nearest-neighbors #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #data-integration #automation #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in public procurement software is fighting to let an agency run a compliant, protest-resistant solicitation without a specialist assembling it from precedent — and whoever makes compliance a property of the process takes the account.

## The Problem
A municipality needs to buy grounds maintenance equipment. Three cooperative purchasing organisations and two state contracts already hold competitively awarded agreements for exactly this category, any of which the municipality could use, at prices reflecting far larger aggregate volume. Finding them requires knowing which cooperatives the municipality has joined, searching each one's contract listing separately with inconsistent categorisation, and determining whether the scope covers what is needed. The procurement officer, who has eleven other solicitations in progress, runs her own — which takes six weeks, costs staff time, attracts three local bidders and produces a worse price.

## Why It's Still Broken
Cooperative contracts are published by many separate organisations with no common catalogue, no shared categorisation and no search that spans them. Each cooperative has an interest in its own contracts being found and none in a comprehensive index. Agencies join several and track their memberships informally. And the procurement officer's default is to run a solicitation because that is the process they know is compliant, whereas establishing that a cooperative contract is properly usable requires a determination they may not be confident making.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Build the index and make it searchable by need rather than by category label. Aggregate the published contract listings across cooperatives, state contracts and other agencies' awarded contracts that permit piggybacking, normalised into a common classification — which is the same classification problem as the first niche in this industry and is solvable the same way. Search by what the agency actually wants to buy, in plain language, rather than by navigating each organisation's taxonomy. Surface the applicable ones at the moment a requisition or solicitation is started, which is the intervention point, with the agency's own memberships and eligibility checked. Include the usability determination: what the contract covers, what it costs, whether this agency may use it under its own rules and the contract's terms, and what documentation the file needs — since the determination is the officer's real barrier. And report what the alternative would have cost, which is the number that makes the case and which nobody currently computes.

## Who Feels the Pain
Procurement officers running redundant solicitations they had no time for; agencies paying more than an existing competitively awarded contract would have cost; and the public, which funds both the process and the price difference.

## Impact If Fixed
Cooperative contracts exist specifically to prevent this duplication and are underused because of a discovery problem with an ordinary solution. Surfacing applicable contracts at the moment a solicitation is being started, with the eligibility determination attached, removes both the search cost and the confidence barrier — and the avoided solicitation is six weeks of a scarce officer's time as well as a better price.
