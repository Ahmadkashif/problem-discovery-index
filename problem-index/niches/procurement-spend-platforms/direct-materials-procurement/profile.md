# Direct Materials Procurement

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/procurement-spend-platforms|Procurement & Spend Platforms]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in direct materials software is fighting to get an engineering change into sourcing before it reaches production — and whoever closes the loop between design and supply takes the manufacturer.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.2B US software spend across direct materials sourcing and supply management at manufacturers
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~13% of procurement software revenue
**Digital Adoption:** Low-Medium — the ERP handles transactions and the sourcing side runs on spreadsheets and email
**Target Buyer:** Supply chain, commodity management and engineering leaders at manufacturers
**Automation Potential:** High — the design-to-source chain is a data flow that is currently a set of emailed drawings

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Direct materials — the components and raw materials that go into a product — behave nothing like indirect spend. The requirement originates in engineering rather than in a requisition: a part is designed, specified in a bill of materials, and must then be sourced against drawings, tolerances, qualification requirements and sometimes tooling investment. Changes originate in engineering too, and an engineering change that reaches production before it reaches sourcing produces obsolete inventory, an unqualified supplier or a line stoppage. The cost structure is different — a direct part's cost is a large multiple of its purchase price once tooling, qualification, quality and change management are counted — and the supplier relationship is long, technical and frequently single-source by necessity. The source-to-pay suites were built for indirect spend and model almost none of this, which is why manufacturers run direct sourcing on spreadsheets alongside an ERP that handles the transactions.

## Current Tools & Gaps
ERPs handle bills of materials, purchase orders and material requirements planning; product lifecycle management systems hold the engineering data; specialist direct materials sourcing tools exist and are thinly adopted; quality management systems hold supplier qualification. The gaps are at the joins: the engineering change process and the sourcing process are separate, so change notification to sourcing is an email and the resulting obsolescence, requalification and cost impact are managed manually; should-cost modelling — computing what a part ought to cost from its material, process and volume — is a specialist capability available to large manufacturers and nobody else; supplier qualification records live in quality and are not visible during sourcing; and tooling ownership and amortisation, which materially affects the true cost of switching suppliers, is tracked in spreadsheets.
