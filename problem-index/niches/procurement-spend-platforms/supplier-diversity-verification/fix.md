# The Percentage That Says Nothing About Outcomes

**Niche:** [[niches/procurement-spend-platforms/supplier-diversity-verification/profile|Supplier Diversity Verification]]
**Industry:** [[industries/procurement-spend-platforms|Procurement & Spend Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Supplier diversity is reported as a share of spend, which can be satisfied by a few large contracts, and nobody measures what diverse suppliers actually experience — win rates, contract sizes, payment terms and whether they are retained.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #survival-analysis #hypothesis-testing #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #quick-win #compliance
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in supplier diversity software is fighting to make a diverse spend claim verifiable rather than self-reported — and whoever can evidence what actually reached diverse suppliers takes the programme.

## The Problem
A company reports a diverse spend percentage that has improved for three years. Within it, the number of distinct diverse suppliers has fallen, the increase comes from two large contracts with established firms, diverse suppliers win a smaller share of the events they bid in than others do, they are paid on longer effective terms because more of their invoices stall in approval, and their median relationship length is shorter. Every one of those is computable from the company's own data and none is reported, because the metric that exists is a spend share and it is going up.

## Why It's Still Broken
The percentage is what gets asked for — by customers, by reporting frameworks, by public commitments — so it is what gets measured and managed. Outcome measures would show that a programme can improve its headline number while the experience of diverse suppliers gets worse, which is a finding nobody commissions. And the data needed sits across sourcing events, contracts and accounts payable, which is the usual reason a computable thing stays uncomputed.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Report the outcomes alongside the percentage. Number of distinct diverse suppliers and the trend, which distinguishes a programme that is broadening participation from one that is concentrating it. Win rate in competitive events, compared to non-diverse bidders in the same events, which is the fairest available measure of whether the process is working and is a straightforward comparison. Median and distribution of contract value, since a programme whose diverse suppliers are confined to small contracts is doing something different from what it claims. Effective payment terms by supplier size and status, which connects directly to the small supplier payment problem elsewhere in this industry and is frequently the least flattering number. Retention and relationship length, because a supplier won and lost within a year is not participation. And bid invitation rates, since a supplier who is never invited cannot win — which is the earliest point in the funnel and the one most within the buyer's control. Publishing outcomes rather than only spend share is the change that would make these programmes evaluable.

## Who Feels the Pain
Diverse suppliers whose participation is counted and whose experience is not measured; programme leads managing to a metric that can improve while the underlying reality does not; and the customers and public bodies relying on the reported figure.

## Impact If Fixed
Every outcome measure here is computable from data the organisation already holds, and together they describe what the programme is actually doing rather than what it spends. The win rate and invitation rate comparisons in particular locate where in the process diverse suppliers are being lost, which is the information a programme needs to change anything.
