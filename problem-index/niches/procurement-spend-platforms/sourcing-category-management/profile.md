# Sourcing & Category Management

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/procurement-spend-platforms|Procurement & Spend Platforms]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in sourcing software is fighting to remove the assembly work from a sourcing event so the category manager spends the time on negotiation — and whoever cuts specification and bid normalisation effort most takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.8B US strategic sourcing, e-sourcing and category management software
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~20% of procurement software revenue
**Digital Adoption:** Medium-High — sourcing events run in software and are assembled in spreadsheets
**Target Buyer:** Category managers and strategic sourcing leaders
**Automation Potential:** Very High — specification assembly, bid normalisation and scenario analysis are all mechanical

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
A sourcing event is the procurement function's core professional activity: define what is being bought, identify who can supply it, gather comparable offers, analyse them, and negotiate. The value is concentrated almost entirely in the last step, and the time is concentrated almost entirely in the first four. A category manager preparing a competitive event for a services category assembles a specification from prior contracts and stakeholder interviews, builds a bid template, chases responses, and then normalises the responses — because suppliers answer in their own structures, price different scope bundles, include different assumptions and quote in different units — into a spreadsheet that permits comparison. That normalisation alone routinely consumes days, and the analysis it enables is usually a simple total-cost comparison with a handful of scenarios that nobody has time to explore properly. The event is bounded by assembly, not by judgement.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Coupa, Ariba, Jaggaer and the e-sourcing specialists provide event management, supplier invitation, structured bid collection and basic scenario analysis; optimisation-based award tools exist at the enterprise end and are used rarely. The gaps: specifications are built from scratch each time rather than assembled from the organisation's own prior events and contracts; bid normalisation is manual whenever a supplier deviates from the template, which is always; should-cost and price benchmarking is unavailable to most category managers, so the negotiation reference is the incumbent's current price; scenario and award optimisation — which is where genuine value sits in multi-lot, multi-supplier events — is either absent or too complex to use; and nothing captures what was learned, so the next event in the same category starts from the same blank page.
