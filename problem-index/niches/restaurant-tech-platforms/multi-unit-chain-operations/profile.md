# Multi-Unit Chains — Making Units Comparable

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/restaurant-tech-platforms|Restaurant Tech Platforms]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor selling to multi-unit restaurant operators is fighting to make forty locations' numbers mean the same thing, so that an underperforming unit can be identified rather than argued about — and whoever makes units genuinely comparable takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.6B US software spend across multi-unit restaurant groups and franchise systems
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~13% of restaurant technology revenue, with the highest contract values in the category
**Digital Adoption:** High — multi-unit operators run above-store reporting, inventory and labour systems
**Target Buyer:** VPs of Operations, directors of above-store reporting, franchise operations teams
**Automation Potential:** High — the work is reconciliation, standardisation and variance investigation, all of it currently performed by analysts

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
A forty-unit operator has abundant data and cannot use it, because the units are not measured the same way. One general manager records waste diligently and another does not, so the unit that looks worse on food cost may simply be the one with honest reporting. Item names drift as local managers modify the POS. Labour categories are applied inconsistently. Comps and voids are used differently from store to store. Every above-store meeting therefore begins with an argument about whether a variance is real, and the operations director's actual job is adjudicating data quality rather than managing performance. This is a completely different contest from the independent's forecasting problem: the data volume is sufficient and the semantics are broken.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Crunchtime, Restaurant365, Qu, Delaget and the above-store modules of the major platforms serve this market with consolidated reporting, inventory and labour management, and franchise systems add compliance and royalty reporting. All of them consolidate. None of them establishes comparability first, which means they consolidate inconsistent inputs into an authoritative-looking number. There is no data quality layer that says this unit's waste reporting is incomplete, this unit's item mapping diverged in March, this unit's labour categories are applied differently from the rest. Variance investigation — the daily work of an above-store team — is manual, starts from a dashboard, and frequently ends with the discovery that the variance was a reporting artefact.
