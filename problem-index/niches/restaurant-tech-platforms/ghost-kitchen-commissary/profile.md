# Commissary & Ghost Kitchen Operations

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/restaurant-tech-platforms|Restaurant Tech Platforms]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in shared kitchen software is fighting to allocate capacity, labour and cost across brands sharing one kitchen in a way the tenants will accept as fair — and whoever makes the allocation defensible takes the facility.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$450M US software spend across commissary kitchens, shared-use food facilities, ghost kitchens and multi-brand virtual restaurant operations
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~4% of restaurant technology revenue, volatile with the segment's own fortunes
**Digital Adoption:** Low — the facilities run on spreadsheets, booking calendars and manual allocation
**Target Buyer:** Operators of shared kitchen facilities, multi-brand virtual restaurant companies, food business incubators
**Automation Potential:** High — capacity allocation and cost apportionment are optimisation and accounting problems being solved by argument

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
A shared kitchen has a problem no single-tenant restaurant has: multiple independent economic units consuming one physical resource with no natural unit of account. A commissary rents hours or stations to food businesses. A multi-brand ghost kitchen runs six virtual restaurants from one line with shared staff. In both cases the questions that decide the business — which brand or tenant is actually profitable, what a given hour of kitchen capacity is worth, whether a new brand should be added, whether a tenant is using more than they pay for — depend on allocating shared cost and capacity, and the allocation is currently a negotiation. Restaurant software has no concept of this because it assumes one kitchen serves one restaurant. Food manufacturing software has the concepts and assumes a scale and a production planning discipline these facilities do not have.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Shared kitchen management products exist and are thin — largely booking calendars with invoicing, which handles the rental transaction and nothing about production. Multi-brand ghost kitchen operators typically build internal tools or run several instances of a restaurant point of sale side by side, which keeps each brand's sales separate and leaves every shared cost to be allocated in a spreadsheet. Kitchen display systems do not model multiple brands sharing a line, so ticket sequencing across brands is manual and the resulting throughput loss is invisible. Nothing measures station or equipment utilisation, which is the facility's actual inventory. The gap is the absence of a unit of account: nobody has defined what an hour of a shared kitchen's capacity is, so nothing can be priced, allocated or optimised against it.
