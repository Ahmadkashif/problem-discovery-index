# Digital Ordering & Channel Middleware

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/restaurant-tech-platforms|Restaurant Tech Platforms]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in ordering middleware is fighting to keep one menu correct across five channels that each model a modifier differently — and whoever requires the fewest manual rebuilds takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.8B US digital ordering, menu management and delivery integration software
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~15% of restaurant technology revenue and the fastest-growing block
**Digital Adoption:** High — delivery marketplace participation is near-universal and middleware is broadly deployed
**Target Buyer:** Product leads at Olo, Otter, Deliverect, Chowly and the POS vendors' own integration teams; multi-unit digital directors
**Automation Potential:** Very High — menu translation between structured catalogues is a mapping problem being solved by data entry staff

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
A restaurant's menu now exists in five to eight places: the point of sale, its own website, a kiosk, three delivery marketplaces and possibly a catering channel. Each of those models the menu differently — modifier groups, required versus optional selections, nesting depth, price overrides, availability windows, image and description requirements — and no two agree. The consequence is that every menu change is performed multiple times by hand, every new restaurant's onboarding includes a person building the same menu five ways, and drift between channels produces the specific failures that cost a restaurant money: an item available on a marketplace that the kitchen removed, a modifier priced differently on one channel, an order arriving with a combination the kitchen cannot make. Middleware vendors compete on integration count, which is the wrong metric, in the same way construction platforms compete on interface count.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Olo, Otter, Deliverect, Chowly, ItsaCheckmate and the POS vendors' own integrations all move orders and menus between systems, and order injection is largely a solved problem. Menu synchronisation is the unsolved half. The gaps are specific and consistent: modifier structures do not map cleanly, so vendors either flatten them — losing the structure the kitchen depends on — or require manual mapping per channel; availability and eighty-sixing propagate slowly or one way only; and nobody measures or publishes menu fidelity, so a restaurant cannot tell which of its channels currently disagrees with the point of sale. Onboarding a new restaurant onto a channel remains a human task measured in hours per menu.
