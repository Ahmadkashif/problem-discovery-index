# The Pour Size Nobody Has Ever Measured

**Niche:** [[niches/restaurant-tech-platforms/bar-nightlife-operations/profile|Bar & Nightlife Operations]]
**Industry:** [[industries/restaurant-tech-platforms|Restaurant Tech Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Every drink is costed on an assumed pour size, every bar's actual pour size differs from the assumption, and the difference between a 1.5 ounce recipe and a 1.8 ounce reality is a fifth of the beverage cost of goods.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #hypothesis-testing #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #worker-facing #quick-win #compliance
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in beverage operations software is fighting to reconcile what was poured against what was sold, at a granularity and a cost that a bar will actually sustain — and whoever closes that variance takes the account.

## The Problem
Every cocktail recipe in every bar's system specifies a pour in ounces, every cost calculation and every menu price derives from it, and almost no bar has ever measured what its bartenders actually pour. Free-pouring, which is standard in most American bars, produces a distribution rather than a number, and that distribution is wider and higher than the recipe. A consistent third of an ounce over on a well spirit, across a busy bar, is a material cost that shows up as unexplained variance and is treated as shrinkage rather than as what it is — a costing assumption that was never validated.

## Why It's Still Broken
Measuring pour size requires either jiggers, which most bars reject for speed and style reasons, or instrumentation, which costs money. But the deeper reason is framing: the industry treats variance as a loss-prevention question, which makes it about honesty and therefore adversarial, when a large part of it is a measurement error in the recipe. Nobody has separated the two, so bars run loss-prevention responses to what is partly a costing problem, and the staff resentment that follows makes the whole subject difficult to approach.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Measure the distribution once, deliberately, as a costing exercise rather than an investigation. A short structured measurement — pours captured by scale or flow meter over a representative period, across bartenders and service conditions — yields the actual distribution per product per station. Recost every recipe against the measured pour rather than the assumed one, which immediately corrects the menu pricing and the theoretical cost that every subsequent variance is measured against. Then, and only then, the residual variance means something, because the costing error has been removed from it. Repeat the measurement periodically as staff change. Frame it to the team as what it is — a correction to the recipe, not an accusation — which is both true and the only way the measurement will be representative rather than performed carefully while being watched.

## Who Feels the Pain
Owners chasing shrinkage that is partly a spreadsheet assumption; bartenders suspected of theft when the recipe was wrong; and beverage directors pricing a menu off a number nobody has checked.

## Impact If Fixed
Recosting against measured pours typically moves theoretical beverage cost by several points and reprices a menu accordingly, which is a direct margin correction requiring no behaviour change at all. Separating the costing error from the loss-prevention question is also what makes the remaining variance investigable without poisoning the staff relationship.
