# Kitchen Staff & Back-of-House Tools

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/restaurant-tech-platforms|Restaurant Tech Platforms]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor building for the back of house is fighting to get the prep list, the par levels and the station setup to the line in a form usable with wet hands during service — and whoever the cooks actually use takes the kitchen.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$600M US software spend attributable to back-of-house tools, prep management and kitchen operations
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~5% of restaurant technology revenue, against a workforce that is roughly half of restaurant labour
**Digital Adoption:** Low — the kitchen's software is a printed ticket and a laminated sheet
**Target Buyer:** Chefs and kitchen managers; the actual users are line and prep cooks
**Automation Potential:** High — prep quantities, par levels and station assignments are computable and are currently written on a clipboard

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Restaurant technology has been built almost entirely for the front of house and the office. The point of sale is a cashier's tool. Scheduling is a manager's tool. Reporting is an owner's tool. The kitchen — where roughly half the labour and most of the food cost lives — receives a printed ticket. Everything else in a kitchen's information life is manual: prep lists written each morning by a sous chef from memory and a walk-in inventory, par levels held in someone's head, station setups and mise en place decided by convention, recipes on laminated cards, and the transfer of all of that to a new cook accomplished by being told. This is the largest underserved population in hospitality technology and the one with the most physically constrained interface requirements, which is why the products that have been tried — tablets with forms — have not survived contact with a line.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Kitchen display systems replaced the printed ticket and are broadly deployed and surprisingly primitive: they display tickets and do little else. Recipe and prep management modules exist inside back-office products and are used by chefs at a desk rather than by cooks on a line. Inventory products focus on cost rather than on production. The gaps are the whole job: nothing computes tomorrow's prep from a demand forecast and current inventory, nothing maintains par levels adaptively, nothing captures what was actually produced or wasted at the station where it happens, and nothing addresses the training and knowledge transfer problem in a workforce with high turnover and, frequently, a primary language other than English. Every one of those is solvable and none is sold.
