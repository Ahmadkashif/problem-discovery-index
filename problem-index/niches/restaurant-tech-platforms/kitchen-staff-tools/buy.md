# Multilingual Instruction Delivery for a Multilingual Line

**One-liner:** Translation, speech and short-form video are commodity technologies, and American commercial kitchens — among the most linguistically diverse workplaces in the country — run on laminated English recipe cards and being shown once.

## The Problem
A kitchen's staff may speak Spanish, Mandarin, Bengali and English across one line. Recipes, specs, allergen procedures and food safety instructions are written in English. Training happens by demonstration, which works and does not scale, and produces variation between cooks that shows up as inconsistent plates. When a new dish is added, the transfer of how to make it is a chef showing each cook individually across several shifts. The most consequential instructions — allergen handling, temperature and holding rules — are the ones where a language gap has the worst consequences and are delivered in the least accessible form.

## What Already Exists
Machine translation across all the relevant languages is cheap and good. Text-to-speech and speech recognition are commodity. Short video hosting and playback is free. Multilingual signage and instruction delivery is a solved problem in manufacturing and warehousing, where the same workforce dynamics exist and have been addressed for years. Every component is available; hospitality has adopted almost none of it.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to a kitchen's physical reality and its vocabulary. It requires: (1) culinary terminology handled properly, since generic translation mangles technique words and ingredient names in ways that produce genuinely wrong instructions, and a reviewed terminology base per kitchen is the difference; (2) video as the primary medium rather than text, because technique is shown rather than described and a fifteen-second clip crosses language barriers better than any translation; (3) delivery at the station on a wipeable or voice-driven surface, since a cook cannot hold a phone, which is the constraint that has defeated every previous attempt; (4) safety-critical content — allergens, temperatures, holding times — reviewed by a human translator rather than machine-generated, with the product enforcing that distinction rather than leaving it to whoever uploads; and (5) making the chef's own demonstration the authoring act, so recording how a dish is made once produces the instruction rather than requiring someone to write it.

## Target Customer
Restaurant groups standardising execution across kitchens, high-turnover high-volume operations, and back-of-house vendors whose recipe modules currently render English text.

## Impact If Solved
Consistency of execution is the thing multi-unit operators care most about and cannot achieve through demonstration alone, and instruction in a cook's own language is the most direct available lever. For the cooks it is the difference between being shown once and being able to check — which in a workforce where asking can feel costly is a meaningful improvement in working life as well as output.
