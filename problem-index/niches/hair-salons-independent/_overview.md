## Why These Niches

Color services drive the majority of salon revenue and require the most specialized product inventory, formula management, and continuing education. Booth-rental operations represent the dominant independent salon business model but create unique management challenges around occupancy, common-area maintenance, and hybrid contractor/employee relationships. Textured hair salons and mobile stylists are the most digitally neglected segments — textured hair requires entirely different product lines, techniques, and service timing, while mobile stylists lack any purpose-built practice management tools. Aging clientele and male grooming represent underserved demographic segments with distinct service requirements. The two automation niches target the most error-prone manual workflows: retail product management and the complex payroll calculations inherent in commission/booth-rent hybrid salon models.

## Why These Pockets

One pocket qualified, and it qualified on a change that is three years old. The Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act imposed the first substantive federal requirements on cosmetics since 1938 — facility registration, product listing, adverse event reporting, and mandatory safety substantiation — and every professional colour and care brand selling into salons is now inside it. That created a regulatory consulting demand curve with statutory deadlines attached, served by a profession whose method is a toxicologist assembling a document.

The specific opportunity is structural. A colour line is two hundred shades of one chemistry, and each shade's safety assessment is produced as though the firm had never seen the ingredients before, because no system in the practice represents a portfolio as a set of related formulas. The available regulatory information management platforms are all built for pharma — a few products with enormous dossiers — and cosmetics is the exact inverse. And the firm's own accumulated knowledge of what regulators accept sits in a folder tree organized by client, which is the one axis along which nobody ever needs to search it.

Everything else in the industry has the same problem in different clothes. Brand education organizations are the largest technical workforce in beauty, given away to sell colour. Distributors hold salon-level purchase histories that manufacturers cannot see. Booking marketplaces can observe directly whether a client follows a stylist to a new salon — the industry's central retention question, per Pass 1's 30-40% annual client loss — and sell appointment software. The salon coaching organizations are the near miss: they hold the only real P&L benchmark corpus for a segment that publishes nothing, and they deliver it as coaching conversations rather than as a product.

Retail measurement is the same pocket already indexed under Food Distributors at 54, logged here for completeness and deliberately not double-entered. It is also worth noting what it does not cover: the professional salon channel sits outside retail measurement entirely, which is why the small specialist research firms exist at all.
