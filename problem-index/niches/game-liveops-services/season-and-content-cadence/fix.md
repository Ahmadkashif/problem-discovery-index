# Season Fourteen Has to Beat Season Thirteen

**Niche:** [[niches/game-liveops-services/season-and-content-cadence/profile|Season & Content Cadence]]
**Industry:** [[industries/game-liveops-services|Game LiveOps Services]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The only stated requirement for the next season is that it outperforms the last one, and that requirement has been applied thirteen times.
**Tags:** #quick-win #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #time-series-forecasting #confidence-intervals #worker-facing #revenue-impact #survival-analysis
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to set a season and content cadence that both the players and the team can sustain for years, when the only planning input is that each season must beat the last — and whoever models the fatigue takes the account.

## The Problem
Season planning in most live games begins with a single input: the previous season's numbers, plus growth. Content volume, reward value and event density all move up because nothing in the process can produce a smaller plan. Thirteen iterations later the team is compressed, the players are saturated, and the fourteenth plan is built on the same rule. Nobody has ever put the escalation on a chart.

## Why It's Still Broken
The ratchet is in the planning process itself — a planning rule that can only produce a larger number will produce larger numbers indefinitely, regardless of what the system can bear, and no one step in the sequence is unreasonable. Nobody charts the trend across seasons. Proposing less looks like proposing failure. And the cost lands on people rather than on a line item.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Put the escalation on one chart and let it argue for itself. Plot content volume, reward value, event count and required player hours across all previous seasons, which is the fix and usually ends the discussion on sight. Chart the team's delivery effort and overtime alongside it, since the two curves together are the whole case. Show seasonal completion rates falling as demand rises, which is the player-side evidence and is already in the data. Track the recovery gap between seasons, as it has usually disappeared without anyone deciding to remove it. Model what the next three seasons require if the rule continues, because the extrapolation is more persuasive than the history. Name the planning rule explicitly so it can be changed, which is the actual intervention. Set an explicit ceiling on required player hours per season, which gives the team something to design against. Compare against other operated titles where data exists. Bring the chart to the planning meeting rather than to a retrospective, since that is the only moment it can change anything. And propose one deliberately lighter season as an experiment with a measured outcome.

## Who Feels the Pain
Live teams delivering a schedule that compresses each quarter; players who cannot keep up and quietly stop; product leads with no way to propose less; and games that end because they cannot be staffed.

## Impact If Fixed
A planning rule that can only produce a larger number will produce larger numbers indefinitely, regardless of what the system can bear. Charting thirteen seasons of escalation alongside falling completion rates is the argument nobody has assembled.
