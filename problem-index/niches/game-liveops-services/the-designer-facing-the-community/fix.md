# The Fortnight After the Patch Note

**Niche:** [[niches/game-liveops-services/the-designer-facing-the-community/profile|The Designer Facing the Community]]
**Industry:** [[industries/game-liveops-services|Game LiveOps Services]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The change was right, the reaction was personal, and the designer read all of it because nobody told them not to.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #quick-win #workflow-orchestration #automation #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #compliance
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to let a designer make a change the data clearly supports without spending the following fortnight being told publicly that they have ruined the game — and whoever solves that takes the account.

## The Problem
After an unpopular change, the designer responsible reads the response. Some of it is substantive. A great deal of it is personal, sustained and abusive, and it continues for a fortnight. Nobody instructed them to read it, nobody instructed them not to, and there is no filter, no rota and no support. The predictable outcome is that experienced designers become unwilling to propose changes that are correct but unpopular.

## Why It's Still Broken
Nobody owns the designer's exposure — an unassigned harm is nobody's problem to fix, and reading the feed is treated as diligence rather than as a hazard. Filtering feels like ignoring feedback. The abuse is normalised in the sector. And the person absorbing it is usually the one least able to say so.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Put a process between the person and the feed. Assign community monitoring to a rota rather than to the designer, which is the fix and costs nothing but a decision. Filter abusive content out of what reaches the design team, since the substantive feedback survives filtering and the abuse does not need to be read to be handled. Summarise the substantive response so designers get the signal without the volume. Attribute changes to the team in all public materials, which removes the individual target. Prepare a response plan before shipping a change expected to be unpopular. Set an explicit expectation that designers are not required to read the feed, because the absence of that instruction is what makes them. Track abuse directed at named staff and act on it, which most teams do not do at all. Check in with the designer after a difficult change rather than assuming they are fine. Record which changes drew the worst response and whether the explanation helped, so the practice improves. And separate the substantive disagreement, which is worth engaging, from the abuse, which is not.

## Who Feels the Pain
Designers absorbing a fortnight of personal abuse for correct decisions; teams that stop proposing necessary changes; community managers caught between; and games that drift because nobody will fix them.

## Impact If Fixed
An unassigned harm is nobody's problem to fix, and reading the feed is treated as diligence rather than as a hazard. A monitoring rota and a filter keep the substantive feedback and remove the rest.
