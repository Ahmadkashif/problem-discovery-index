# The Rarity Change That Wiped a Market

**Niche:** [[niches/virtual-economy-operators/monetary-policy/profile|Monetary Policy & Supply Design]]
**Industry:** [[industries/virtual-economy-operators|Virtual Economy Operators]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A patch changed how an item drops, prices fell by most of their value overnight, and holders lost real money the operator had never modelled.
**Tags:** #quick-win #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #causal-inference #time-series-forecasting #compliance #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to set drop rates, sinks and limited releases without a forecast of what they do to prices people pay real money at — and whoever supplies that forecast takes the account.

## The Problem
A routine balance patch alters how an item is obtained. Overnight, the secondary price collapses, and people who paid real money for that item lose most of it. The designer who made the change was optimising the game and had no view of the market. There was no forecast, no warning, and no consideration of the holders — who frequently learn what happened from the market rather than from the patch notes.

## Why It's Still Broken
The design tool has no market in it — a decision made in an interface that cannot show the price consequence will be made without regard to it, every time, by people acting entirely reasonably. Nobody reviews changes for monetary effect. The loss falls on players. And acknowledging the harm implies responsibility for the economy.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Put a market check in the change process. Add a monetary review step to any change affecting item supply or acquisition, which is the fix and is a checklist rather than a model. Show the designer the affected items' current market prices and holdings at the point of the change. Estimate the value held by players in the affected items, since the number is usually far larger than anyone in the room expects. Compare against past similar changes and what they did to prices, as the operator's own history is the best available guide. Announce supply-affecting changes in advance where possible, which lets holders act and is what a market expects. Phase large changes rather than applying them at once, which spreads the adjustment. Exclude or compensate in the most extreme cases, which some operators already do informally and none do by policy. Report the realised price effect afterwards, so the review process learns. Keep a register of which items have real secondary value, because not all do and the review should be proportionate. And say plainly in patch notes when a change is expected to affect item values, which costs nothing and changes the relationship.

## Who Feels the Pain
Players who lost real money overnight; designers blamed for a consequence they could not see; support handling the complaints; and the operator's standing with a market it says it does not run.

## Impact If Fixed
A decision made in an interface that cannot show the price consequence will be made without regard to it, every time, by people acting entirely reasonably. A monetary review step showing prices and holdings is a checklist against a real transfer of value.
