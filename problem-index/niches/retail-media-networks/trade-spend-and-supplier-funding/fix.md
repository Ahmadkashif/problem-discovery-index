# The Media Budget That Was Really a Listing Fee

**Niche:** [[niches/retail-media-networks/trade-spend-and-supplier-funding/profile|Trade Spend & Supplier Funding]]
**Industry:** [[industries/retail-media-networks|Retail Media Networks]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The supplier's media commitment was agreed in a supply negotiation, is treated as advertising by the marketing team, and is measured as if it were a choice.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #compliance #revenue-impact #hypothesis-testing #quick-win #workflow-orchestration #confidence-intervals
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to show a supplier and a retailer what the total money between them actually bought, across trade funds and media spend together — and whoever does that settles the argument both sides are currently having blind.

## The Problem
A commitment to spend a certain amount on the retailer's media network is agreed during a supply negotiation, alongside terms on price, listings and shelf space. It arrives at the supplier's marketing team as a budget to deploy. That team then optimises it, reports on its return, and includes it in their effectiveness analysis alongside genuinely discretionary media. The number they report is a return on spend that was never optional, and it is used to justify further investment in a channel whose performance has never actually been tested against the alternative of not spending it. Both organisations are analysing a mandated payment as though it were a decision.

## Why It's Still Broken
The commitment is agreed by sales and executed by marketing, and no one records which budgets were negotiated and which were chosen — that classification simply does not exist as a field anywhere. Treating it as media is convenient for both parties' accounting. Questioning it disturbs a supply relationship. And the resulting return figure flatters the channel, which suits everyone reporting on it.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Classify the money by how it was decided. Tag every budget as negotiated or discretionary at the point it is agreed, which is the fix, is one field, and immediately separates a measurement from an obligation. Report discretionary performance separately, since that is the only spend whose return is a decision-relevant number and mixing the two makes both meaningless. Account for negotiated commitments as a cost of doing business in the supplier's own reporting, which is what they are, while still deploying them as well as possible. Evaluate whether a negotiated commitment could be better spent on another instrument, because the amount may be fixed while its form is often not — this is the practical value and is available immediately. Make the retailer's side explicit too, since a network whose growth is substantially negotiated has a different business from one whose growth is earned, and that distinction matters to anyone valuing it. Track the share of network revenue that is negotiated over time, which is the category's most important unreported statistic. Give the supplier's marketing team the context so they stop reporting an obligation as an achievement. Feed genuine effectiveness back into the negotiation, so the next agreement is informed by evidence rather than by leverage alone. Separate the measurement function from the negotiation entirely, which is the structural safeguard. And publish the distinction as a reporting standard, because until negotiated and discretionary spend are separated everywhere, no comparison across networks means anything.

## Who Feels the Pain
Supplier marketing teams reporting on money they never chose; retailers whose network growth looks like demand and is partly leverage; and anyone valuing a retail media business on revenue whose nature is undisclosed.

## Impact If Fixed
Nobody records which budgets were negotiated and which were chosen, so obligations are reported as achievements. One classification field separates decision-relevant performance from a cost of doing business, and the negotiated share of network revenue is the category's most important unreported number.
