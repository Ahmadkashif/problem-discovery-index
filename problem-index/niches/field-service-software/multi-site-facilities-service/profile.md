# Multi-Site Facilities Service

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/field-service-software|Field Service Software]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in multi-site facilities software is fighting to prove that service actually happened, to the standard promised, at a site nobody supervises — and whoever makes verification credible to the client takes the contract.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$520M US software spend across multi-site janitorial, facilities maintenance and grounds contractors
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~10% of field service software revenue
**Digital Adoption:** Low-Medium — time and attendance is digitised, quality and verification are not
**Target Buyer:** Operations directors at multi-site service contractors; facilities managers on the client side
**Automation Potential:** High — verification, inspection scoring and issue routing are all structurable and are currently a clipboard

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Facilities service is performed at sites where nobody from the contractor's management is present and frequently nobody from the client is either — a cleaning crew works an office building at eleven at night, a grounds crew visits forty retail sites a month, a maintenance technician covers a region of unstaffed properties. The service is judged after the fact by whether anyone complains, which means the contractor's quality is measured by client irritation and its own supervisors spend their time driving between sites to inspect. Contracts are frequently won on price and lost on a perception of quality that neither party can evidence. This makes the contested capability unusual among service businesses: not diagnosis or dispatch, but proof — establishing that the work was done, to the standard specified, in a way the client accepts without a supervisor standing there.

## Current Tools & Gaps
The market is served by a mix of general field service platforms, janitorial-specific products and workforce management tools focused on time and attendance, which is the part that is digitised because payroll requires it. Inspection apps exist and are used by supervisors doing site visits. Client-facing reporting is typically a monthly summary. The gaps are the ones that matter: task-level completion is self-reported by the crew with no verification, quality inspection is a supervisor's periodic sample rather than a continuous signal, client-reported issues arrive by email to an account manager and are resolved without ever becoming data, and nothing joins the three. A contractor therefore cannot say which sites are actually well-served, which crews perform consistently, or what its own quality is — it can only say who has complained.
