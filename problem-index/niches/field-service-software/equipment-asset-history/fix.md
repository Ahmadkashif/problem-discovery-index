# The Maintenance Agreement on Equipment Nobody Can Identify

**Niche:** [[niches/field-service-software/equipment-asset-history/profile|Equipment Asset History]]
**Industry:** [[industries/field-service-software|Field Service Software]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Maintenance agreements are the most valuable recurring revenue in the trades and are sold against equipment the contractor cannot identify, at a price set without knowing what it costs to maintain.
**Tags:** #survival-analysis #gradient-boosting #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #automation #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in field service is fighting to populate an equipment record completely without costing the technician a minute — and whoever gets record completeness per visit highest takes the capability everything else in the category depends on.

## The Problem
A contractor sells twelve hundred maintenance agreements at a flat annual price. The agreements cover equipment whose age, model and condition are unknown for most of the base, because the equipment records are empty. Some of those units are four years old and will consume one short visit a year; some are eighteen years old and will consume two visits plus emergency calls plus goodwill repairs. They are priced identically. The programme's overall margin is known and its composition is not, so the contractor cannot tell whether the programme is profitable because most units are young or unprofitable in a growing tail — and cannot price, target or manage it.

## Why It's Still Broken
Agreements were sold before anyone thought to record what they covered, and the base accumulated. Retroactively identifying twelve hundred units means visiting them, which happens anyway during the agreement's own maintenance visits — so the fix is available and has never been organised. And the programme is measured in aggregate, at which level it usually looks fine, so there is no visible problem to investigate until the tail is large.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Use the visits the agreements already generate. Every maintenance visit under an agreement is an opportunity to identify the unit at near-zero cost given the capture described in this niche, so within one annual cycle the base is documented. Then compute what has never been computed: cost to serve per agreement by equipment age and model, against the price paid. Rank the base by expected cost so the contractor can see the tail. From there the options are ordinary and unavailable today — price by equipment age at renewal, target replacement conversations at the units that will consume the most, and decline to renew on equipment past a point where an agreement is an insurance policy against certain failure. The replacement conversation in particular is the contractor's most valuable sales opportunity and is currently triggered by a breakdown rather than by an assessment.

## Who Feels the Pain
Contractors whose agreement programme margin is eroding invisibly as their base ages; technicians sent repeatedly to equipment that should have been replaced years ago; and customers paying a flat price for a plan that is either a bargain or a poor deal, with no way to know which.

## Impact If Fixed
Documenting the agreement base through visits already scheduled costs nothing beyond the capture and turns the trades' most valuable recurring revenue programme into something that can be priced and managed. Age-ranked replacement targeting converts a reactive breakdown conversation into a planned one, which is better for the margin and substantially better for the customer.
