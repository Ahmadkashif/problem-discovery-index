## Why These Niches

The category's two large blocks split by who owns the equipment being serviced. In residential trades the servicer knows nothing about the unit until the technician is standing in front of it, so the contested capability is diagnosis before arrival — predicting what the job actually is from a customer's vague description plus whatever history exists, and matching a technician and a part to it. That single number, first-time fix rate, determines whether a residential service business makes money.

Commercial and enterprise field service **failed the filter as one niche**: an OEM servicing its own installed base and a third-party maintainer servicing equipment it did not build face opposite problems. The OEM has telemetry and design knowledge and is fighting to act on it before the customer calls; the third-party maintainer has neither and is fighting to source and certify parts for machines whose manufacturer would prefer it did not. Decomposed below.

Multi-site facilities work and equipment dealer service are the two underdigitised operating models, each with a specific structural cause: facilities service happens at sites nobody supervises, and dealer service departments run on a manufacturer's warranty system plus a whiteboard. Technicians and low-density territories are the two underserved constituencies — the technician is the industry's primary worker and the last to receive a tool built for them, and rural service economics are different enough that the routing and pricing assumptions of every product in the category do not hold.

The two automation niches are the vendors' permanent content burdens: price books that must be right per trade, per region and per equipment type, and equipment records that exist in every platform and are populated in almost none.

## Filter Notes

**Niche 2 failed the filter as stated and was decomposed.** "Commercial and enterprise field service" groups two businesses whose contested capabilities are opposites. A manufacturer servicing its own installed base holds telemetry, engineering data and warranty history, and every competitor in that space is fighting to convert those into a dispatched action before the customer notices a fault — a prediction problem with abundant proprietary data. A third-party maintainer holds none of that, services equipment across manufacturers who actively restrict parts and documentation, and is fighting to source and certify a part for a machine whose maker would rather it did not — a supply and provenance problem. Different data, different adversaries, different products. Both sub-niches are terminal.

**Niches 1, 3–8 are terminal as stated.** Each names a measurable capability: first-time fix rate, verified service delivery at unsupervised sites, machine downtime inside a seasonal window, time from last task to truck moving, revenue per drive hour in a low-density territory, price book accuracy against local won-and-lost jobs, and equipment record completeness per visit.
