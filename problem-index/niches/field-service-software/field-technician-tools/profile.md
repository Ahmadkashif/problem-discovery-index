# Field Technician Tools

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/field-service-software|Field Service Software]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor building for technicians is fighting to have the visit record complete before the truck pulls out of the driveway, without the technician typing — and whoever gets time-from-last-task-to-moving lowest takes the technician's loyalty and the owner's account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$450M US software spend attributable to technician-facing field applications
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~9% of field service software revenue, against the workforce that performs all of the work
**Digital Adoption:** Low in substance — technicians carry a device and use it as a form
**Target Buyer:** Bought by owners and operations managers; used by technicians, whose adoption determines everything
**Automation Potential:** Very High — the visit record can be assembled from photos, parts scans and speech, all of which are commodity

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
The technician is the industry's primary worker and the last constituency to be designed for. Field service software has been built for the dispatcher, the owner and the accountant, and the technician receives the data entry burden that those three parties' reports require. An hour a day of unpaid or grudgingly paid administration is the commonly cited figure and it matches what technicians describe: typing up what they just did, photographing and uploading, filling in equipment fields, completing forms for compliance, chasing a signature, and processing payment. Every one of those is a legitimate business requirement and every one of them is implemented as a form. The consequence is not only lost time but lost data: technicians skip the optional fields, which are precisely the ones — equipment details, diagnosis specifics, condition notes — that the rest of the industry's unrealised capability depends on.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Every platform ships a technician mobile app with work orders, forms, photo capture, parts usage, invoicing and payment. They are competently built and they are forms. Voice input appears in some. Offline support is variable and matters enormously in crawlspaces, basements and rural territories. The gaps are consistent across vendors: nothing assembles a visit record from the artefacts the visit already produced; parts consumption is typed rather than scanned; equipment identification is manual despite nameplates being photographable; and the technician's own view of their work — what they earned, how they are performing, what is coming — is either absent or designed as a management dashboard pointed at them.
