# POS Payments & Merchant Services

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/retail-pos-platforms|Retail POS Platforms]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** *Not terminal as stated.* The honest partial sentence is that every competitor is fighting to make payments the profitable part of a POS business — but approving a merchant safely and winning a chargeback are separate contests with separate adversaries. Decomposed into contested sub-niches below.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$3.4B US attributable to payments, risk and merchant services within retail POS platforms
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~34% of retail POS revenue and a far higher share of its profit
**Digital Adoption:** High — payment acceptance is the most systematised part of the category
**Target Buyer:** Payments, risk and underwriting leaders at POS platforms, ISOs and acquirers
**Automation Potential:** Very High — underwriting and dispute handling are both decision problems on rich transactional data

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
The software in this category is substantially a wedge and the payments are the business. A POS platform earns on processing volume, which means its economics are an acquirer's economics: approve merchants quickly to grow volume, and absorb the losses when an approved merchant turns out to be fraudulent or fails leaving unfulfilled obligations. That tension — speed of onboarding against loss rate — is the defining commercial question of the block, and it is not the same question as handling disputes after transactions have occurred. Both are large, both are contested, and grouping them produces a niche whose contested sentence has to be written so broadly that it says nothing. Hence the decomposition.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Square, Stripe, Shopify, Clover and the acquirer-backed platforms all operate underwriting and risk functions with a mixture of automated scoring and manual review, and all of them offer dispute handling as a merchant-facing feature. Third-party chargeback representment services exist as a category. The gaps that span both halves: the platform's own merchant lifecycle data — how merchants who later failed behaved in their first weeks — is rarely used systematically for either underwriting or for early intervention; loss attribution is aggregated rather than decomposed, so the platform cannot say which approval decisions produced which losses; and merchants receive almost no help understanding either why they were declined or why a dispute was lost, which is the same opacity that recurs everywhere in this vault.
