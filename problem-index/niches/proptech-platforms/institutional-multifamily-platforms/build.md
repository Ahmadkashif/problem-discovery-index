# Unit Pricing From First-Party Demand Alone

**Niche:** [[niches/proptech-platforms/institutional-multifamily-platforms/profile|Institutional Multifamily Platforms]]
**Industry:** [[industries/proptech-platforms|Proptech Platforms]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** The industry priced units from pooled competitor data because it was easy, and now needs to price them from a single operator's own demand signal — which nobody has built, and which the data comfortably supports.
**Tags:** #bayesian-inference #gradient-boosting #time-series-forecasting #logistic-regression #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #causal-inference #compliance
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in multifamily revenue management is now fighting to price a unit well using only what one operator legitimately knows about its own demand — and whoever prices accurately without pooled competitor data takes the market.

## The Problem
A two-bedroom unit comes available in April. What should it be listed at? The operator knows its own enquiry volume by price point across hundreds of similar units, how many enquiries became tours, how many tours became applications, how long each unit sat at each price, what concessions were offered and whether they converted, and what every renewing resident accepted. That is a complete demand experiment run continuously over its own portfolio. The pricing product it uses instead asked what competitors were charging, and that mechanism is now contested in court.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
The pooled approach worked well enough and was commercially simpler — a competitor price is an observation, whereas estimating demand from one's own funnel requires modelling a curve from data generated by the operator's own past pricing decisions, which is a causal inference problem rather than a regression. Handling it properly means confronting the fact that price was not set randomly, so observed conversion at each price is confounded by whatever made the operator choose that price. There is a clean answer — deliberate, bounded price variation across comparable units, which a large portfolio can run continuously at trivial cost — and nobody ran it because the shortcut existed.

## What to Build
A demand model per unit type per submarket per season, estimated from the operator's own funnel with the pricing endogeneity addressed rather than ignored. Enquiry, tour and application conversion at each offered price give the response curve; vacancy duration gives the cost of being wrong upward; concession usage and conversion give the effective price, which is frequently not the listed one. Bounded price experimentation is built into the product as a first-class feature, with the variation small enough to be commercially unremarkable and structured enough to identify the curve. The output is a recommended price with an expected revenue distribution that explicitly trades rent against days vacant, which is the actual decision and is currently made by a rule. Nothing about the model requires another landlord's data, and the product should be able to demonstrate that — which is a compliance feature as much as a technical one.

## Target Customer
Institutional multifamily owners and managers, the platform vendors whose revenue management modules now carry legal risk, and new entrants for whom the legal cloud over incumbents is the opening.

## Impact If Built
A pricing capability built on first-party data alone removes a live legal exposure from a category that has depended on the disputed mechanism, which is worth more to a large operator than any incremental accuracy. And it is likely to price better in several respects — renewal, concessions and vacancy cost are all first-party questions that the competitor-anchored approach handled poorly precisely because it was anchored somewhere else.
