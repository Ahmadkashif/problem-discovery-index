# Resident-Facing Tools

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/proptech-platforms|Proptech Platforms]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor building resident-facing software is fighting to let a renter answer a question about their own tenancy — what they owe, what was fixed, what their lease says, where their deposit is — without calling the office, and whoever makes the record genuinely legible to the resident takes the resident-experience market.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$750M US spend on resident portals, payments, insurance and resident experience products
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~7% of proptech revenue, monetised largely through fees charged to residents
**Digital Adoption:** Low in substance — portals are widely deployed and used mainly to pay rent
**Target Buyer:** The operator buys; the resident uses and frequently pays the fees
**Automation Potential:** High — the resident's questions are answerable from data the operator already holds

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
The resident is the only party to a tenancy who cannot see their own record. The operator knows the payment ledger, the work order history, the lease terms and amendments, the deposit and its status, the renewal offer history and the notices served. The resident gets a portal that accepts payments, submits maintenance requests, and shows a balance whose composition is frequently unexplained. When they want to know why the balance is higher than the rent, what happened to the work order from three weeks ago, what their lease says about a fee, or when their deposit will be returned, they call the office — which is also the site manager's day. The asymmetry is not incidental to the product design; resident-facing tools are bought by the operator and are optimised for rent collection and for ancillary revenue, several streams of which are fees charged to the resident for the privilege of using the portal.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Every major platform ships a resident portal, and Entrata in particular competes on resident experience. Payments, renters insurance, rent reporting to credit bureaus and resident rewards have become substantial ancillary revenue lines. The functional gaps are consistent: ledger entries appear as codes with no explanation, so a resident cannot reconstruct their own balance; maintenance status is a status field rather than an account of what happened; lease documents are stored as PDFs rather than as searchable terms; deposit status is absent entirely; and communication history is not visible to the resident even when it concerns them. Several of these are not oversights but consequences of who the buyer is, which is the honest thing to say about this niche.
