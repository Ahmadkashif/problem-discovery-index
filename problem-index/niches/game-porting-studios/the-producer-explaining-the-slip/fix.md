# Nobody Priced the Fortnightly Patch

**Niche:** [[niches/game-porting-studios/the-producer-explaining-the-slip/profile|The Producer Explaining the Slip]]
**Industry:** [[industries/game-porting-studios|Game Porting Studios]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The contract says fixed price and fixed date and says nothing about the client shipping a patch every two weeks.
**Tags:** #quick-win #compliance #workflow-orchestration #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #revenue-impact #data-integration #worker-facing
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to let the person between a fixed date and a moving codebase explain the slip with evidence rather than apology — and whoever gives them that takes the account.

## The Problem
Porting contracts are written as though the source codebase is static. In practice the client continues development throughout, shipping patches and content that must be merged and re-verified. The contract contains no provision for this — no assumed patch cadence, no mechanism when it is exceeded, no pricing for the rework. So the studio absorbs it, the producer manages the consequences, and the same omission recurs in the next contract.

## Why It's Still Broken
The assumption was never written down — a contract that is silent on the client's ongoing development implicitly assigns all of its cost to the studio, and nobody notices until the schedule is already gone. The cadence is not measured, so there is nothing to negotiate with. Raising it during the project looks adversarial. And the next contract is drafted from the last one.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Write the assumption into the contract and measure against it. State an assumed patch cadence and merge scope in the contract with a mechanism when it is exceeded, which is the fix and costs one clause. Measure the actual cadence and merge cost from the first week, since the evidence must exist before the conversation. Define integration points rather than accepting continuous merges, which is often welcome to the client too and is simply never proposed. Report merge and re-verification cost separately in every status update, so it is visible from the start rather than raised at the end. Agree a code freeze period before milestones, which is standard and frequently omitted. Show the client the cost of their cadence in their own terms, as most do not know and many will adjust. Keep a variation register from day one rather than assembling one in a dispute. Price the moving-target risk explicitly in the bid, which is the durable fix and belongs in estimation. Review the actual cadence against the assumption at each milestone. And carry the measured figures into the next contract, which is how the omission stops recurring.

## Who Feels the Pain
Producers managing an unpriced cost; studios absorbing rework out of margin; engineers redoing finished work; and clients surprised by a slip they caused and were never told about.

## Impact If Fixed
A contract silent on the client's ongoing development implicitly assigns all of its cost to the studio, and nobody notices until the schedule is gone. One clause stating an assumed cadence, plus measurement from week one, makes it negotiable.
