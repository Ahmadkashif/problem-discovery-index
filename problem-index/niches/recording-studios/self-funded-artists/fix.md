# The Album That Stops at Song Seven

**Niche:** [[niches/recording-studios/self-funded-artists/profile|Self-Funded Independent Artists]]
**Industry:** [[industries/recording-studios|Recording Studios]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Hourly billing with no running forecast lets a self-funded album spend its whole budget on the first seven songs, and nobody notices until the money is gone.
**Tags:** #time-series-forecasting #linear-regression #worker-facing #quick-win #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to sell a self-funded artist a fixed price for a release-ready record and then deliver it without the hours running over, and whoever estimates a project's real hours best keeps the artist's next release.

## The Problem
Tracking runs long because the first songs take longest; overdubs expand; the artist wants to try one more idea. Each day is billed, but nobody shows the artist how much of the budget is gone against how much of the record is finished. When the money runs out, the last songs and the mixes remain, and the project stalls, sometimes for good.

## Why It's Still Broken
Studios bill time, not progress, and owner-engineers are busy recording rather than managing budgets. Raising budget concerns mid-session feels like bad customer service. The artist doesn't know what normal looks like.

## What a Fix Looks Like
A burn-down view per project updated from the session log: budget spent, songs at each phase, and the projected cost to finish at the current pace, sent to the artist weekly or after each day. When the projection crosses the budget, both sides get a prompt to decide (drop a song, simplify overdubs, move mixing to a fixed price) while there is still money to finish.

## Who Feels the Pain
Self-funded artists left with unfinished records; studio owners who lose the client and the review.

## Impact If Fixed
More projects finished within budget, and a decision forced early enough to save the release.
