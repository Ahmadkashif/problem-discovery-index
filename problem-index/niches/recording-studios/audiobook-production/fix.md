## The Problem
Genre fiction, where audiobook revenue concentrates, comes in long series. A narrator voices dozens of recurring characters, each with a pitch, accent, pace and verbal habit. Between instalments they record many other books. When the next one arrives they listen back through old files to find each character's first appearance, and listeners still notice when a voice has drifted.

## Why It's Still Broken
The reference for each voice is buried in hours of finished audio with no index by character. Notes, where they exist, are a few words in a notebook. Production houses don't own the narrator's prep, so nobody builds the archive.

## What a Fix Looks Like
A character-voice library built automatically from finished books: speaker and character attribution aligned to the manuscript, a short reference clip for each character's key lines, and a description of pitch and pace. Before a session the narrator plays the clips for every character in the next chapter. During proofing, each line attributed to a character is compared against that character's reference so drift is flagged.

## Who Feels the Pain
Narrators of long series, their directors, and publishers whose series sales depend on listeners staying through every volume.

## Impact If Fixed
Hours of prep per instalment saved, and fewer one-star reviews blaming a changed voice on a series where each book sells the next.
