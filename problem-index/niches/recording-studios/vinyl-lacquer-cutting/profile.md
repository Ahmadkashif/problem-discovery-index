# Vinyl Lacquer Cutting

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/recording-studios|Recording Studios]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to cut lacquers that pass the test pressing first time, and whoever wastes the fewest scarce lacquers and plant-queue weeks on recuts keeps the label's vinyl work.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$40M US spend on lacquer cutting, direct metal mastering and vinyl pre-mastering (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~1% (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Low — the lathe is analogue, set by ear, meter and microscope; orders and test-pressing feedback arrive by email
**Target Buyer:** Owner-cutting engineer of a mastering room with a lathe; label production manager
**Automation Potential:** Low for the cut itself; medium for pre-cut analysis and feedback capture

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Cutting turns a digital master into a groove on a lacquer disc, which the pressing plant plates and presses. It is one of the few steps in modern recording that is physical, done on lathes that are mostly decades old, by a small number of engineers (a few dozen US rooms, estimate). The supply of blank lacquers has been fragile since the February 2020 fire at Apollo Masters left Japan's MDC as the only lacquer maker. The engineer trades off side length, level, bass and sibilance by ear. The verdict comes weeks later, when the label or artist hears a test pressing from a plant with its own queue (pressing plants are a separate industry still queued, media replication plants). A failed test means a recut, more lacquers and a new place in that queue.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Neumann lathes with Ortofon or Westrex cutting heads; microscopes to inspect the groove; meters for phase correlation and level; mastering software for the pre-master. The gaps: no record of which settings produced passing tests on which material; plant defect reports and client test-pressing notes arrive as unstructured emails; and the judgement of how hot a side can be cut lives in each engineer's ear.
