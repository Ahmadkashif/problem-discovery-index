# Packaging Brand & Shelf Design

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/graphic-design-studios|Graphic Design Studios]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to prove, before the retailer's shelf reset, that a redesign will be found faster and chosen more often than the pack it replaces, and whoever brings shelf evidence the brand manager can defend takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.2B US fees for packaging brand design, redesigns and range architecture (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~7% (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Low-Medium — design is digital, but evidence comes from expensive consumer tests or from nobody at all
**Target Buyer:** Brand manager or packaging innovation lead at a consumer-goods company; on the studio side, the strategy or creative director
**Automation Potential:** Low-Medium — shelf simulation and attention prediction automate; the concept does not

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
A pack redesign is one of the riskiest decisions a brand manager makes: the current pack carries years of shopper recognition, and a redesign that makes the product harder to find can lose sales before anyone notices why. Brand managers therefore want evidence before the retailer's planogram reset, and the studio that can supply it — or design so that the evidence comes back favourable — wins the work. The contest is about shelf findability and choice, judged in a competitive set, not about artwork accuracy, which is why it separates from production.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Pack testing from specialist research firms (Behaviorally, formerly PRS IN VIVO, runs eye-tracking and simulated-shelf studies; Ipsos and Kantar offer equivalents), predictive attention tools such as 3M's Visual Attention Software and Dragonfly AI, and in-store photographs taken by the brand's own team. Gaps: testing is costly and slow, so smaller brands skip it; predictive attention models are trained largely on web and advertising imagery rather than shelves; and studios rarely see the test results or the in-market sales that would tell them which of their design decisions work.
