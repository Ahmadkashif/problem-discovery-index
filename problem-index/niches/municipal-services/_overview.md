## Why These Niches

Municipal services span thousands of local governments with wildly different budgets, populations, and technology maturity. Water/sewer and parks represent the two largest budget categories and highest citizen-interaction volume — the revenue centers of municipal operations. Code enforcement and small-town public works are operationally critical but still run on paper forms, spreadsheets, and phone calls in most jurisdictions. ADA accessibility and rural permitting serve populations that commercial software vendors routinely ignore because deal sizes are small. Utility billing and seasonal workforce management are high-volume, rule-heavy processes where automation can eliminate tens of thousands of manual hours annually. Excluded: fire/EMS, police, and transit, which have their own mature vendor ecosystems.

## Why These Pockets

Government procurement kills most of the positions inside this value chain, and the strong pocket sits entirely outside it — on the capital markets side, where the buyer is a rating agency rather than a city.

Municipal credit ratings determine what every city, school district, and water utility in the country pays to borrow, which is the difference between a water main replaced and deferred. The rating agencies' public finance groups hold financial statements, budgets, and operating data for essentially every US municipal issuer collected over decades, joined to a multi-decade record of which credits actually deteriorated or defaulted — and assign each rating by having an analyst read one issuer's documents against published criteria.

The corpus is not used to predict. Municipal defaults are rare, which is exactly the regime where only systematic analysis across a large population finds what individual experience cannot, and rating changes are instead driven by surveillance calendars — so an issuer whose fundamentals have deteriorated for three years is downgraded when their analyst next looks. The post-crisis regulatory emphasis on analyst judgment and criteria transparency is the stated reason for keeping models away from ratings, and it does not apply to deciding which of an analyst's fifty credits to examine first, which is where the value is.

Two supporting problems. Every input is a several-hundred-page audited report with no standard layout, filed a year or more late, where the analytically meaningful figures require combining across funds and the earliest warnings sit in the notes as prose — so extraction is the constraint on both coverage depth and surveillance frequency. And the qualitative judgment that separates two credits with identical ratios — whether a council can actually raise rates, how a state behaves when a city fails, whether a manager's projections have historically held — is recorded as a letter and a published paragraph, and leaves with the analyst.
