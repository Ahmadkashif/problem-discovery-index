# Fix: Three Days of a Senior Person Formatting

**Niche:** Assessment Deliverable Production
**Industry:** [[industries/fractional-cto-services|Fractional CTO Services]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The most expensive person in the practice spends the last three days of every engagement rebuilding charts and reconciling an executive summary with a body that keeps changing.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #workflow-orchestration #automation #worker-facing #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Whether the report that carries the engagement's judgement is assembled from the evidence that produced it or retyped from scratch.

## The Problem

The analysis is done. What remains is production, and production is where the practitioner's rate and the work's nature diverge most sharply.

The specific tasks: retyping metrics from the analysis into prose. Rebuilding charts in the presentation tool because the analysis produced them in a different one. Applying house formatting to sections written at speed. Writing the executive summary, then rewriting it after a body edit, then rewriting it again. Producing the board version, then propagating a late change into both. Scanning for leftover text from the previous client. Checking that the severity in the summary matches the severity in the body.

None of it requires the person doing it. All of it is done by that person, because it is entangled with the content and delegating it means briefing someone on a document that is still moving. In a ten-day assessment this is regularly three days, which is thirty per cent of the engagement's cost on work that produces none of its value.

It is also where the errors are. Every mismatched figure and stale reference that has ever embarrassed an advisory firm came from this phase, under deadline, at the end of a tiring engagement.

## Why It's Still Broken

**It is billed.** The client pays for a ten-day engagement, and whether three of those days were spent formatting is invisible to them. The practice has no external pressure to fix it, and the internal pressure — utilisation looks fine — points the other way.

**Delegation is genuinely hard.** An analyst or editor could do most of it if the document were stable, and it is not. The practitioner is still changing findings on day nine, so handing it over creates coordination cost that exceeds the saving. This is a real reason, not an excuse, and it is why the fix must be structural rather than organisational.

**Craft identity.** Practitioners often take pride in the document and resist anything that looks like industrialising it. The pride is legitimate and attaches to the argument, not to the chart rebuild, but the two feel connected when someone proposes a process change.

**Every engagement has a bespoke shape.** Templates get abandoned because each client's situation demands a slightly different structure, so the reusable portion is smaller than it first appears and previous reports get copied instead.

**Nobody measures it.** Practices track billable days, not what happened inside them. The three days are invisible in every system the firm runs, so the problem has never been quantified and therefore never prioritised.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Measure it first.** A practice that tracks production hours separately for two months will find a number large enough to force action. This costs nothing and is the prerequisite for everything else, because nobody funds a fix for a cost they cannot state.

**Separate the stable from the bespoke.** Most reports share eighty per cent of their structure and differ in the middle. Fixing the shared parts — front matter, methodology, scope, appendices, the framing of recurring findings — as genuine reusable components removes most of the work without constraining the part that has to be bespoke. The mistake previous template efforts made was templating the whole thing.

**Stop rebuilding charts.** Whatever produced the analysis should emit the visual in the report's format directly. This is usually a one-off week of setup and recovers hours per engagement forever, and almost no practice has done it.

**Write the summary from the findings, last and mechanically.** If findings are structured, the summary is an assembly rather than a composition, and the reconciliation problem disappears. Practitioners who resist this are usually resisting generated prose; assembling their own sentences in the right order is a different proposition.

**Make the pre-delivery check a checklist.** Figure consistency, severity consistency, no previous-client residue, every summary finding present in the body, every claim evidenced. Ten minutes, mechanical, catches the failures that actually damage reputations.

**Hire one production person.** For a practice above about eight practitioners, an editor or analyst who owns the last two days of every engagement is straightforwardly profitable — they cost a fraction of a practitioner day rate and recover senior days that can be billed. The obstacle is structural rather than economic: the document must stabilise earlier, which means the practice has to adopt a genuine draft-freeze discipline.

## Who Feels the Pain

The practitioner, ending every engagement with three days of tired formatting and a late night before delivery, which is a meaningful contributor to why this work exhausts people.

The practice owner, paying senior rates for document assembly and unable to see the cost in any system they run.

The client, twice: paying an advisory rate for production work, and occasionally receiving the mismatched figure that survived the last-night edit.

And the next engagement, which does not start because the practitioner is finishing this one.

## Impact If Fixed

Twenty to thirty per cent of engagement capacity returned, which in a capacity-constrained professional services business is the whole growth question.

The reliability failures stop. The errors in this phase are disproportionately visible — a client who finds one mismatched number discounts the whole report — and they are entirely preventable with a checklist.

And the practitioner ends the engagement on the argument rather than on the formatting, which is both a better use of them and a materially better job.
