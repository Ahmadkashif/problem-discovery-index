# Fix: Authority Without Standing

**Niche:** The Senior Engineer Left Behind
**Industry:** [[industries/fractional-cto-services|Fractional CTO Services]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The engineer who inherits the plan is given its responsibility and none of the authority that got it approved, and nobody involved acknowledges the gap.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #compliance #confidence-intervals
**Contested on:** Whether the person who inherits a half-executed technical direction can reconstruct enough of its reasoning to own it.

## The Problem

The fractional CTO had authority from three sources: a title, a price, and being an outsider whose judgement was purchased precisely because it was independent. When they recommended restructuring a team, the recommendation carried all three.

The engineer who inherits the plan has none of them. Usually no new title, frequently no pay change, and definitely not the outsider's licence. They are a colleague, known to everyone, who yesterday was writing code and today is expected to hold a technical direction in leadership meetings against a head of product who wants the roadmap changed and a CFO who wants the spend explained.

The failures follow predictably. A prioritisation decision the fractional CTO would have made in a sentence becomes a week of negotiation. A recommendation to restructure the team, agreed in principle while the advisor was present, quietly stops happening. The head of product, who deferred to the advisor, does not defer to a peer. And when a decision needs to be reversed — which is the most valuable thing the inheritor could do, since they can see the plan no longer fits — they have no standing to reopen a question the board settled.

The engineer absorbs this as personal failure. They are not failing. They have been handed a role that was never constituted.

## Why It's Still Broken

**Nobody decided it.** There is no moment at which someone appoints the inheritor. The advisor leaves, questions arrive, and the most senior engineer answers them. Because there is no decision, there is no title, no mandate, no conversation about scope, and no one accountable for the arrangement.

**The company saves money by not noticing.** Constituting the role means a title, a raise and possibly backfilling the engineering work. Leaving it ambiguous means the work gets done for free by someone who will not complain, and the saving is invisible because the cost is borne entirely by one person.

**The advisor's incentive ends at delivery.** Whether the plan survives depends heavily on whether someone is positioned to carry it, and no practitioner is paid to ensure that. Most would help if asked. Nobody asks, because raising it at the end of an engagement sounds like asking the client to spend more.

**The engineer will not ask.** Requesting authority is culturally difficult for engineers, reads as a status grab, and risks being told no — after which their position is worse. So they absorb the ambiguity and hope competence substitutes for standing. It does not.

**No one is watching for it.** In a company without a technology executive, the person best placed to notice the inheritor is drowning is the technology executive who left.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Constitute the role at the engagement's end, explicitly.** The final act of the engagement should be a documented transition: who owns this direction, what decisions they can make alone, what needs escalation, who they escalate to, and what changes about their title, pay and remaining engineering load. Half an hour of the advisor's time, in a room with the CEO, at a moment when the CEO is receptive because they are worried about the plan. The advisor is the only person in the building who can raise this without it sounding self-interested, which makes it their job.

**Transfer authority visibly.** The CEO states in a leadership meeting, with the advisor present, that this person now owns the direction and that their decisions on it stand. Standing is conferred socially, in public, or not at all — a line in a document does nothing. This costs nothing and is skipped almost universally.

**Define the escalation path.** The inheritor needs somewhere to take decisions above their level. Without it they either overreach, which ends badly, or stall, which ends slowly. A named person and a standing slot solves it.

**Retain a thin advisory line.** A monthly hour with the departed practitioner for six months, contracted at the outset. Cheap, and it gives the inheritor both a source for the rationale and — more importantly — an external voice they can invoke when a decision is challenged. It also gives the practitioner the outcome visibility that everything in [[niches/fractional-cto-services/assessment-calibration/profile|🎯 Assessment Calibration]] depends on, so both sides gain.

**Explicitly license reversal.** The inheritor must be told, in public, that changing the plan when conditions change is part of what they own and not a betrayal of the investment. Without that licence, plans get executed past their expiry by people who can see the problem and do not feel permitted to name it — which is the most expensive failure in this entire niche.

**Reduce the other load.** Someone doing a technical leadership job on top of a full engineering workload will do both badly. Naming what comes off their plate is part of constituting the role, and it is the part companies most consistently refuse.

## Who Feels the Pain

The engineer, acutely and usually silently, often concluding after a bad year that they are not leadership material when what actually happened is that they were handed a role nobody constituted.

The company, which watches a direction it paid for decay and attributes it to the plan or the person rather than to the arrangement.

The other engineers, who report to someone with unclear authority and get inconsistent decisions as a result.

And the profession, whose outcomes are systematically worse than its assessments deserve because the final and cheapest step — making sure someone is positioned to carry the work — is nobody's responsibility.

## Impact If Fixed

A half-hour transition conversation and a public statement of authority are close to free and materially change whether a six-figure direction survives. The ratio of cost to effect here is among the best in this vault.

The engineer gets a real opportunity instead of an ambush. Many people in this position would grow into genuinely good technical leaders given a constituted role, and are instead broken by an unconstituted one.

And the advisory practice gets better outcomes on the thing it sells. A practitioner whose plans survive is worth more than one whose plans are excellent and stranded, and right now the market cannot tell the difference because the failure happens after everyone has stopped looking.
