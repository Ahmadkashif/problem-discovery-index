# The Fractional CTO

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/fractional-cto-services|Fractional CTO Services]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Whether a practitioner holding four unrelated complex situations can reload each one to real depth in minutes, or pays a context-switching tax that quietly caps how many clients they can serve well.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$300M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~10%
**Digital Adoption:** Low — notes, memory and calendar
**Target Buyer:** Independent fractional practitioners and small practices
**Automation Potential:** High for context reload, none for the judgement

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

Four clients, four codebases, four sets of team politics and four crises that do not coordinate their timing, held by one person whose entire value is depth of understanding. That is the working condition of the fractional practitioner, and it is unlike almost any other professional arrangement.

The depth is the product. A fractional CTO is worth their rate because they hold a rich model of a company's system, its people and its constraints, and can bring it to bear in a thirty-minute conversation. The fractional structure is what makes that affordable — a company that cannot pay for a full-time technology executive gets a day a week of a very good one.

The two are in tension. Holding four rich models simultaneously, with two or three days between visits to each, means every session begins with a reload: what was happening here, what did I commit to, what has changed since, what did that engineer say about the migration, what is the political situation with the head of product. The reload is unbilled, imperfect and exhausting, and it degrades as the client count rises. Most practitioners cap themselves at three or four not because the calendar is full but because a fifth would make all five worse.

This is an underserved audience in the strict sense: the buyers are individuals or tiny firms with no procurement process, no budget line for tooling, and a working pattern nothing was designed for.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Practitioners run on general-purpose tools: a notes app per client, a calendar, a to-do list, and increasingly a meeting transcription service. The organised ones maintain a per-client document that they update after each session. Personal knowledge management systems — Obsidian, Notion, Roam — have real adoption among the more systematic practitioners. Client access is fragmented across four Slacks, four email domains, four issue trackers and four repository hosts, each with its own credentials and none of them talking to the others.

The gaps are specific. Nothing assembles a reload brief before a session. Nothing tracks commitments made across all clients in one place, so promises made in a Tuesday conversation surface a fortnight later only if the practitioner wrote them down. Nothing watches a client's systems between visits, so anything that happened in the gap arrives as a surprise in the meeting. Nothing carries the pattern of what worked at one client into the situation at another, which is the practitioner's principal advantage and is entirely unassisted. And the multi-tenant access problem — four organisations, strict separation, no cross-contamination — is exactly what no personal tool handles.
