# Handover & Continuity

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/fractional-cto-services|Fractional CTO Services]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Whether the reasoning behind a technical direction survives the departure of the person who set it, or leaves with them and strands the plan halfway through.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$330M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~11%
**Digital Adoption:** Very low — a final deck and a farewell
**Target Buyer:** Client leadership, incoming engineering leaders, practice owners
**Automation Potential:** Moderate — capture automates, reasoning does not

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

A fractional engagement ends by design. The practitioner arrives, assesses, recommends, sets a direction in motion, and leaves — that is the product, and the temporariness is why it is affordable. What is not by design is that the reasoning leaves with them.

A technical direction is not a list of steps. It is a set of decisions, each with a rationale, a set of assumptions about the business and the team, a set of conditions under which it should be revisited, and a set of alternatives that were considered and rejected for reasons. The deliverable captures the steps. Almost none of the rest survives, because it was never written down — it lived in conversations, in the practitioner's head, and in the shared context of people who were in the room.

Six months later the plan is half executed, a condition has changed, and nobody left in the building can say whether the change invalidates the plan, because nobody knows what the plan assumed. The organisation continues executing a direction it cannot evaluate, or abandons it and loses the investment already made.

This is a distinct market because the buyer is the client rather than the practitioner, the failure happens entirely after the engagement, and the fix is a deliverable format rather than an analysis tool. It is also the niche where the profession's structure — expertise rented by the day — creates a cost the profession does not price.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Architecture decision records are the closest existing practice and are genuinely good: a short document per decision capturing context, options, choice and consequences. Adoption is patchy and they are written by engineering teams for themselves rather than by advisors for a successor. Documentation platforms, wikis and knowledge bases hold whatever anyone puts in them. Some consultancies run structured handover checklists. Transition planning is mature in interim executive search and absent in technical advisory.

The gaps: nothing captures rationale as a by-product of the engagement rather than as a final task nobody has time for. Nothing records the assumptions a direction depends on in a form that can be checked later, or the trigger conditions under which a decision should be reopened. Nothing connects a recommendation to the person who will own it after the handover. And the deliverable format itself — a persuasive deck aimed at a board — is structurally wrong for the job of surviving its author.
