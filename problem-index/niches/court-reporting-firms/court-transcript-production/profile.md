# Court and Appeal Transcript Production

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/court-reporting-firms|Court Reporting Firms]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to deliver certified transcripts of court proceedings, from an official reporter's notes or the court's own audio, inside the appellate record deadline at the statutory page rate, and whoever clears appeal transcripts without extension requests takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$600M US spend on court transcripts produced by official reporters' private transcript businesses, contract reporters and approved transcription companies working from court audio (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~15% (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — more than 45 states record some proceedings digitally (Ducker), but orders, deposits and deadlines run on forms and email
**Target Buyer:** Court reporting services manager or court administrator; appellate counsel and public defenders ordering records
**Automation Potential:** Medium-High for audio-based transcripts; lower where the official's steno notes are the only record

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
An appeal cannot proceed without the record, and the record of what was said is the transcript. Prices are set by statute or court rule, not by the market. In federal court the Judicial Conference caps the page rate from $4.40 for 30-day delivery to $8.70 for hourly. Appellate rules require the transcript by a set date and an extension request when it slips. Official reporters employed by courts often sell transcripts as a private side business on their own time, and courts that record digitally send audio to approved transcription companies. The court systems themselves belong to the queued courts industry; this niche is the transcript production they buy.

## Current Tools & Gaps
CAT software for officials, court audio systems (For The Record, JAVS, CourtSmart), transcriber portals, and court case management (Tyler Odyssey and others). The gaps: nobody checks audio quality until a transcript is ordered months later, and backlogs per reporter are tracked by appellate clerks one extension at a time.
