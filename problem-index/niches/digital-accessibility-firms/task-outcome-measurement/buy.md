## The Problem
Usability practice settled long ago that the unit of measurement is the task: define the tasks users come to perform, observe whether they complete them, measure time and error, and report success rate with the failures explained. It is the discipline's foundational method. Accessibility grew up alongside it, under regulatory and legal pressure, and adopted a standards-conformance frame instead — measuring the artefact rather than the outcome.

## What Already Exists
Task-based test design; completion rate, time and error measurement; severity rating by impact on task; think-aloud protocols; and reporting that ties every finding to a task consequence.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to users whose interaction is mediated by assistive technology with its own behaviour. It requires: (1) the technology stack being part of the test condition — a flow may complete with one screen reader and fail with another, so the matrix is a first-class dimension no usability study has — this is the substantive difference; (2) participants who are expert assistive technology users, whose expertise means a flow they complete may still be unusable for a less practised user; (3) findings that must map to standards criteria for legal and procurement purposes as well as to tasks; (4) severity defined by whether a barrier is absolute rather than by time lost; and (5) an evidentiary standard, since the output may be used in a legal context.

## Target Customer
Accessibility firms, client accessibility and product leadership, procurement functions, and usability research providers.

## Impact If Solved
Usability settled that the unit of measurement is the task and built the methods. The assistive technology matrix as a first-class test condition, and findings that must also map to criteria, is what accessibility has to add.
