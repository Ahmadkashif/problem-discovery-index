# Can the Person Finish

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-accessibility-firms/task-outcome-measurement/profile|Task Outcome Measurement]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-accessibility-firms|Digital Accessibility Firms]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** The report counts criteria failures and the question is whether somebody could buy the ticket.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #compliance #confidence-intervals #descriptive-statistics #automation #data-integration #hypothesis-testing #worker-facing
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to state whether a disabled person can complete the task they came for, rather than how many standards criteria a page fails — and whoever measures that takes the account.

## The Problem
Accessibility work is delivered as conformance: a list of success criteria failures, located and rated. The thing that matters is whether a disabled person using assistive technology can complete a task — find the product, book the appointment, submit the claim. The two diverge in both directions, so an organisation can invest heavily, close every finding, and still have users who cannot complete a purchase. Nothing in the standard deliverable would reveal that.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
Conformance is cheap, documentable and defensible in a demand letter, and outcome measurement is none of those things. Task testing requires people using assistive technology, which is expensive and has not been productised. Legal incentives reward documented effort. And nobody has been asked for an outcome statement.

## What to Build
Define the tasks, test them properly, and report completion. Establish the small set of flows that matter — the tasks users actually come to do — with the client, which is the core and is the step that reframes the entire engagement. Test completion of those flows with real assistive technology across the combinations users actually have, rather than auditing pages against criteria. Report completion rate and blocking failures per flow per technology, which is a statement about people rather than a count of defects. Link every blocking failure to the criterion it violates, so the conformance deliverable is produced as a by-product rather than replaced. Cover the combinations that matter rather than all of them, since coverage is a cost decision that should be made explicitly. Re-test the flows on a cadence rather than annually, as the flows change continuously. Report severity by whether a task is blocked, degraded or merely inelegant, which is the distinction the current severity ratings do not make. Include disabled specialists as the assessors rather than as participants, which is both correct and better. Produce evidence a legal process can rely on, since that is what the client is also buying. And state plainly what was not tested, because an outcome claim is only as good as its scope.

## Target Customer
Accessibility firms and consultancies, client accessibility and product leadership, procurement functions requiring assurance, and regulators and advocacy bodies.

## Impact If Built
Conformance and task completion come apart in both directions, so closing every finding can leave users unable to buy. Testing the flows that matter across real assistive technology turns a list into a statement about people.
