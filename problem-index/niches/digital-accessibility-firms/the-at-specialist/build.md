# Engaging the Expertise as Expertise

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-accessibility-firms/the-at-specialist/profile|The Assistive Technology Specialist]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-accessibility-firms|Digital Accessibility Firms]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** The people whose judgement the whole assessment depends on are engaged as subjects rather than as assessors.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #evaluation-metrics #compliance #revenue-impact #descriptive-statistics #tacit-knowledge-ml #data-integration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to engage the disabled experts who can actually say whether a site works as specialists rather than as research participants — and whoever builds that practice takes the account.

## The Problem
Whether a site works with a screen reader is a question that an expert screen reader user can answer authoritatively and nobody else can. The industry's standard way of accessing that expertise is a research session: recruit a participant, run a protocol, observe. The expertise is treated as lived experience to be observed rather than as professional judgement to be engaged, which sets the rate, the standing of the finding and the career prospects of the people involved.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
Research recruitment is the existing channel and it works procedurally. There is no credentialing route that distinguishes an expert assessor from a participant. Firms' cost models were built around participant rates. And the reframing requires the industry to change how it describes its own method.

## What to Build
Build the professional engagement route the expertise warrants. Establish a specialist engagement model with professional rates and contracted assessment rather than participant recruitment, which is the core and is a commercial and structural change rather than a technical one. Create a credentialing route that recognises assistive technology expertise as a specialism, since the absence of one is why the distinction is not made. Build tooling designed for an expert assessor — capture, annotation, criterion mapping — rather than for observing a participant, which is a different product. Give specialists a route into permanent roles within accessibility firms, which several already do and most do not. Publish rate benchmarks for the specialism, as the absence of any reference is what sustains participant-level pay. Let specialists sign their assessments, which matters for standing and for legal weight. Support the range of assistive technologies rather than defaulting to one, since the expertise is technology-specific. Route the assessment work rather than the observation work to them, which is the substantive change. Build a practitioner community, as the expertise is currently isolated. And ensure the firms' own products and tooling are usable by the specialists themselves, which is embarrassingly often not the case.

## Target Customer
Accessibility firms and consultancies, assistive technology specialists, disability advocacy and professional bodies, and client accessibility functions.

## Impact If Built
The expertise the whole assessment rests on is accessed through a research protocol rather than a professional engagement, which sets the rate and the standing. A specialist engagement model with credentialing changes both.
