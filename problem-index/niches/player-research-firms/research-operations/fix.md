# The Participant Who Did Not Turn Up

**Niche:** [[niches/player-research-firms/research-operations/profile|Research Operations]]
**Industry:** [[industries/player-research-firms|Player Research Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Two of the eight booked participants did not arrive, the sessions are gone, and the sample is now six.
**Tags:** #quick-win #automation #workflow-orchestration #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #logistic-regression #data-integration #confidence-intervals
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to get the right participants into the right sessions at the right time without consuming the research team, and whoever automates that takes the account.

## The Problem
No-shows are routine and expensive. A booked slot with a moderator, a facility and often an observing client goes unused, and the study's already-small sample shrinks further. The response is a scramble for a replacement, which usually means taking whoever is available — which is exactly the recruitment bias the study was trying to avoid. The rate is predictable and nobody manages it as a rate.

## Why It's Still Broken
Nobody treats it as a predictable rate — a no-show handled as an individual surprise each time will never be planned for, even when it happens in one session out of six reliably. Reminders are manual and inconsistent. Overbooking feels wasteful. And the replacement's effect on the sample is not recorded.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Measure the rate and plan against it. Track the no-show rate by participant type, study format and lead time, which is the fix and turns a surprise into a number. Overbook by the measured rate rather than booking exactly, which is the standard remedy and costs less than an empty slot. Send reminders on a schedule that has been tested rather than assumed, since the cadence matters more than most teams think. Confirm the day before with a response required, which converts a silent no-show into an early one. Maintain a standby list of pre-screened participants who match the criteria, so replacements do not degrade the sample. Pay a partial incentive for timely cancellation, which is cheap and materially reduces silent no-shows. Shorten the lead time between recruitment and session where possible, as the rate rises sharply with it. Record every replacement and its effect on sample composition, which keeps the sampling picture honest. Report the achieved sample against the planned one in the findings. And handle remote sessions separately, since their no-show behaviour is different.

## Who Feels the Pain
Researchers with a smaller sample than designed; moderators with an empty slot in a full day; clients who travelled to observe; and the study's conclusions, drawn from six people instead of eight.

## Impact If Fixed
A no-show handled as an individual surprise each time will never be planned for, even when it happens reliably in one session out of six. Measuring the rate and overbooking against it is planning rather than a scramble.
