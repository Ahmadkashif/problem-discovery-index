# Six Sessions Then Write Them Up

**Niche:** [[niches/player-research-firms/the-moderator/profile|The Moderator]]
**Industry:** [[industries/player-research-firms|Player Research Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The schedule puts six sessions in a day because the participants were available, and the analysis is due Thursday.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #quick-win #workflow-orchestration #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #automation #data-integration #compliance
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to make back-to-back moderated sessions sustainable for the person running them, who is also the person writing them up — and whoever does that takes the account.

## The Problem
Session schedules are built around participant availability and studio access, not around the moderator. Six sessions in a day with twenty minutes between them is common. By the fifth the moderator is running on procedure rather than attention, which is when the observation that would have mattered is missed. Then the analysis is due, produced by the same person, from a week that already exhausted them.

## Why It's Still Broken
Nobody constrains the schedule — a calendar built entirely around participant and facility availability will fill every slot, because no input to it represents the moderator's capacity. The cost shows up as quality nobody measures. Moderators do not object because it is normal. And the analysis deadline is fixed independently.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Put a limit in the scheduling constraints and split the roles where you can. Cap sessions per moderator per day as a scheduling rule, which is the fix and is a constraint in a calendar rather than a policy debate. Build real gaps between sessions rather than turnaround time, since a gap that is only long enough to reset the room is not a break. Rotate moderators within a study where the protocol allows, which spreads the load and adds a second perspective. Assign note-taking to an observer, which is the single largest reduction in in-session load. Separate the analysis owner from the moderator on longer studies. Schedule analysis time that does not immediately follow the session block, which is where the compression currently lands. Track sessions per moderator per week as an operational metric, since an unmeasured load is never managed. Flag the fifth and sixth sessions as lower-confidence in the analysis, which is honest and surprisingly rarely done. Ask moderators to set their own limits and respect them. And price studies with realistic session counts rather than winning them on a schedule nobody can run well.

## Who Feels the Pain
Moderators depleted by the fifth session and then analysing; clients receiving findings from sessions where attention had gone; participants in a session with a tired moderator; and firms losing experienced people to a schedule.

## Impact If Fixed
A calendar built entirely around participant and facility availability will fill every slot, because no input to it represents the moderator's capacity. A daily cap and a dedicated note-taker are scheduling constraints, not a policy debate.
