# Asking the Question Nobody Asks

**Niche:** [[niches/player-research-firms/implementation-tracking/profile|Implementation Tracking]]
**Industry:** [[industries/player-research-firms|Player Research Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Two months after the readout nobody has asked the client what they did, and the client would have told them.
**Tags:** #quick-win #workflow-orchestration #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #automation #data-integration #worker-facing #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to find out which of its recommendations the development team actually acted on — and whoever establishes that takes the account.

## The Problem
The simplest version of the whole problem: the firm never asks. The relationship is good, the client is reachable, and a short message two months after the readout asking which recommendations were taken would be answered. It does not happen because the project closed, the researcher moved to the next study, and nobody's role includes it. The information is available for the cost of an email and is not collected.

## Why It's Still Broken
Nobody's job includes the follow-up — a task that belongs to no role after a project closes will not happen, however cheap it is and however much everyone agrees it would be useful. The engagement is billed and complete. Researchers are on the next study. And asking feels like it needs a reason.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Put the follow-up in the process and keep it short. Schedule a follow-up at project close rather than relying on anyone remembering, which is the fix and is a calendar entry. Keep it to three questions, since a short message gets answered and a survey does not. Ask at the readout what the team intends to do, as that is the cheapest data point and is free. Assign the follow-up to whoever holds the account rather than to the researcher, which suits both roles better. Record answers in a consistent form so they accumulate rather than sitting in a mailbox. Report the aggregate rate quarterly, which is what makes the practice stick. Frame it as improving the firm's work rather than as auditing the client, which changes how it is received entirely. Include the rejected recommendations explicitly, since the reasons are the valuable part. Follow up once and not repeatedly, as persistence costs goodwill and adds little. And use what comes back in the next proposal to that client, which is where it pays for itself.

## Who Feels the Pain
Researchers who never learn whether their work mattered; firms with no measure of their own influence; clients who would have answered; and a discipline that could have been accumulating this all along.

## Impact If Fixed
A task that belongs to no role after a project closes will not happen, however cheap it is and however much everyone agrees it would be useful. A scheduled three-question follow-up is a calendar entry that starts the whole loop.
