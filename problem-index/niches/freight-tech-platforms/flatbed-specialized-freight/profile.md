# Flatbed & Specialised Freight

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/freight-tech-platforms|Freight Tech Platforms]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in specialised freight software is fighting to plan and re-plan a legal, permitted route for an oversize load without a permit service and a telephone — and whoever turns permitted routing into a self-serve minute takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$540M US software spend across flatbed, heavy haul, oversize and specialised carriers and the permit services around them
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~7% of freight technology revenue
**Digital Adoption:** Low — the segment runs on permit services, phone calls and experienced dispatchers
**Target Buyer:** Owners and dispatchers at flatbed and heavy haul carriers; permit service providers; project cargo forwarders
**Automation Potential:** High — permit rules and route restrictions are published state data that nobody has assembled

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Specialised freight is the part of trucking that dry van software does not describe. A flatbed load requires securement decisions that depend on the commodity and are safety-critical. An oversize or overweight load requires permits from every state and sometimes every county it crosses, each with its own application process, fee schedule, lead time, and route restrictions — bridge weight limits, overhead clearances, curfews, escort requirements, holiday and weekend restrictions. A route that is legal in one state becomes illegal at the border. Planning one is a skilled job performed by dispatchers and permit services using state websites, telephone calls and accumulated knowledge, and re-planning one mid-move because a bridge is under construction is worse. The general freight platforms model none of this, so the segment with the highest per-load complexity has the least software.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Permit services are the dominant intermediary and operate largely as a service business with staff and relationships. Routing products with truck restrictions exist — the commercial truck navigation providers model height, weight and hazmat restrictions reasonably well — but stop short of permitted oversize routing, which requires the permit rules rather than only the road attributes. Some states offer automated permitting portals and many do not. Load boards carry flatbed freight without modelling its requirements. The consistent gaps: no assembled cross-state permit rule set, no route planner that produces a permittable route rather than a legal-for-standard-truck route, no securement guidance connected to the commodity, and no mechanism for re-planning when a route becomes unavailable mid-move.
