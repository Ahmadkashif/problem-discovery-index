# Securement Knowledge That Lives in One Driver's Head

**Niche:** [[niches/freight-tech-platforms/flatbed-specialized-freight/profile|Flatbed & Specialised Freight]]
**Industry:** [[industries/freight-tech-platforms|Freight Tech Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** How to secure a specific commodity on a flatbed is safety-critical, regulated, learned by apprenticeship, and captured nowhere — so a carrier's securement competence is a property of which drivers happen to be employed this year.
**Tags:** #cnns #object-detection #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #compliance #worker-facing #tacit-knowledge-ml
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in specialised freight software is fighting to plan and re-plan a legal, permitted route for an oversize load without a permit service and a telephone — and whoever turns permitted routing into a self-serve minute takes the account.

## The Problem
A driver loads steel coils, or lumber bundles, or a piece of machinery with an awkward centre of gravity. Federal cargo securement regulations specify working load limits, the number and placement of tiedowns, and commodity-specific rules. Applying them to the actual load in front of you — what to block, where to place the chains, how to handle a piece that is heavier at one end — is judgement built over years. When an experienced flatbed driver retires, that judgement leaves. Newer drivers learn from whoever is around, inconsistently. The consequences range from a violation at a roadside inspection to lost cargo on an interstate.

## Why It's Still Broken
Securement has been taught by apprenticeship because it genuinely is physical judgement, and the industry has never had a medium that transfers it — a regulation document specifies minimums and does not tell you how to secure this particular coil on this particular trailer. Carriers train, and the training is generic. There is also a liability caution: a company that provides specific securement instruction assumes a responsibility it currently leaves with the driver, which is a real legal consideration and also an uncomfortable allocation of risk onto the person with the least protection.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Capture and transfer what the experienced drivers know. Commodity-specific securement guidance — as short video and annotated photographs contributed by the carrier's own experienced drivers, reviewed for regulatory compliance — is a medium that matches the knowledge. Present it by commodity at the moment of loading, on a phone, in the driver's language. Compute the regulatory minimum automatically from the commodity, weight and configuration, so working load limit arithmetic stops being mental. Photograph the secured load before departure, which is a thirty-second act that creates an inspection record, a damage-claim defence and a training corpus at once, and which image analysis can check against the expected pattern for that commodity as an assistive flag rather than an approval. Frame the whole thing as support for the driver's judgement rather than as a replacement for it, because it is, and because anything else will be rejected by the people it is for.

## Who Feels the Pain
Newer drivers making safety-critical judgements with generic training; experienced drivers whose knowledge disperses when they leave; and carriers carrying violation and cargo-loss exposure they manage by hiring experience.

## Impact If Fixed
Securement knowledge is the specialised segment's most valuable tacit asset and its most fragile, in a workforce that is ageing. Capturing it as commodity-specific guidance contributed by the people who hold it is both the most durable transfer mechanism available and a recognition of expertise that flatbed drivers rarely receive.
