# Carrier Identity & Vetting

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/freight-tech-platforms|Freight Tech Platforms]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in carrier vetting is fighting to establish who is actually going to haul a load before it moves — and whoever detects the fraudulent and re-brokering carriers earliest takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.4B US carrier vetting, fraud prevention and compliance monitoring
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~18% of freight technology revenue and the fastest-growing line
**Digital Adoption:** Medium-High — vetting is digitised and the underlying identity model is not
**Target Buyer:** Compliance and operations leaders at freight brokerages; insurers and shippers with cargo exposure
**Automation Potential:** Very High — the signal is relational and sits in cross-broker data nobody has assembled

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Freight fraud escalated from a nuisance to a structural crisis. Double brokering — a carrier accepting a load and quietly re-brokering it to someone else, leaving the original broker liable and the actual hauler unpaid — has become endemic. Dormant carrier authorities are bought or taken over and used to accept freight that is then stolen. Losses run to hundreds of millions annually and the affected parties include brokers, shippers, insurers and the legitimate carriers whose identities are used. The vetting infrastructure was built for a world in which an active operating authority, a current insurance certificate and a signed carrier packet were sufficient evidence, and all three of those are now routinely available to a fraudulent actor. The contested capability is therefore identity: establishing that the entity accepting a load is the entity that will haul it, before the freight moves.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Highway and Carrier Assure emerged specifically for this problem and have grown fast; RMIS, MyCarrierPackets and the established compliance monitoring providers handle authority, insurance and safety data. Federal data — operating authority, safety ratings, insurance filings — is public and thin, and it is exactly what a fraudulent actor can present. Load boards remain the largest fraud surface and have improved their own controls without solving the underlying problem. The gaps are structural: vetting is performed per carrier in isolation rather than against the network of relationships in which fraud is visible, brokers are structurally reluctant to share their own bad experiences with competitors, and nothing verifies at the moment that matters — when the driver arrives at the pickup, which is where the identity actually has to hold and where almost no verification occurs.
