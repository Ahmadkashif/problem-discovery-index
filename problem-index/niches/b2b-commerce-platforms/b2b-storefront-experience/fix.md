# Merchandising Nobody Asked For

**Niche:** [[niches/b2b-commerce-platforms/b2b-storefront-experience/profile|B2B Storefront Experience]]
**Industry:** [[industries/b2b-commerce-platforms|B2B Commerce Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The storefront's homepage, banners, recommendation carousels and promotional modules were built because the platform provides them, and no business buyer has ever used one.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #worker-facing #revenue-impact #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #quick-win #automation
**Contested on:** Not terminal — the contest differs by whether the buyer already knows what they want, and the decomposition is recorded in the profile.

## The Problem
The homepage carries a hero banner, three featured categories, a new products carousel and a promotional block. The analytics show that ninety-odd percent of sessions begin by going straight to search, order history or a requisition list, and the interaction rate on every merchandising module is negligible. The modules are maintained by somebody, refreshed on a calendar, and occupy the space above the things buyers actually use. They exist because the platform has the capability and because the team's experience is consumer retail, and no one has looked at whether they earn their place.

## Why It's Still Broken
Merchandising modules are the platform's features and using them feels like using the product properly. The digital team frequently comes from consumer retail where these modules genuinely work. Nobody measured their interaction rate because nobody expected the answer to be zero. And removing them looks like doing less rather than like making the surface work.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Measure the modules and reclaim the space. Report interaction rate and attributable revenue per module, which is a straightforward analytics query and will settle the question immediately — this is the fix, and the evidence is usually decisive. Replace the homepage with the buyer's own context: their recent orders, their requisition lists, their pending approvals, their open quotes, their scheduled deliveries — which is what they came for and is currently below the fold or behind a menu. Keep the merchandising that survives the measurement, since some of it works — a promotion on consumables a buyer already purchases is genuinely useful — and the point is to keep what earns its place rather than to remove everything. Move the supplier's promotional intent into the surfaces the buyer does use, such as a relevant alternative on a part page or a quantity break prompt in the cart, which is where it can actually influence a decision. Report the space given to modules against their contribution, which frames the trade honestly. Test removals rather than debating them, since the platform supports it and the effect is measurable in a fortnight. Redirect the effort spent maintaining modules toward catalogue data quality, which the buyers do use and which is always understaffed. And stop importing consumer patterns without testing them, since that is the general failure of which this is one instance.

## Who Feels the Pain
Buyers scrolling past four modules to reach their order history; digital teams maintaining content nobody reads; and suppliers whose promotional effort reaches nobody.

## Impact If Fixed
Interaction rate per module is a straightforward query and the answer is usually decisive. Replacing the homepage with the buyer's own orders, lists, approvals and quotes gives them what they came for, and redirecting the maintenance effort to catalogue data moves it to something they use.
