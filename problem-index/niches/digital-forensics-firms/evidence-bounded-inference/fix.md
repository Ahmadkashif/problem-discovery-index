# Fix: The Reasoning Lives in One Person's Head

**Niche:** Evidence-Bounded Inference
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-forensics-firms|Digital Forensics Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The model of what this engagement can and cannot establish exists only in the lead responder's mind, which is why every incident requires the most senior people first.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #worker-facing #tacit-knowledge-ml #workflow-orchestration #compliance
**Contested on:** Whether, mid-incident, a firm can state what the surviving evidence supports as a calibrated bound rather than as a narrative.

## The Problem

A lead responder arrives at an incident and within two days holds a working model of the whole investigation: what must be answered, what evidence exists, what the gaps permit, where to look next and what the emerging picture is consistent with.

Nothing about that model is written down. It appears in status updates as conclusions, in the eventual report as findings, and in the responder's head as the structure connecting them.

The consequences run through the whole industry. Only senior people can build the model, so every incident needs the most experienced practitioners in the first days, which is exactly when the labour pool is most constrained and is why the working pattern looks the way it does. A handover between shifts loses most of it. If the lead is unavailable, the engagement effectively restarts. Junior responders execute tasks without seeing the structure the tasks belong to, so they learn slowly. And when the conclusion is challenged in litigation two years later, the reasoning is reconstructed from a report and a memory.

The knowledge is real and it is not mystical. It is a structure that could be recorded as it is built, by the person building it, if anything in the workflow asked for it.

## Why It's Still Broken

**There is no time during an incident.** The model is built under continuous pressure, and recording it competes with the work.

**The report is the only artefact.** Everything produced during an engagement is aimed at the report, which contains conclusions rather than the reasoning structure behind them.

**Privilege discourages documentation.** Investigations under privilege create a reflex against writing anything that is not necessary, which suppresses exactly this kind of record.

**Handover is treated as a briefing.** Shift and engagement handovers are conversations, which transfer conclusions and not the model.

**It is how expertise is recognised.** The ability to hold the model is what makes someone a lead, and the field's professional identity is built around it, which creates a quiet resistance to externalising it.

**Nobody has designed the artefact.** There is no accepted format for recording an investigation's inference structure, so even a willing practitioner has nowhere to put it.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Make the question-and-evidence map a day-one deliverable.** The questions this engagement must answer, the evidence sources that would answer each, and what exists. Half an hour on the first day, updated as the picture changes. It is the model's skeleton and recording it costs almost nothing.

**Record inferences as they are made, briefly.** One line per significant conclusion: what was concluded, from what, and what would change it. This is the material that makes the reasoning reviewable now and defensible later.

**Structure handover around the model, not the status.** A handover that transfers the question map and the open inferences transfers the model; one that transfers a status update does not.

**Let junior responders see the structure.** Giving them the map rather than a task list is how the model becomes learnable, and it is the fastest route to relieving the senior constraint.

**Review the reasoning, not just the conclusion.** Peer review that examines the inference structure catches errors that reviewing the conclusion does not, and it builds the shared vocabulary that makes the model transferable.

**Carry it into the report.** The question map, with its answered, bounded and unanswerable categories, belongs in the deliverable — which is the same change that fixes the scope communication problem and costs nothing extra once the map exists.

**Debrief afterwards and keep the structure.** A short structured debrief capturing what evidence mattered, what was missing and what the team would look for differently — de-identified and retained — is the beginning of the corpus the field has never built.

## Who Feels the Pain

The lead responder, holding the entire investigation in their head continuously for weeks, unable to hand any of it over, which is a substantial part of why the work is exhausting.

Junior responders, executing tasks without seeing the structure, learning slowly in a field that desperately needs more practitioners.

The firm, whose delivery capacity is capped by the number of people who can hold the model and which loses it entirely when one of them leaves.

And the client, whose engagement effectively depends on one individual's continuous availability.

## Impact If Fixed

A day-one question-and-evidence map costs half an hour and is the skeleton of the model, which makes it the cheapest possible start on the field's largest structural problem.

Recording inferences as they are made makes the reasoning reviewable during the engagement and defensible years later in litigation, which is protective for the practitioner as well as useful for the firm.

And making the structure visible to junior responders is the only route to relieving the senior-practitioner constraint that shapes the entire industry's working pattern.
