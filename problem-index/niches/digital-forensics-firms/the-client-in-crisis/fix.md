# Fix: The Executives Have Never Done This Before

**Niche:** The Client During the Incident
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-forensics-firms|Digital Forensics Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The incident response plan was written for the security team and rehearsed by the security team, and the decisions belong to a general counsel and a chief executive who have never made them.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #compliance #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #confidence-intervals
**Contested on:** Whether the person making the notification decision receives what they need to make it, or a technical narrative they must interpret.

## The Problem

An organisation has an incident response plan. It covers detection, escalation, containment, evidence preservation and recovery, and it was written by the security team. If it has been exercised, the exercise was a technical scenario run with the technical team.

The decisions that will actually determine how the incident goes belong to other people. Whether to notify and whom. Whether to pay. What to tell customers. When to inform the board. What the public statement says. Whether the incident is material for disclosure. These sit with the general counsel, the chief executive and the board, and none of them has done any of it before.

They have not read the plan. They have not met the breach coach or the forensics firm. They do not know what a notification decision requires or what the deadlines are. They have not thought about what they would say publicly. And they will be making these decisions for the first time, at speed, while the organisation is in crisis and the technical picture is incomplete.

The gap is filled reactively by the breach coach, who is very good at this and is brought in on day one, which means the executives are being coached through their first ever breach decisions by someone they met that morning.

## Why It's Still Broken

**The plan is owned by security.** It was written by the people who own the technical response, for the technical response, and the decision layer was never their scope.

**Exercises are technical because the technical team runs them.** A tabletop exercise organised by security is attended by security, and getting a chief executive into a two-hour cyber exercise requires an argument nobody makes.

**Executives assume counsel will handle it.** The presence of a breach coach in the plan creates a reasonable impression that the decisions are covered, which reduces the felt need for preparation.

**The decisions feel like legal advice.** Notification is understood as a legal question, which makes it counsel's problem rather than something the decision-maker needs to have thought about in advance.

**Preparation competes with everything.** Executive time is the scarcest resource in an organisation, and rehearsing a hypothetical breach loses to whatever is actually happening.

**Nobody owns executive readiness.** Security owns the technical plan, legal owns the retainer, and nobody owns whether the executives are prepared.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Run an exercise for the decision-makers.** Two hours, the general counsel and the executive team, walking through a realistic scenario to the decisions — notify or not, what to say, what the board is told. Not technical. This is the fix and it is the thing organisations most consistently skip.

**Introduce the breach coach and the forensics firm in advance.** A thirty-minute conversation with the people who will be on the bridge call, before the crisis. The relationship then exists when it is needed.

**Write the decision layer into the plan.** Who decides what, with what authority, on what timeline, and who is informed. Most plans specify technical escalation and leave the decision authority to be worked out in the moment.

**Draft the communications in advance.** Holding statements, customer notifications, regulator submissions and internal messages, prepared calmly. They will need editing and starting from a draft under crisis conditions is enormously better than starting from nothing.

**Know the clocks before the incident.** Which notification deadlines apply, from what trigger, in which jurisdictions. A page, prepared once, that counsel would otherwise assemble at three in the morning.

**Decide the ransom position in advance.** Whether the organisation would consider paying, who decides, and what the sanctions screening process is. Making that decision under duress, for the first time, with a countdown running, is the worst possible circumstance.

**Insurers should require it.** The parties funding the response have an interest in the decisions being made well, and an executive exercise is a cheap loss-mitigation requirement.

## Who Feels the Pain

The general counsel, making career-defining decisions for the first time, at speed, coached by someone they met that day.

The chief executive, approving a public statement about an incident whose scope is bounded rather than known.

The organisation, whose response quality depends on how quickly its executives can learn a set of decisions during the crisis itself.

And the security team, who prepared thoroughly for their part and watch the decisions above them being improvised.

## Impact If Fixed

A two-hour executive exercise is the highest-return preparation available in this entire industry and is the thing organisations most reliably skip.

Pre-drafted communications and a known list of clocks remove work from the worst possible moment at essentially no cost, prepared calmly by people with time to think.

And deciding the ransom position in advance means that when the question arrives with a countdown attached, the organisation is executing a decision rather than making one.
