**Contested on:** Whether an attribution claim carries a confidence anyone has calibrated.

## The Problem

The rules governing what an expert may say, and how, are well developed. Admissibility standards require that a method be reliable, that it has been tested, that error rates are known or estimable, and that the expert's conclusion is within what the method supports. Expert reports in litigation must state the basis for each opinion and the facts relied upon. Cross-examination exists specifically to test whether the confidence is warranted.

Most attribution claims never reach that scrutiny. They appear in incident reports delivered under privilege to a client, are relied upon by insurers and counsel, inform public statements and regulatory submissions, and are never tested by anyone whose job is to challenge them.

So the field operates in two modes with very different standards. The same practitioner writing an expert report for litigation will state carefully what their opinion rests on, and writing an incident report will state an attribution as a finding — and the second is relied upon by more parties for more consequential decisions.

## What Already Exists

Admissibility standards: the expert evidence rules in the major jurisdictions, requiring reliability, testing and known error rates.

Expert report requirements: the procedural rules specifying that an expert must state the facts relied upon, the basis for each opinion, and any qualifications or limitations.

Forensic science reform: the standards work that followed the overstatement crisis in physical forensics, including evaluative reporting guidance and the requirement to state evidential strength properly.

Professional codes: duties owed to the court by experts, which override the duty to the instructing party.

Digital forensics practice: laboratory accreditation covering process, and professional certifications covering competence.

## The Customization Gap

**The standards apply to a minority of the work.** Litigation reports meet them; incident reports do not, and the incident report is what most decisions rest on. Applying the same discipline to non-litigation work is the adaptation.

**Error rates are unknown for attribution inferences.** Admissibility standards ask for them. Nobody has established how often an infrastructure-overlap inference is wrong, which means the method could not meet the standard if it were tested.

**Basis statements are not required outside litigation.** An expert report must state what each opinion rests on. An incident report states conclusions, and adding a basis statement is a formatting change with substantial effect.

**The duty-to-the-court principle has no analogue.** An expert in litigation owes a duty that overrides the instructing party's interest. An incident responder writing for a client under privilege has no equivalent obligation, and the pressure toward a client-favourable attribution is real.

**Nobody tests the claims.** Cross-examination is the mechanism that disciplines expert confidence. Incident reports face no equivalent, which is why the confidence in them drifts.

**Evaluative reporting is not used.** Physical forensics moved to expressing evidential strength under competing propositions. Digital attribution states conclusions.

## Target Customer

Firms whose practitioners do both litigation and incident work, who already know the standard and could apply it in both modes — and who would find the inconsistency uncomfortable once named.

Cyber insurers, who could require basis statements and defined confidence as a panel condition, and who have the strongest interest since exclusion decisions rest on these claims.

Professional bodies and accreditation schemes, as the route by which the standards would apply to the work generally rather than only where litigation is anticipated.

## Impact If Solved

A discipline with well-developed standards for expert assertion applies them to a minority of its output and not to the part that drives most decisions.

Requiring a basis statement for every attribution is a formatting change borrowed directly from expert report requirements, and it would make the claim assessable by the insurers and counsel who rely on it.

And establishing error rates for common attribution inferences is the study the admissibility standards would demand if these claims were ever tested — and it is the one that would tell the field how much confidence its methods actually support.
