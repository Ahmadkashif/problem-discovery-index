# Attribution & Expert Testimony

**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Whether an attribution claim carries a confidence anyone has calibrated, given that it affects insurance coverage, sanctions exposure and public statements.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$500M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~10%

**Automation Potential:** Low — structure helps, the judgement does not automate

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

An incident report attributes the intrusion to a named actor, or to a category — a ransomware affiliate, a state-associated group, a criminal enterprise. That claim then does work far beyond the technical.

Insurance coverage may turn on it, since war and state-actor exclusions exist and have been litigated. Sanctions exposure turns on it, because paying a ransom to a designated entity is unlawful. The public statement turns on it. Litigation strategy turns on it. And regulatory characterisation of the incident's seriousness is influenced by it.

The claim is made with confidence language nobody has calibrated. High confidence means what the firm's internal convention says it means, which varies between firms and is not defined in terms anyone downstream can use. The reasoning is narrative. And the consequences are decided by lawyers, insurers and regulators reading a sentence whose evidential weight they cannot assess.

This is a distinct market because the consumer is legal and financial rather than technical, the standard of proof is different, and the cost of overstatement is borne by parties who had no part in the analysis.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Attribution reasoning drawing on infrastructure, tooling, tradecraft and targeting. Threat intelligence vendor group taxonomies, which differ between vendors and overlap inconsistently. Internal confidence conventions. Expert witness practice with its own admissibility standards where litigation is involved. Sanctions screening against designated entity lists.

The gaps are consequential. Confidence terms have no defined meaning, so a high-confidence attribution from one firm and another are not comparable. The reasoning is narrative, so the specific inferences carrying the weight are not identifiable. Group naming is vendor-specific and the relationship between a technical cluster and a named group is a separate and weaker claim that is routinely collapsed. Nothing tracks how often attributions later proved wrong. And the downstream consumers — insurers applying exclusions, counsel assessing sanctions risk — receive a claim without the basis for weighing it.
