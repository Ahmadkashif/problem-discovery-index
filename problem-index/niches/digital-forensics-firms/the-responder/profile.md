# The Responder

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/digital-forensics-firms|Digital Forensics Firms]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Whether a working pattern the field has called unsustainable for a decade is a fixed property of the work or a consequence of how engagements are staffed and priced.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$450M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~9%

**Target Buyer:** Firm operations, practice leadership, insurers setting panel terms
**Automation Potential:** Moderate — load reduction automates, the arrival pattern does not

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

Incidents arrive without notice, run continuously for weeks, and demand the most senior practitioners in the first days. The labour pool is chronically scarce. Utilisation is the business model. And the field has openly described its working pattern as unsustainable for a decade without changing it.

The specifics are consistent across firms. A responder is on call, mobilises within hours, and then works continuously — frequently sixteen-hour days for the first week — because the statutory clock runs, the attacker may still be inside, and the client is in crisis. The engagement ends and the next begins. Reports are written in the evenings during the following incident. And the work itself involves sustained exposure to organisations at their worst, with the emotional weight that carries.

The consequence is attrition from a profession that takes years to train into, which tightens the labour pool, which increases the load on those remaining. The field has recognised the loop and has not broken it, because breaking it requires either more people or less work per engagement, and the first is scarce and the second is nobody's project.

## Current Tools & Gaps

On-call rotations of varying quality. Utilisation targets. Retainer and panel obligations that constrain how work can be declined. Some firms have surge arrangements internally. Wellbeing provision exists and is typically an assistance programme.

The gaps are structural rather than provisional. Nothing measures the actual load — hours worked, consecutive days, time to recovery — so the pattern is discussed anecdotally and managed by nobody. Fatigue limits do not exist, in contrast to every comparable emergency discipline. The senior-practitioner dependency in the first days is unaddressed, so surge capacity cannot be created from mid-level staff. Reporting time is unscheduled, so it lands in the evenings. And no firm can decline work without breaching panel obligations, which removes the main lever a firm would otherwise have.
