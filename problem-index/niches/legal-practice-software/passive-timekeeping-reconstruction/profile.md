# Passive Timekeeping & Billable Reconstruction

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/legal-practice-software|Legal Practice Software]]
**Category:** Highly Automatable
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in legal timekeeping is fighting to produce a reconstructed timesheet a lawyer accepts with minimal editing — and whoever gets the accepted-unedited share highest takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$400M attributable to timekeeping capability within legal practice software, plus the standalone passive-capture vendors
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~11% of legal practice software revenue and effectively 100% of its marketing message
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — timers exist everywhere and are used inconsistently; passive capture is deployed at a small fraction of firms
**Target Buyer:** Product leads at every practice management vendor in the category; managing partners buying on the promise of captured time
**Automation Potential:** Very High — the activity signal is complete and continuous, and the labels come free from the lawyer's own edits

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
This is the promise the entire category is sold on. Unbilled time at small firms is estimated at a fifth to a third of professional hours, and every practice management vendor competes on recovering it. The product delivered is a timer the lawyer forgets to start, plus a reconstruction feature that produces a list of documents opened and emails sent — raw activity presented as though it were a timesheet. The lawyer looks at it, finds it more work to interpret than to remember, and types entries from memory on Friday. What makes the niche terminal under the filter is that the success criterion is sharp and measurable: the fraction of reconstructed entries a lawyer accepts without editing. Every competitor can be ranked on it and none of them publishes it.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Smokeball's passive capture is the most complete implementation in the small-firm market; Clio, MyCase and PracticePanther offer timers and activity logs; standalone vendors (Ping, Chrometa, Timekeep-style tools) have pursued automatic capture for years with mixed commercial results. The technical capability to observe activity is not the gap — operating systems and applications emit plenty. The gap is everywhere after capture. Activity has to be segmented into work sessions, attributed to a matter, classified into a task code, narrated in language that survives a client's or a carrier's review, and estimated in duration excluding the twenty minutes the lawyer spent reading news. Products do the first and last of these badly and the middle three barely at all, which is why the output is a log rather than a draft.
