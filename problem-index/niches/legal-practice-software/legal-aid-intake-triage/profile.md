# Legal Aid & Access-to-Justice Intake

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/legal-practice-software|Legal Practice Software]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor serving legal aid is fighting to triage far more requests than the organisation can ever serve, defensibly and consistently, and to send the rest somewhere real — and whoever makes that triage defensible takes the contract.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$150M US software spend across legal aid organisations, law school clinics, court self-help centres and pro bono programmes
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~4% of legal practice software revenue, funded by grants rather than by fees
**Digital Adoption:** Low — the sector is chronically under-resourced and buys software last
**Target Buyer:** Executive directors, intake managers and technology directors at legal aid organisations; state access-to-justice commissions
**Automation Potential:** High — intake screening, eligibility determination and referral are rule-governed and are performed at volumes far exceeding staff capacity

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Legal aid operates under a constraint no commercial practice faces: demand permanently and vastly exceeds capacity, and the organisation's central act is deciding who not to help. Studies of civil legal need consistently find that a large majority of low-income households' legal problems receive no or inadequate assistance, and every legal aid organisation turns away a substantial share of eligible applicants for lack of capacity. The triage decision is therefore made thousands of times a year by intake staff applying priority guidelines under time pressure, and it determines outcomes in eviction, custody, benefits and domestic violence matters. It is the highest-stakes routing decision in the legal system and it is supported by less technology than a restaurant booking.

## Current Tools & Gaps
LegalServer dominates case management in the sector, with Kemp's and a few others serving parts of it; the Legal Services Corporation's reporting requirements shape what gets recorded. Statewide legal help websites and guided interview tooling — much of it built on the Docassemble open-source stack by the sector's own technologists, who are genuinely good — provide self-help and form completion. The gaps are structural rather than technical. Triage criteria are written as priority guidelines and applied inconsistently between intake workers, with no measurement of that inconsistency. Referrals out are made to organisations whose actual capacity is unknown, so a referral is frequently a polite ending rather than a handoff. And outcomes for the people turned away are recorded nowhere, which means the sector cannot evidence the consequence of the gap it exists to close.
