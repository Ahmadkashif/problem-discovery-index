# Scores That Mean the Same Thing

**Niche:** [[niches/localization-services/the-reviewer/profile|The Reviewer]]
**Industry:** [[industries/localization-services|Localization Services]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** A number that decides who gets work is produced by a process nobody has calibrated.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #descriptive-statistics #compliance #data-integration #tacit-knowledge-ml
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to make the reviewer's judgement count for something, when their scores are the industry's quality currency and nothing validates them — and whoever grounds those scores takes the account.

## The Problem
Review scores are the industry's quality currency. They determine whether a linguist keeps getting work, whether a vendor keeps an account, and what a client is told about quality. They are produced by individuals applying a typology to a sample, with no calibration between reviewers, no separation of error from preference, and no validation against anything external. A linguist can lose an account on a score that another reviewer would not have given.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
Calibration requires reviewers to score the same material and compare, which costs time nobody has allocated. The typology's categories are interpretable and interpretation varies. No external validation exists, which is the market outcome problem. And the scores are useful enough administratively that their reliability is not questioned.

## What to Build
Calibrate the reviewers and separate preference from error. Run periodic calibration exercises where reviewers score the same material and the spread is measured, which is the core and is the minimum any scoring regime requires. Report inter-reviewer agreement, since a score with unknown reliability should not carry the consequences these do. Separate error from preference explicitly in the form, which is the distinction reviewers know matters and the typology blurs. Require evidence for each recorded error rather than a category selection, which raises the standard and reduces the preference problem. Give reviewers feedback on their own severity relative to peers, which most would welcome and none receive. Weight scores by reviewer reliability where it is known. Allow the linguist to contest a score with a defined route, since the consequences are real and the process currently has no appeal. Validate the scores against market outcomes where those become available, which is what would ground the whole apparatus. Record the score's uncertainty alongside it, especially on small samples. And be explicit with everyone that a score is an assessment by one person rather than a measurement.

## Target Customer
Language service providers, language leadership and reviewers, translator associations, and quality management tooling vendors.

## Impact If Built
A score with unknown reliability decides who keeps working, and nothing calibrates it. Inter-reviewer agreement measurement plus a preference-versus-error distinction is the minimum any scoring regime with these consequences requires.
