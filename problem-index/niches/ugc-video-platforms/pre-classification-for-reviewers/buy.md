## The Problem
Safety-critical engineering treats the allocation of function between automation and people as a design decision with stated reasoning: what the machine is better at, what the human is better at, what the human needs to remain capable, and what workload the arrangement produces. Aviation, process control and medicine all do this explicitly. Content moderation's boundary is wherever a classifier's precision-recall tuning happened to put it, and its workload consequences were never part of the decision.

## What Already Exists
Function allocation methodologies; human factors workload analysis; automation confidence and handover design; operator capability preservation; and workload measurement as a design criterion.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to a boundary whose human side carries psychological harm. It requires: (1) workload measured in exposure to traumatic material rather than in cognitive load, which is a different and more serious criterion — this is the substantive adaptation and no function allocation framework contemplates it; (2) a human side staffed through vendors, so the workload consequence sits outside the designing organisation; (3) volumes where a percentage point of automation coverage is thousands of hours of exposure; (4) an adversary who will probe the automated boundary; and (5) an accuracy requirement driven by policy and regulation that constrains how much can be automated.

## Target Customer
Trust and safety engineering leadership, human factors specialists, vendor operators, regulators, and moderation tooling vendors.

## Impact If Solved
Safety engineering allocates function deliberately with workload as a criterion. Making that criterion exposure to traumatic material, at volumes where one percentage point is thousands of hours, is the adaptation this setting requires.
