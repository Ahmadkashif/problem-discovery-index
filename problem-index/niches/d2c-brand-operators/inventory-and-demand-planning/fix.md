# The Marketing Plan Nobody Told Planning About

**Niche:** [[niches/d2c-brand-operators/inventory-and-demand-planning/profile|Inventory & Demand Planning]]
**Industry:** [[industries/d2c-brand-operators|D2C Brand Operators]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The marketing team decides which products to promote and how hard, the planning team forecasts demand from history, and the two plans are made independently in the same company.
**Tags:** #time-series-forecasting #data-integration #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #quick-win #causal-inference
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to tell a brand how much to buy six months ahead without a stockout or a markdown — and whoever does that takes the account, because that decision commits more cash than any other the brand makes and is currently made in a spreadsheet.

## The Problem
Marketing plans a major push behind three products for the spring. Planning, working from last year's sales, buys those three at last year's volumes and the others at a small increase. The push works, the three sell out in a month, and marketing spends the rest of the season driving traffic to products that are unavailable while the products they did buy sit. Both teams did their jobs correctly. Nobody connected the plan that creates demand to the plan that supplies it, in a business where the same company controls both — which is an advantage over conventional retail that the category systematically fails to use.

## Why It's Still Broken
Marketing and merchandising sit in different functions with different calendars and different systems, and the handover is an email if it happens at all. Marketing plans change after the buy is committed, which is used as a reason not to share the plan rather than a reason to plan jointly. Planning has no way to express demand as a function of spend. And the failure is attributed to demand being unpredictable rather than to a plan that was known and unshared.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Plan the demand and the supply together. Make the marketing calendar and planned spend by product a required input to the forecast, which is a process change costing one recurring meeting and which resolves the most predictable forecast error the brand makes. Estimate the demand response to spend per product from history, so a planned push translates into a quantity rather than into a hope. Run the buy against scenarios, since the marketing plan will change and a buy that is robust across plausible plans is better than one optimised for a plan that will not survive. Feed inventory position back into marketing execution continuously, so spend moves away from products about to stock out and toward those with cover — which is a daily operational link most brands do not have and which recovers a large part of the loss even when the buy was wrong. Flag products where the planned push exceeds the bought quantity before the season, which is a single comparison and catches the worst cases outright. Share a common plan document with both quantities and spend, since the two teams currently hold different numbers. Review jointly in season, because the season is a series of decisions and treating it as one is what makes the initial error permanent. And attribute the cost of stockouts and markdowns to the joint plan rather than to one function, since blaming either separately is what keeps them separate.

## Who Feels the Pain
Merchandisers blamed for a buy that a marketing plan they never saw invalidated; marketers driving traffic to sold-out products; and the brand, absorbing both a stockout and a markdown in the same season.

## Impact If Fixed
The company controls both the demand and the supply plan and makes them independently, which is an advantage over retail it fails to use. Feeding live inventory position back into spend allocation recovers much of the loss even when the original buy was wrong.
