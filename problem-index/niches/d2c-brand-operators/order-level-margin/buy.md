# Activity-Based Costing and Profitability Analytics

**Niche:** [[niches/d2c-brand-operators/order-level-margin/profile|Order-Level Margin]]
**Industry:** [[industries/d2c-brand-operators|D2C Brand Operators]]
**Type:** Buy & Customize (Vertical Adaptation)
**One-liner:** Retail and manufacturing built customer and product profitability analysis decades ago, and direct-to-consumer brands run on gross margin and a monthly management account.
**Tags:** #revenue-impact #data-integration #descriptive-statistics #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #hypothesis-testing #convex-optimization #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to tell a brand what each order actually made after everything — and whoever does that takes the account, because every decision in the business is currently made on revenue while the margin varies from healthy to negative order by order.

## The Problem
Attributing costs to the products, customers and transactions that cause them is what activity-based costing does, and customer profitability analysis has been standard in retail and banking for decades — including the finding, which surprises every organisation that runs it for the first time, that a substantial share of customers are unprofitable. The methods, the software category and the practice all exist. These brands, whose cost structure is unusually attributable because almost everything is digital and per-order, use none of it.

## What Already Exists
Activity-based costing with cost driver identification; customer and product profitability analysis; contribution margin reporting by dimension; cost-to-serve analysis from retail and distribution; and profitability analytics modules in enterprise finance systems.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to a small business with excellent per-transaction data and no finance function. It requires: (1) cost drivers that are directly observable rather than allocated, since almost every cost here is per-order and attributable — which makes this far easier than the manufacturing setting the methods came from and is why the absence is remarkable; (2) acquisition cost as a cost driver, which is the largest attributable cost in the business and which activity-based costing traditions treat as a period overhead; (3) return cost modelled probabilistically at the time of order, since the return has not happened yet and a decision made at order time needs an expected value; (4) operation without a finance team, which means the model must be built into the commerce stack rather than into an enterprise finance system; and (5) a price point that suits a brand doing single-digit millions, which is why the enterprise modules are irrelevant here.

## Target Customer
Brand finance and growth functions, commerce analytics vendors, and the management accounting profession for whom this is an unusually tractable and unserved application.

## Impact If Solved
Almost every cost here is per-order and directly attributable, which makes this easier than the setting the methods came from and the absence more striking. Treating acquisition cost as a driver rather than an overhead is the adaptation, since it is the largest attributable cost in the business.
