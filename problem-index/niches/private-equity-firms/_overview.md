# Niche Analysis — Private Equity Firms

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/private-equity-firms|Private Equity Firms]]

## Niche Selection

A lower-middle or middle-market sponsor is a research organisation that reviews hundreds of companies a year, does deep work on a few dozen, buys a handful, and then reports on them every month for five years. Its research workflow — sourcing, screening, diligence, memo, monitoring, LP reporting — is repeatable in shape, runs on clocks set by sellers, lenders, auditors and LPs, and is graded by the market with unusual completeness: every company it passed on eventually trades in public view. The eight niches below split the category by what competitors actually fight over.

## Why These Niches

Sourcing and diligence take the largest share because they are where a sponsor's money and senior time go before a deal exists: sourcing is a forecasting contest over which founder-owned companies will become sellable, and diligence is the most expensive and time-critical research effort in the cycle. The two low-digitized niches are the post-close reporting machine — monthly packages in a dozen formats mapped by hand, and LP questionnaires answered from Word files — where the platforms exist and the data still gets rekeyed. The two underserved audiences are the associate who does the firm's transcription at midnight and the portfolio CFO who produces the same numbers three ways for the owner, the lender and the board. The two automatable niches are the firm's own unrecorded judgement — ten years of passes nobody graded — and the value creation story nobody measured.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. Transaction Diligence is not: the label names a phase of the deal rather than a contest, and writing the contested statement produces two sentences with different winners. Financial diligence asks whether the earnings are real — which add-backs survive, whether working capital is normal — and is fought on the target's general ledger by accounting firms' transaction advisory practices. Commercial diligence asks whether the revenue will persist — market growth, share, switching, pricing — and is fought on surveys, interviews and market data by strategy consultancies and CDD boutiques. Different evidence, different providers, different failure modes (a wrong number versus a wrong story about the future). It therefore decomposes into **Quality of Earnings Review** and **Commercial Diligence**. Legal, insurance, IT and environmental diligence were considered as further sub-niches and not split out: in the mid-market they are smaller, more commoditised workstreams whose contest is price and turnaround rather than a distinct analytical question.

Deal Sourcing was tested for the same split — proprietary founder outreach versus coverage of banker-run processes — and kept whole: both halves are fighting for the same thing, being in the room early enough to avoid paying the auction price, and the winning capability (knowing which companies are becoming sellable, and having the relationship) is shared.

Three candidates were rejected. **Fund administration and accounting** is a real workload but is outsourced to administrators and is accounting rather than a contest among sponsors; it appears in Pass 2 as a supplier pocket. **Buy-and-build add-on acquisition** is a strategy rather than a niche here, and the rollup platforms' acquisition analytics are already mapped from the operator side in the vault (for example [[niches/hvac-contractors/home-services-rollup-analytics/profile|Home Services Rollup Analytics]]). **Exit preparation and sell-side processes** belong to the M&A advisers who run them, not to the sponsor's own contest.

## Why These Pockets

Two qualifiers, both in the specialist advisory position, and both sell research to sponsors on a clock the sponsor does not control. Portfolio valuation advisory is the cleaner of the two: every quarter, independent valuers mark thousands of private companies at fair value for sponsors and private credit lenders, the mark is the invoice, the work is the same shape every quarter, and the deadline is set by fund reporting and audit. The practice holds a quarter-by-quarter panel of private-company financials and concluded values across hundreds of sponsors — Lincoln International alone reports marking more than 7,000 companies a quarter — and uses it mainly to publish an index. The two defects are the familiar ones: the routine majority of marks are rebuilt by hand rather than pre-computed with honest intervals, and marks are almost never graded against the exit price that eventually arrives.

Commercial due diligence consultancies are the second: the report is the invoice, studies are repeatable in shape and staffed linearly, and the exclusivity window is the hardest clock in professional services. Their under-used asset is market-level evidence accumulated across many studies of the same markets for different sponsors — survey distributions, market models, interview themes — that currently carries over only in partners' heads and old decks. The confidentiality boundary (target findings are walled; market evidence is the firm's working knowledge) is real and shapes the product, but does not stall it.

The highest-scoring pocket that does not qualify is transaction advisory and quality-of-earnings practices, at 54 with a live kill switch: they hold the best record in existence of how mid-market add-backs behave, and the data is each target's under NDA. The pattern across the rest of the sweep is the same as Pass 1 found inside the sponsors: almost every party around a private equity deal makes a forecast that the market later grades — the LP consultant's manager rating, the R&W underwriter's exclusions, the lender's credit rating, the secondary buyer's bid, the CDD provider's growth rate, the valuer's mark — and almost none of them keeps a scored record of their own calls. Where the grading data is the party's own, that is a product; where it belongs to someone else, it is a kill switch.
