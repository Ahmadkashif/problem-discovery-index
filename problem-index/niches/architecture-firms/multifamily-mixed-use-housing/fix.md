# The Unit Count That Shrinks After Feasibility

**Niche:** [[niches/architecture-firms/multifamily-mixed-use-housing/profile|Multifamily & Mixed-Use Housing]]
**Industry:** [[industries/architecture-firms|Architecture Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The feasibility study promised a unit count; schematic design loses a few to stairs, shafts and code, and the developer's pro forma loses its margin.
**Tags:** #linear-regression #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to fit the most rentable units onto a site under zoning and code at a construction cost per unit that makes the developer's pro forma pencil, and whoever proves yield and cost fastest at feasibility takes the account.

## The Problem
Feasibility massing assumes efficient floor plates. When the real design adds exit stairs at code-required separation, trash and mechanical shafts, a second elevator, structural transfer zones and corridor widths, rentable area and units fall. A few lost units can turn a viable deal marginal.

## Why It's Still Broken
Feasibility is done fast and cheap to win the work, and nobody systematically compares the feasibility unit count with the permitted unit count on completed projects, so the optimism is never calibrated.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Track feasibility versus permitted units and rentable area on every project, fit a simple loss factor by building type and site shape, and apply it to every new feasibility study as a stated range.

## Who Feels the Pain
Developers underwriting land on optimistic counts; architects redesigning to recover units; lenders relying on the pro forma.

## Impact If Fixed
Feasibility numbers developers can trust, and fewer schematic designs spent recovering lost units.
