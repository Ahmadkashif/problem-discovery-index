# Niche Analysis — Architecture Firms

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/architecture-firms|Architecture Firms]]

## Niche Selection

An architecture firm earns a fixed fee for a set of documents that a contractor prices, a building department approves and a court may later read, and it spends that fee across design, documentation, permitting and construction administration. Institutional owners — schools, hospitals, universities, governments — pay more than half of all billings; three firms in four have fewer than ten people. The eight niches below split the industry by what competitors, vendors and in-house teams actually fight over. Market sizes are estimates of the fee value of each segment or function — its share of the ~$104B in 2023 billings — except the licensure niche, which counts preparation and support spend; software spend in every niche is a small fraction of these figures.

## Why These Niches

Institutional work earns the largest share because public and nonprofit owners fund the buildings that need the most design per dollar of construction — 53–55% of billings against ~20% or less of construction spending in 2023 (estimate). Multifamily housing is second because it is the largest private segment where the architect's output is directly tied to the client's return. The two low-digitized niches are where paper, PDFs and memory still run the work: construction administration, where submittals and RFIs are compared by reading, and historic rehabilitation, where existing conditions are measured by hand and approvals hang on reviewers' reading of the Secretary of the Interior's Standards. The two underserved audiences are the sole practitioner who has no second reviewer and the unlicensed architectural staff whose path to licensure takes over a decade. The two automatable niches are the most mechanical parts of the job: assembling code analysis and answering correction letters, and turning a model into an internally consistent sheet set.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. Institutional Architecture is not: writing its contested sentence produced two sentences with different winners. Health systems select architects on clinical planning and on a record of passing FGI Guidelines-based state review — in California, review by the Department of Health Care Access and Information — and the winner is whoever holds that clinical and regulatory knowledge. School districts and civic owners select through qualifications-based processes on bond-funded programmes with a voter-approved budget and, in California, Division of the State Architect review, and the winner is whoever can evidence on-budget delivery. Different reviewers, clocks and evidence, so the niche decomposes into **Healthcare Facility Design** and **Public K-12 & Civic Bond Programmes**. Higher education sits between them and was left inside the parent rather than forced into a third sub-niche.

Two terminal calls were close. **Construction Administration Services** could split into submittal review and RFI response, but both run on the same contractual response clock and the same liability boundary, so one contest governs. **Multifamily & Mixed-Use Housing** could split into market-rate and affordable housing, where low-income housing tax credit scoring adds its own criteria; the contest — yield that pencils under zoning, code and funding constraints — binds both, so it stays whole.

Three candidates were considered and rejected. **Commercial office and workplace interiors** was rejected because its contest — tenant fit-out speed and furniture specification — belongs to the interior design industry, and the architect's remaining share collapses into a generic "better service" sentence. **Single-family custom residential** was rejected because the honest contested sentence is "design taste and client chemistry", which fails the filter, and it is 8% of billings. **Design-build partnerships** were rejected because the contest — who controls price and schedule — is won by the contractor, and belongs to [[industries/general-contractors|General Contractors]].

## Pass 2 — Insight-Layer Discovery

Second, orthogonal sweep. Pass 1 searched across the operator layer, where three firms in four have fewer than ten people and even the largest practices sell design rather than research. This pass sweeps seven positions in the value chain — aggregator/rollup, payer & intermediary, data & benchmark vendors, association research arms, specialist advisory & valuation, regulatory bodies, and suppliers selling into the industry — looking for a dedicated insight function of 10+ people whose analytical output is the billable deliverable. Full method in `_direction.md`.

Building code publishers, design professional liability insurers, design firm financial benchmarking, BIM content libraries and forensic engineering serve this industry too and were logged under `engineering-consultants`; construction fund control, building product specification teams and construction cost escalation forecasting were logged under `general-contractors`. ## Why These Pockets

The architecture firm itself is not a qualifier, and neither are the largest firms' research institutes. The operators sell design, and the insight they produce — post-occupancy studies, workplace surveys, a principal's sense of which details fail — is either marketing or locked inside client engagements. The large-firm research groups (pocket 19) show it plainly: real researchers in the right firms, a corpus collected in clients' buildings, and a kill switch on reuse. No pocket clears the bar. The closest sit beside the architect, where a specialist is paid for an interpretation the architect cannot make alone.

Building and fire code consultants come closest, at 49. The code report or alternative-means request is the invoice; the work recurs across thousands of difficult buildings; and the proprietary asset is unusual — not the reports themselves, which go to the client, but the record of which approaches each authority accepted, conditioned or rejected, under which code edition. That record is the knowledge senior consultants trade on, and it sits unindexed in project email. What holds the pocket below 50 is the clock: it is the owner's permit schedule, not a deadline any third party sets on the consultant, which puts it level with the accessibility and cost consultants rather than with court- or regulator-driven pockets. It is still the industry-level version of the Pass 1 tacit problem: the architect's principal knows which details fail, the code consultant's principal knows which arguments each fire marshal accepts, and neither has written it down.

Design-stage cost consultants tie at 49: the cost plan is the invoice, the clock is the owner's stage gate, and their priced-project database is real — but general contractors' preconstruction teams and published cost data hold overlapping evidence, which holds the moat at 4. Green building certification bodies (47) own an unmatched corpus of certified-project documentation and are held back by a mostly voluntary clock and a handful of buyers; third-party plan reviewers (47) have the hardest clocks and most repeatable work in the sweep, with a review record split between themselves and the jurisdictions they serve. Building enclosure consultants (44) hold the best outside record of which architectural details leak, which makes them the natural data partner for the high-impact problem even though they fall short as a buyer.
