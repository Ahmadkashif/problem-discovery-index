# Architectural & Interiors Photography

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/commercial-photographers|Commercial Photographers]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to license one shoot to every party on a project (architect, contractor, interior designer, product makers) on terms each can see and none can stretch, and whoever makes the cost-share easy to join and hard to leak takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$350M in US architectural, interiors and hospitality photography fees (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~8% of commercial photography revenue (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Low — capture is digital, but cost-sharing, licences and third-party permissions run on email and PDF
**Target Buyer:** Architectural photographer; marketing director at the commissioning architecture or design firm
**Automation Potential:** Medium — licence administration automates well, the shoot does not

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Architectural photographers shoot finished buildings and interiors for the firms that designed, built, furnished or supplied them. [[industries/architecture-firms|Architecture firms]] and [[industries/interior-design-firms|interior design firms]] use the images for awards, proposals, publications and websites. Contractors, engineers, lighting and furniture makers want the same images for their own marketing. The common arrangement is a cost-share: one photographer shoots, several parties each buy a licence, often at a markup per extra party (Esto reportedly adds ~25%), for savings of up to 60% each by one photographer's account. Ownership and use of project photography is a known source of disputes. The shoot itself is craft: the right hour for the façade, verticals corrected, interiors lit to balance window and lamp. Real estate listing photography, a different high-volume business, is analysed under [[niches/video-production-smb/real-estate-media-producers/profile|Real Estate Media Producers]].

## Current Tools & Gaps
Client galleries (PhotoShelter, Pixieset), Dropbox delivery, licences as PDFs, cost-share invitations by email from the architect's marketing team. The gaps: no shared view of who has licensed what; images forwarded from a paying party to a non-paying one; and award and publication requests handled one by one.
