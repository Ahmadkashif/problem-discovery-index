## Why These Niches

Tutoring centers fragment along student population (college-bound test preppers vs. struggling learners vs. special needs vs. homeschool), business model (franchise vs. independent, affluent suburbs vs. Title I provider), and operational function (instruction vs. assessment vs. enrollment). These 8 niches cover the two largest revenue segments (test prep and math specialty franchises), the two most digitally neglected service types (special needs tutoring with IEP-aligned documentation requirements, and homeschool enrichment centers operating outside standard ed-tech ecosystems), the two most underserved populations (low-income students in high-dosage tutoring programs funded by ESSER/Title I, and ESL families needing language-integrated academic support), and the two highest-ROI automation targets (diagnostic assessment — the core tacit knowledge bottleneck — and scheduling/enrollment operations). Excluded: online-only tutoring platforms (different delivery model), corporate/adult tutoring (different buyer), and private tutor marketplaces (marketplace business, not centers).

## Why These Pockets

No qualifiers, and the reason has a name this sweep has not previously had to use much: student privacy law is education's HIPAA. Seven of twelve pockets carry it, and they include every position where the data would answer the industry's own question.

That question is simple and unanswered: does tutoring work, and which tutoring, for which student. The evidence exists in five places and is used in none of them.

Online tutoring marketplaces hold millions of recorded one-to-one sessions with transcripts, tutor attributes and continuation outcomes — the largest corpus of individual instruction that has ever existed — and use it for matching and safety monitoring. What distinguishes an effective tutor from an ineffective one is directly answerable there and is not asked. Learning platforms hold billions of item responses with response times and hint usage across long trajectories, and report mastery percentages against their own internal scale rather than against any external outcome. Franchise systems hold assessment results joined to hours of instruction across hundreds of centres and report enrolment. Test preparation publishers predict official scores from practice performance for millions of students, receive real scores back, and never publish how accurate the predictions were.

And the one place where the question could be settled at scale — statewide longitudinal data systems linking every student's assessment history to programme participation — sits behind both government procurement and the strongest access wall in education.

The three pockets above 50 are all indexed elsewhere: the screening bureau that clears every adult who will be alone with a child, and the two assessment organisations — one that places the student, one that this industry largely exists to prepare them for.

One structural note on the field's evidence base. Curriculum publishers commission the efficacy studies that adoption requires, and the studies are designed by the party selling the programme. The independent evidence review layer that is supposed to correct this is funded entirely by a federal programme, which makes it a government-dependent arbiter of a commercial market.
