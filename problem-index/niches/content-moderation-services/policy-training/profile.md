# Policy Training & Consistency

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/content-moderation-services|Content Moderation Services]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Whether consistency across thousands of reviewers comes from a policy document they are asked to internalise, or from the adjudicated examples that show what the document means.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$1.5B
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~12%
**Digital Adoption:** Very low — a long document, a slide deck and a weekly update email
**Target Buyer:** Policy trainers, vendor quality leadership, platform policy teams
**Automation Potential:** High — the adjudicated corpus exists and is unused

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

New reviewers are trained on a policy document — often hundreds of pages, covering dozens of content categories with nested definitions and exceptions — and consistency across tens of thousands of people in dozens of languages is expected to emerge from that. The document changes weekly, sometimes more often, and the change arrives as an email, a slide or a team huddle.

The premise is wrong in a way the industry half-knows. Nobody applies a long policy from memory. What experienced reviewers actually hold is a library of adjudicated cases — this kind of thing, decided this way, for this reason — built up over months of doing the work and asking colleagues. That library is where consistency actually comes from, it is entirely tacit, it is different for every reviewer, and it walks out of the building at a turnover rate this industry measures in months.

This is a distinct market because the deliverable is different from everything else in the industry. It is not routing, measurement or protection; it is the transfer of a working understanding to a large, fast-turning, multilingual workforce, against a specification that moves faster than any training cycle.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Training runs on classroom or virtual induction of a few weeks, a policy document, category-specific modules, practice queues with feedback, and a certification test. Ongoing updates arrive by email, huddle or a change log. Learning management systems track completion. Some operations maintain an internal wiki of examples, usually informal and always stale.

The gaps are large and specific. The adjudicated corpus — millions of decisions with outcomes, the vendor's own richest asset — is never turned into training material. Policy changes are distributed as text without the examples that would make the change concrete, so thousands of people reinterpret a sentence independently. There is no retrieval at the moment of decision, so a reviewer facing an unusual case cannot ask what was decided last time. Nothing measures whether a policy update actually changed behaviour, which means nobody knows if the weekly change landed. And the tacit case library that carries real consistency is never externalised, so every departure destroys some of it and every new hire rebuilds it from scratch.
