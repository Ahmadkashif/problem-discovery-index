# Niche Analysis — Legal Support Services

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/legal-support-services|Legal Support Services]]

## Niche Selection

Legal support services do legal work without practising law: serving process, filing papers, drafting under an attorney's supervision, preparing forms for people without lawyers, and searching patent literature. Census counts ~$5.99B of employer receipts at 4,860 firms in 2022, with sole proprietors adding more, and most of the work runs to a clock somebody else set — a service window, a filing deadline, an IPR bar. The eight niches below split the industry by what competitors actually fight over. Market sizes are estimates of buyer spend on each service — operator receipts and, for niche 8, the service-of-process share of registered-agent fees, which may sit partly outside this NAICS code — not of total industry revenue.

## Why These Niches

Process serving takes the largest share because it is the industry's commonest business by establishment count and the one step of civil procedure that needs a person at a door; patent search is second because it is the industry's purest research product and its highest-value work per job. The two low-digitized niches still run on paper and memory: requests to foreign Central Authorities that come back by post months later, and freelance paralegals taking two-line instructions by email from attorneys whose preferences live in their heads. The two underserved audiences are people the tools ignore because they buy once and cannot judge quality — the self-represented party whose preparer may fill forms but not advise, and the first-time inventor deciding whether a patent is worth paying for. The two automatable niches are the most mechanical, highest-volume steps in the chain, both governed by unforgiving deadlines: getting a filing past the clerk, and reading every served document correctly at the receiving end.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. Process Serving is not: its contested sentence produced two sentences with different winners. Volume filers — debt buyers' law firms and landlords' attorneys — buy defensible proof at a per-serve price, and the contest is making every affidavit provably true at scale, because a default built on false service is a class action or a regulator's audit; national networks with device trails win it. Litigators chasing an evasive defendant buy a found person inside the service window, and the contest is converting dead-address and evasive files into valid service; local specialists with skip-trace and stakeout skills win it. Different buyers, evidence, pricing and winners, so it decomposes into **Volume Creditor & Eviction Service** and **Hard-to-Serve & Skip-Trace Service**.

Three terminal calls were close and are recorded honestly. **Patent Search & Prior-Art Analysis** could split into patentability and invalidity searching, which have different buyers and clocks; it is kept whole because one test governs both — whether the search is later beaten by art someone else finds — and freedom-to-operate work, which is claim reading rather than prior-art search, is a minority of volume. The split matters in Pass 2 instead, where the two halves are logged as separate pockets with different clocks and buyers. **Court Filing & E-Filing Services** sits next to [[niches/legal-practice-software/court-rules-deadline-content/profile|Court Rules & Deadline Content]]; it is kept because its contest is acceptance by the clerk once the deadline is known, not computing the deadline. **Freelance & Contract Paralegal Services** and **Self-Represented Litigants & Legal Document Preparers** both draft legal documents for non-lawyer pay; they are separate because one works under attorney supervision and sells review time saved, while the other faces the public directly and sells staying on the right side of the unauthorised-practice line.

Five candidates were considered and rejected. **Notary and loan-signing work** is in this NAICS code but is built as its own industry, [[industries/notary-signing-services|Notary & Signing Services]]. **Trial and jury consulting** is also in the code; its contest is bespoke trial strategy, and the pocket is already profiled as [[niches/personal-injury-law/trial-consulting-jury-research/profile|Trial Consulting & Jury Research]]. **Subpoenaed records retrieval** was rejected because its bulk is medical records, already analysed under [[niches/personal-injury-law/medical-record-review-chronology/profile|Medical Record Retrieval & Chronology Services]]. **Patent prosecution by independent registered agents** — drafting applications and answering office actions without a law firm — is covered only where it serves individual inventors and startups, in niche 6; agent prosecution for corporate filers was rejected as its own niche because most of the USPTO's 14,873 active agents work inside law firms and corporate IP departments rather than as independent 541199 practices (estimate), and the contest there — claim scope that survives examination at a fixed fee — is the law firm's, not this industry's. **Patent annuity and renewal payment services** were rejected because the honest contest is the lowest mark-up on official fees, a generic price contest, and their operators are IP management firms rather than searchers. Transcripts and depositions belong to the queued court-reporting-firms industry, and private investigation to the queued investigation-and-screening-firms industry.

## Why These Pockets

No pocket qualifies, and that is the finding. The operators fail the gate almost everywhere. Process servers, paralegals and document preparers work alone or in tiny firms; even the national service networks, which hold millions of geotagged attempts, employ servers and dispatchers rather than analysts; registered agents, which see every suit served on their clients before most dockets do, staff intake rather than research and hold documents that belong to the entities served; and the rollups' corporate development teams are too small to count. Several pockets that touch this chain are already profiled elsewhere and get no second profile: [[niches/personal-injury-law/litigation-analytics-court-data/profile|Court Data & Litigation Analytics Platforms]] (53, indexed under Personal Injury Law), [[niches/collections-agencies/identity-skip-trace-data-providers/profile|Identity Resolution & Skip Trace Data Providers]] (54, the skip-trace data servers buy), [[niches/hr-consultants/background-screening-providers/profile|Background Screening Providers]] (55, whose court-record researchers overlap courthouse runners), [[niches/collections-agencies/collection-litigation-analytics/profile|Consumer Finance Litigation Analytics]] (44, the volume filers' side), [[niches/small-law-firms/e-discovery-providers/profile|E-Discovery Service Providers]], [[niches/small-law-firms/alternative-legal-service-providers/profile|Alternative Legal Service Providers]] and [[niches/personal-injury-law/legal-process-outsourcing-demand-drafting/profile|Legal Process Outsourcing & Demand Package Drafting]] (all fenced by privilege and client data ownership), [[niches/personal-injury-law/trial-consulting-jury-research/profile|Trial Consulting & Jury Research]] (43, in this NAICS code), and [[niches/graphic-design-studios/trademark-clearance-search-providers/profile|Trademark Clearance & Design-Mark Search Providers]] (47, the trademark counterpart of patent search).

The only research functions of real size are the industry's own top tier of patent search, a field of ~10–20 houses with ten or more search analysts, mostly India-based (estimate). Their invalidity work (49) has the shape this sweep looks for — a forecast-like product that is later tested against public, dated outcomes, since IPR petitions, institution decisions and reexamination requests are public — but the archive that would teach an engine is the searches delivered to clients, commissioned through litigation counsel as work product, and the house may not reuse it across engagements. That is the vault's majority rule for litigation work done through counsel, so the pocket logs behind a kill switch. Patentability and freedom-to-operate searching (46) is the same houses under the same rule: patentability work is confidential until 18-month publication, and FTO maps public patents against a client's confidential product. Invalidity contentions, the district-court half of the work, are served rather than docketed and often sit under protective order, so even the label is only partly public. The PTAB's 2025 swing toward discretionary denial, which cut institution rates from 68% to 23% across three quarters before a rebound to 41% in the fourth, has made the first search more consequential and the public label set thinner.

The strongest clean near-miss is patent information databases (48): the expert-rewritten corpus every searcher works in, with no outside clock and a handful of producers, scored consistently with chemistry reference databases. Defensive patent aggregators (42) do real research with public labels for a market of four or five organisations. PCT search contractors (40) have the hardest clock in the sweep, own no data, and sell to one federal buyer behind a confidentiality gate.
