# Niche Analysis — Executive Search Firms

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/executive-search-firms|Executive Search Firms]]

## Niche Selection

Executive search sells a judgement — this person will succeed here and will actually move — and grades none of it. Around that judgement sit a research workflow rebuilt on every mandate (target companies, levelled maps, longlists, off-limits and privacy clearance) and a set of client-set clocks: a CEO's departure, a board's proxy, a sponsor's plan, a committee's vote. The eight niches below split the industry by what firms and their suppliers actually compete over. Market sizes are estimates of client fee spend and of the research, data and tooling spend inside each niche, not of the ~$12.6B US or ~$20B global industry as a whole.

## Why These Niches

Corporate C-suite search is the largest share because it is where the large firms earn most of their fees — Korn Ferry's $846.2M of fiscal 2025 search revenue and Heidrick's 418 consultants are organised around CEO and functional practices — and private-equity portfolio search is second because sponsors are the most frequent repeat buyers, with an unusually concrete definition of success. The two low-digitized niches are where relationships and paper still run the work: board slates assembled from partners' director networks, and committee-run university and nonprofit searches scored on paper under open-records law. The two underserved audiences are the person the search process forgets the day they start, and the boutique — 93% of the 5,897 US establishments have fewer than 20 employees — that competes with firms whose research centres employ hundreds. The two automatable niches are the most mechanical parts of every search: identifying and levelling who holds each target role, and clearing each name against client terms and candidate privacy law.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. Corporate C-Suite & CEO Search is not: its contested sentence produced two contests with different winners. A CEO succession is bought by the board, timed by the incumbent's tenure and decided on assessment — with 69% of new S&P 500 CEOs promoted internally in Spencer Stuart's 2026 index, the firm that has already assessed the insiders against the market wins when the CEO resigns. A functional C-suite search is bought by the CEO and chief people officer when a vacancy appears and is decided on preparation: the firm with the CFO or CHRO pool already mapped, levelled and priced puts a slate up first. Different buyers, clocks and evidence, so it decomposes into **CEO Succession & Insider-Outsider Benchmarking** and **Functional C-Suite Search**.

Two terminal calls were close and are recorded honestly. **Private Equity Portfolio Leadership Search** bundles post-close searches with pre-deal management assessments, which run on the diligence calendar; it stays terminal because the same talent partner buys both and judges both by one outcome — whether the executive is still in the seat at exit — so one contest governs. **Board Director Search** could have absorbed board effectiveness reviews, which search firms' board practices also sell; reviews are a different contest (interview-based diagnosis on a governance-code cycle, owned by the board), so they are logged as a Pass 2 pocket instead of widening the niche.

Three candidates were considered and rejected. **Interim executive placement** has a different model — a bench of available operators paid by the day — and its contest (who has a vetted interim free next week) sits closer to [[industries/staffing-agencies|Staffing Agencies]] than to retained search. **Diversity-led search** was rejected because, after the Fifth Circuit vacated Nasdaq's board diversity rules in December 2024, the remaining contest is slate breadth, which is already the fix in Board Director Search; a standalone sentence came out generic. **Professional and mid-level search** (contingency recruiting below the executive tier) was rejected as belonging to [[industries/staffing-agencies|Staffing Agencies]] and [[industries/recruiting-tech-vendors|Recruiting Tech Vendors]], per this industry's boundary.

## Why These Pockets

No pocket qualifies, and the reason is consistent: the evidence that would make this industry's insight valuable — assessments of executives before their outcomes are known — is either owned by the client or never written down. The operator layer is unusual in one respect. Most of its 5,897 US establishments are boutiques far below the gate, but the large firms run genuine research functions of hundreds, and their research and knowledge centres (44) are the strongest clean near-miss: growing with headcount, repeatable in shape (Korn Ferry opened more than 6,300 engagements in fiscal 2025), and sitting on decades of interviews and placements nobody else holds. They fall short on the rubric's two outside tests and on one inside one. Clients hire on partner relationships and pay for a placement process, so Q1 is 4; every deadline in a search is the client's own, so Q4 is 3; and part of the headcount behind them (engagement managers) coordinates rather than researches, so Q2 is 4. Placement-outcome tracking — does the hire survive 18 months — exists as a public label (Form 8-K Item 5.02 within four business days) and as nobody's product: the transition counters (27) publish departures without linking them to the search or assessment that preceded them.

The advisory pockets have the right shape and the wrong data rights. Leadership assessment and succession advisory (45) invoices exactly the judgement this sweep looks for, but the assessments are of the client's own executives under confidentiality — the same client-ownership rule that sets engagement survey vendors behind a kill switch. Board effectiveness reviewers (40) have the cleanest external clock in the chain, the UK Code's three-year external review and a US shift to third-party evaluation at 33% of S&P 500 boards, but interview-based reviews scale poorly and the findings belong to each board. Bank fit-and-proper supervisors handled 1,672 individual procedures at the ECB alone in 2025 and fail the gate as case examiners.

The clean data businesses sell public information to a handful of buyers: executive and board profile vendors (37) compile from public sources and are scored consistently with [[niches/directory-and-list-publishers/b2b-contact-research-operations/profile|B2B Contact Research Operations]] (37), and proxy pay data vendors (34) extract public filings for three or four competitors, on a proxy season whose deadlines bind the issuers rather than the vendor. Several neighbouring pockets are already profiled elsewhere and get no second profile here: [[niches/corporate-training/talent-assessment-publishers/profile|Talent Assessment Publishers]] (52, indexed), whose instruments the assessment practices use; [[niches/hr-consultants/executive-compensation-consultants/profile|Executive Compensation Consultants]] (49) and [[niches/hr-consultants/compensation-survey-publishers/profile|Compensation Survey Publishers]] (54, indexed), which own the pay-advice and survey layers; [[niches/asset-managers/proxy-advisory-firms/profile|Proxy Advisory Firms]] (49), whose voting recommendations govern contested director elections; [[niches/hr-consultants/background-screening-providers/profile|Background Screening Providers]] (55, indexed), the volume end of candidate screening; and [[niches/corporate-training/labor-market-skills-data/profile|Labour Market & Skills Taxonomy Data Providers]] (54, indexed), whose posting-based data rarely reaches executive roles. Executive due-diligence investigators — Mintz Group is AESC's due-diligence partner, and the AESC–Mintz 2024 survey found background checks raised concerns or ended a candidacy in 22% of searches — belong to the queued investigation-and-screening-firms industry and are left to it.
