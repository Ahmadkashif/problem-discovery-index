# Testing the Button Colour Again

**Niche:** [[niches/conversion-optimization-firms/test-capacity/profile|Test Capacity Allocation]]
**Industry:** [[industries/conversion-optimization-firms|Conversion Optimization Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The backlog is full of small visual tweaks because they are quick to build, and the calendar is full for a quarter.
**Tags:** #quick-win #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #confidence-intervals #optimization-fundamentals #revenue-impact #hypothesis-testing #workflow-orchestration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to spend a finite number of tests on the changes most likely to produce a detectable effect, when the selection is currently by ease of building — and whoever allocates it takes the account.

## The Problem
Test backlogs fill with small changes: copy tweaks, colour changes, button positions, minor layout adjustments. They are quick to build, easy to get approved, and produce effects far smaller than the programme can detect. The calendar is full, the report shows activity, and the changes that might have produced a real effect — a restructured flow, a removed step, a different offer — are not tested because they are harder to build and harder to get agreed.

## Why It's Still Broken
Ease is the dominant scoring factor — a prioritisation framework that rewards low implementation effort will fill the calendar with low-impact changes, which is exactly what it is designed to do. Bigger changes need engineering and approval. A full calendar looks productive. And nobody reports how much capacity went to undetectable tests.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Report what the capacity bought and shift the mix deliberately. Classify every past test by the size of the change and report the mix, which is the fix and usually shows the problem starkly. Compute what proportion of the programme's capacity went to tests that could not have detected a worthwhile effect. Set a floor for the share of capacity spent on substantive changes, which is a policy rather than a calculation. Bundle small changes into a single test where the mechanism is common, which recovers capacity without losing the ideas. Reserve capacity for larger tests deliberately rather than letting the quick ones fill it. Bring engineering into the prioritisation, since implementation difficulty is why the mix is wrong. Reweight or discard the ease factor in the scoring framework. Report results by change size, which usually shows where the real effects came from. Present the capacity as the scarce resource in client conversations rather than the test count. And stop counting tests run as a programme metric, which is what sustains the whole pattern.

## Who Feels the Pain
Clients paying for a full calendar of undetectable tests; strategists who know the big ideas are not being tested; the programme's results, which are mostly noise from small changes; and the case for the programme at renewal.

## Impact If Fixed
A prioritisation framework that rewards low implementation effort will fill the calendar with low-impact changes, exactly as designed. Reporting the capacity mix by change size usually shows the problem in one chart.
