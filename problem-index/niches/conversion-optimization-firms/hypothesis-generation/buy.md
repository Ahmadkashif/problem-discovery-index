**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to tell which of an endless supply of observations describes a mechanism worth testing — and whoever makes that selection takes the account.

## The Problem
Scientific practice distinguishes carefully between an observation, a proposed mechanism that would explain it, and a prediction the mechanism makes that could be wrong. The discipline exists because observations are unlimited and explanations are cheap, and only a prediction that could fail teaches anything. Conversion optimisation backlogs contain items like "change the button to green" — which is a proposed change with no stated mechanism and no prediction, so whatever happens teaches nothing generalisable.

## What Already Exists
The observation-mechanism-prediction distinction; falsifiable prediction as the standard for a testable claim; competing explanations evaluated against each other; prediction specificity as a quality criterion; and generalisation from mechanism rather than from result.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to a commercial programme where the goal is a better site rather than knowledge. It requires: (1) the practical aim being an improvement rather than an explanation, so the discipline must earn its place by producing better tests rather than by being correct — this is the substantive difference; (2) a weekly cadence with a client expecting changes, which resists deliberation; (3) mechanisms that are about a specific audience on a specific site rather than general principles; (4) practitioners who are strategists rather than researchers; and (5) a body of prior findings that is mostly unreliable, so the literature cannot be leaned on.

## Target Customer
Conversion optimisation firms and experimentation teams, product and UX research functions, behavioural analytics vendors, and testing platform providers.

## Impact If Solved
Science separates observation from mechanism from prediction because only a prediction that could fail teaches anything. A commercial programme wanting a better site rather than knowledge is why the discipline must earn its place by producing better tests.
