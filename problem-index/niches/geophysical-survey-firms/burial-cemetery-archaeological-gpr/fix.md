# The Anomaly Count Reported as a Body Count

**Niche:** [[niches/geophysical-survey-firms/burial-cemetery-archaeological-gpr/profile|Burial, Cemetery & Archaeological GPR]]
**Industry:** [[industries/geophysical-survey-firms|Geophysical Survey Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A report lists "reflections of interest," the number reaches the press as graves found, and when excavation or records later disagree, the method — and the community — are blamed.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #bayesian-inference #confidence-intervals #compliance #worker-facing
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to produce a map of probable burials that families, tribes and regulators accept as honest — neither overclaiming anomalies as graves nor missing them — and whoever's confidence language holds up when ground-truthing happens is hired for the next search.

## The Problem
Searches at residential and boarding school sites have shown how quickly anomaly counts become headlines. Reports are written for technical readers; communities and media need a different explanation; the interpreter is rarely in the room when the number is announced.

## Why It's Still Broken
There is no shared standard for how to express confidence in burial detection, no template for community-facing summaries, and no time budgeted for them in small contracts.

## What a Fix Looks Like
A two-layer deliverable: the technical report, and a plain-language summary generated from it with the confidence categories explained, the number of features in each, what the survey could not see, and what would confirm or rule out each category — reviewed by the interpreter and the community's representative before release.

## Who Feels the Pain
Families and communities, interpreters whose work is misquoted, and the archaeologists and tribal officers who must manage the aftermath.

## Impact If Fixed
Findings communicated once, accurately, in words that survive scrutiny.
