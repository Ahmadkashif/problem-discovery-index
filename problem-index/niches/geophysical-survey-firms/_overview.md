# Niche Analysis — Geophysical Survey Firms

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/geophysical-survey-firms|Geophysical Survey Firms]]

## Niche Selection

A geophysical survey firm sells an interpretation of something nobody can see, and the client's excavator, core drill or drill bit later shows whether it was right. The industry splits into an energy half — seismic crews and processing houses working for oil and gas operators and multi-client libraries, concentrated in Texas — and a near-surface half of small firms designating utilities, classifying munitions, scanning concrete and searching for graves, plus a mining business serving junior explorers. The eight niches below split the category by what competitors actually fight over. Market sizes are estimates of survey, processing, data and tooling spend within each niche, including work billed by firms coded outside NAICS 541360, not of industry receipts (the Census counts ~$1.99B across 968 firms).

## Why These Niches

Energy seismic takes the largest share because it is where the industry's revenue concentrates — Texas holds 35% of 541360 receipts, and a single onshore crew runs 40–100 technicians and hundreds of thousands of channels — and utility designating is second because state DOTs, transit and rail authorities increasingly specify ASCE 38 quality levels in design contracts (MassDOT's SUE directive is one example), making QL-B geophysics the steadiest near-surface revenue. The two low-digitized niches are where the work still runs on paper: seismic access negotiated door to door and recorded in margins, and decades of seismic held as paper sections and tapes. The two underserved audiences are buyers the tools ignore: the junior explorer with one season's money and no geophysicist, and the communities searching for unmarked graves, whose question is consequential and whose tools were built for utilities. The two automatable niches are the most mechanical: munitions classification, where inversion, library matching and a fixed zero-miss acceptance rule define the job, and concrete scanning, the same handheld GPR task on near-identical slabs thousands of times a year.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. Energy Seismic Acquisition & Processing is not: its contested sentence produced two contests with different winners. In the field, the contest is clean source and receiver points recorded per crew-day across permit gaps, weather and terrain — a logistics and equipment contest won by whoever keeps a large-channel crew productive at the bid price. In processing, the contest is a depth image whose velocity model ties the wells before a lease sale or drilling decision — a compute and judgement contest won by the imaging house, which never sees the field. Different firms, buyers, inputs and clocks, so it decomposes into **Onshore Seismic Acquisition Crews** and **Seismic Processing & Imaging**.

Three terminal calls were close. **Subsurface Utility Designating** overlaps with the one-call locating world logged under [[industries/utility-contractors|Utility Contractors]]; it stays separate because its contest — designations that survive QL-A test holes on a design schedule — is a different deliverable to a different buyer from marks painted for a 48-hour excavation ticket. **Concrete Scanning** uses the same GPR physics as designating and is often sold by the same national platforms, but the buyer (a superintendent before a core), the clock (same day) and the failure (a cut tendon) differ. **UXO Advanced Geophysical Classification** could split into DoD land sites and offshore wind; the DAGCAP zero-miss rule governs the land contest, and the offshore risk work is captured as a Pass 2 pocket rather than a level-1 niche.

Four candidates were considered and rejected. **Offshore wind site characterisation** is large in Europe but performed mostly by marine survey companies outside this industry's US base, and its UXO component is logged in Pass 2. **Carbon storage and geothermal monitoring** has no contest distinct from time-lapse processing and induced-seismicity monitoring yet; it is logged in Pass 2 under monitoring services. **Groundwater and environmental resistivity surveys** are real work, but their contested sentence comes out as "a cheaper, faster report", which fails the filter; the work belongs with [[industries/environmental-consultants|Environmental Consultants]]. **Airborne geophysics for mining** was folded into the junior-explorer niche on the buyer side; the airborne firms themselves are a Pass 2 pocket.

## Why These Pockets

The operators fail the gate, and that is the central finding. Three quarters of the 968 US firms have fewer than five employees; the larger seismic contractors run big crews but no research function, and "the results of seismic surveys conducted for a client belong to that client." The same structural fact recurs up the chain. Offshore UXO consultancies, induced-seismicity monitors, airborne survey firms, mining interpretation consultancies, E&P geoscience teams, royalty companies and BOEM all hold rich, gradeable data — and all hold it under someone else's licence, confidentiality or statute, which is why seven of the sixteen pockets carry a kill switch. The test applied to every pocket is the same: a corpus held under client confidentiality with reuse restrictions is a kill switch; a corpus that ends up public only lowers Q3. It is the Pass 1 problem restated: the party that makes the interpretation is rarely the party that owns the ground truth.

No pocket qualifies. The closest, at 48, shows the pattern most clearly. A multi-client library owns its data outright, sells the processed image as the invoice, and works to a clock set by lease sales, budget cycles and mergers. It also has the shape that recurs across indexed pockets in this vault: a forecast-like product with an unambiguous label that arrives later and is never graded. Every licensed depth volume is drilled by licensees, the well tops become public through regulators, and the library does not measure its own depth error — although that record would tell it which vintages to reprocess before the next lease sale and give licensees a reason to pay for its data rather than a competitor's. Its change-of-control fees, triggered by every upstream merger, are a second, plainer revenue leak. It falls short on labour mass and market: licensing an owned library earns without adding analysts (Pulse Seismic runs on 15 staff), and the buyers are five firms.

The next-highest raw score, the offshore and civil UXO risk consultancy (46), is kill-switched: its desk studies are repeatable and run to a hard consent clock, but its cross-project view of how many survey targets turn out to be ordnance sits in developers' confidential reports. Induced-seismicity monitors (43) fail the same way where the arrays belong to the operator. The cleanest near-miss is the site-specific seismic hazard practice (44), whose measured velocity profiles are its own but whose work is bespoke site by site. The near-surface insight business that does clear the bar elsewhere, one-call ticket screening and locating, is indexed under [[niches/utility-contractors/underground-locating-damage-prevention/profile|Underground Utility Locating & Damage Prevention]], and the SUE practices themselves are logged at 45 under [[niches/utility-contractors/subsurface-utility-engineering/profile|Subsurface Utility Engineering & Mapping]]; neither is re-scored here.
