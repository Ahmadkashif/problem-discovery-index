# Legacy Geophysical Data Rescue

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/geophysical-survey-firms|Geophysical Survey Firms]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to turn decades of paper sections, field tapes and observer logs into correctly navigated, loadable digital data with clean licence title, and whoever does it at the lowest cost per line-kilometre without navigation errors wins the archive.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$80M North American transcription, vectorisation and reprocessing preparation of legacy seismic and potential-field data (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~3% of US geophysical survey spend (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Low — the inputs are paper and film sections, nine-track and cartridge tapes, hand-annotated shot-point maps and typed observer reports
**Target Buyer:** Geoscience Data Manager at an operator, library or state geological survey
**Automation Potential:** High for scanning and vectorising; low for reconstructing navigation and resolving ownership

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Much of the onshore US was shot in 2D between the 1960s and 1990s, and much of that data survives only as paper sections, film and tapes in warehouses. New plays, carbon storage site screening and geothermal exploration revive interest in it, and libraries license it — Pulse Seismic's Canadian library alone holds about 829,000 km of 2D. Before it can be used, someone must scan or transcribe it, recover the navigation (where each shot point was), match it to the right line, and establish who owns the licence rights. That is slow, specialist, mostly manual work done by a handful of small firms.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Large-format scanners, raster-to-SEG-Y vectorising software, tape transcription hardware, GIS for digitising shot-point maps, and spreadsheets for chain of title. Gaps: navigation recovery from scanned maps is done by hand, header and map mismatches are found late, and ownership records are separate from the data.
