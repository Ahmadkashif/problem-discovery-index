# Niche Analysis — Aircraft MRO & FBO Shops

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/aircraft-mro-shops|Aircraft MRO & FBO Shops]]

## Niche Selection

An aircraft maintenance shop sells two things: work done to approved data, and the signed record that proves it. Both run against clocks the shop doesn't set: airworthiness directives, inspection intervals, the customer's schedule and the departure time of a transient jet. The eight niches below split the industry by what vendors and internal teams actually compete over. They are planning engine shop visits and keeping their dates, absorbing heavy-check findings, proving an aircraft's history from paper logbooks, turning a transient fault around overnight at an FBO, keeping a small repair station's quality system audit-ready, making new mechanics productive, checking work cards against ADs, and drafting the maintenance record itself. Market sizes are estimates of software, services and the labour those tools would replace in each niche, not of the industry's ~$23.3B in 2022 receipts or North America's ~$31.8B 2026 commercial MRO demand.

## Why These Niches

Engine shop visits take the largest share because engines are the largest MRO segment ($14.8B of North America's $31.8B in 2026, Oliver Wyman), and turn times there have stretched the most. Airframe heavy checks are second because North America's ageing fleet keeps non-routine findings high and hangar slots tight. The two low-digitized niches are where paper still holds the work: the logbooks that prove an aircraft's history at every sale and redelivery, and the FBO shop where a transient fault is diagnosed, authorised and signed off by phone overnight. The two underserved audiences are the small repair station carrying a full Part 145 quality system with one quality manager (2,825 of 3,358 firms in the code have fewer than 20 employees), and the newly certificated mechanic arriving as 41% of the certificated workforce passes 60. The two automatable niches are the most mechanical language work in the hangar: checking a work package against public ADs and SDRs, the owner's MVP idea, and drafting the record the mechanic signs.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. Engine Shop Visits & Overhaul is not. Writing its contested sentence produced two contests with different winners. On cost, the contest is building each engine to the cheapest workscope that still gives the owner the on-wing life they need, with every life-limited part proven back to birth. The winner is whoever predicts module findings and part lives most accurately at induction, which is a records and engineering capability. On the date, the contest is quoting a release date and hitting it while parts and repair vendors slip. The winner is whoever controls slots, repair loops and the test cell best, which is an operations capability. Inputs, buyers inside the shop and failure modes differ, so it decomposes into **LLP & Workscope Planning** and **Engine Shop Slot & Turn-Time Commitments**. The parent keeps the induction assessment and customer approvals that span both.

Three terminal calls were close. **Aircraft Maintenance Records & Logbook Audits** and **AD & Work-Card Compliance Checking** both read AD records. The records contest is proving past compliance complete before a buyer's auditor finds a gap, won on reading decades of logbooks; the AD contest is checking the work about to be signed, won on applicability parsing against the current AD library. Different documents, different clocks, different winners. **Maintenance Entry & Non-Routine Card Drafting** overlaps the AD niche at the work card, but drafting is won on first-time QA acceptance of the mechanic's own words, and checking on missed-applicability rate. **Airframe Heavy Checks** and **Engine Shop Slot & Turn-Time Commitments** both turn on a redelivery date. The heavy check's date is driven by non-routine findings on an aircraft that stays in the hangar, the engine's by pieces sent out to repair vendors, so they stay separate.

Five candidates were considered and rejected. **Used serviceable material trading and parts sourcing** is the business of the queued aviation parts distributors industry; only the shop's use-or-buy decision is kept, inside LLP & Workscope Planning. **Avionics installation shops** were considered as an underserved audience. Their contest is getting an STC or field approval for an installation, which is the DER work already logged as [[niches/aerospace-parts-suppliers/pma-der-engineering-consultancies/profile|🔍 PMA & DER Engineering Consultancies]], and their paperwork problems are the same as any small repair station's. **FBO fuel, ramp and hangar operations** belong to the airfield side and the queued airport operators industry. **Interior completions and refurbishment** were rejected because the contest is craft and design taste, which fails the filter as "better finish". **PMA part installation** was checked because aerospace parts suppliers left it here; installing a PMA part is ordinary maintenance against approved data, with no contest of its own beyond the records and AD checks already covered.

## Why These Pockets

Nothing qualifies, and the operators themselves fail the gate. A repair station's engineering serves its own shop visits, and the records it works from belong to the aircraft owner. The organisations around it repeat two patterns. The first is that the richest maintenance data in the industry is held on someone else's behalf. Tracking services hold owners' records under subscription (39, kill switch). OEM reliability groups hold fleet data inside their own programmes (37, kill switch). The troubleshooting platform's confirmed fixes are gathered through OEM help desks (35, kill switch). Litigation experts work on counsel's files (38, kill switch). Borescope AI developers train on images collected under shop and airline partnerships (32, kill switch on that half). The second is that much of the headcount that looks like analysis is case-by-case production. Hourly-programme technical advisors approve one shop visit at a time, records consultancies audit one aircraft at a time, and GA maintenance managers handle one owner's events at a time, so all three fail the gate even though each sits on an interesting record.

The strongest clean pockets score only 31: the rollup platforms' corporate development teams, whose analysis serves internal capital decisions, and the technical publication libraries, which sell access to licensed content more than analysis. The most valuable record that fails the gate is the hourly engine maintenance programmes' archive. JSSI and the OEM programmes hold the best cross-MRO record of what business aviation shop visits cost, as their own claims data, but the forecasting team that would use it is small and the invoice is a risk-transfer rate. The car-repair counterpart, [[niches/auto-repair-shops/confirmed-fix-databases/profile|🔍 Confirmed-Fix Diagnostic Knowledge Databases]] (52), is the vault's only qualifying repair pocket. Aviation has no independent equivalent, because each manufacturer controls its technical data; the nearest are the OEM-bound fault-isolation platforms (35) and the general aviation engine-data service, whose data-use terms could not be confirmed. Technician testing has the same shape as [[niches/auto-repair-shops/ase-certification-psychometrics/profile|🔍 Technician Certification & Psychometrics]] (44), but in aviation the FAA writes the knowledge tests (recalled), so it is a regulator function and is not scored again. The FAA's own repair-station oversight fails the gate the way its production-oversight office does under aerospace parts ([[niches/aerospace-parts-suppliers/faa-production-oversight/profile|FAA Production Oversight]]): inspectors are production headcount, and its most useful output for this industry is the free SDR file.

Several pockets that matter here were already profiled under aerospace parts suppliers and are linked, not re-scored. MRO and aftermarket demand forecasts (Cirium, Aviation Week, Forecast International) are [[niches/aerospace-parts-suppliers/aerospace-fleet-production-forecasts/profile|🔍 Aerospace Fleet & Production Forecast Data]] (43). MRO strategy boutiques such as AeroDynamic Advisory and Alton Aviation Consultancy sit in [[niches/aerospace-parts-suppliers/aerospace-supply-chain-advisory/profile|🔍 Aerospace Supply Chain & M&A Advisory]] (39, kill switch). Repair and alteration approvals by designees are [[niches/aerospace-parts-suppliers/pma-der-engineering-consultancies/profile|🔍 PMA & DER Engineering Consultancies]] (38, kill switch). The aviation insurers that write hangarkeepers and repair-station liability are the same carriers as [[niches/aerospace-parts-suppliers/aviation-products-liability-underwriters/profile|🔍 Aviation Products Liability Underwriters]] (32). Contract NDT and failure analysis are [[niches/metal-fabrication/nondestructive-testing-services/profile|🔍 Nondestructive Testing Services]] (48, kill switch) and [[niches/metal-fabrication/materials-testing-failure-analysis/profile|🔍 Materials Testing & Failure Analysis]] (46, kill switch). The commercial opening in this industry is therefore Pass 1's. Paperwork and judgement-capture vendors are selling to shops: Zymbly is hiring a forward deployed engineer in San Francisco or London, Fleetcraft a founding engineer in London only and Soji AI a senior forward deployed engineer in Hamburg only, while CAMP (Merrimack, New Hampshire) and JSSI (Chicago) are hiring engineers and data leaders.
