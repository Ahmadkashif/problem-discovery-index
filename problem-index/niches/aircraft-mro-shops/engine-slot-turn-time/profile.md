# Engine Shop Slot & Turn-Time Commitments

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/aircraft-mro-shops|Aircraft MRO & FBO Shops]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to quote an engine's release date at induction and hit it while parts and repair vendors slip, and whoever keeps the most engines inside their quoted turn time takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$150M a year in shop scheduling, repair-loop tracking, expediting and customer-status work (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~1% of North America's ~$14.8B engine MRO demand (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Medium-low — MRO systems hold work orders; the real schedule lives in planners' spreadsheets, daily stand-ups and phone calls to repair vendors
**Target Buyer:** Shop production director or customer programme manager at an engine shop
**Automation Potential:** High for forecasting and status reporting; medium for re-planning; low for the physical constraints (test cells, specialist inspectors)

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
With engine turnaround far above pre-pandemic levels (Bain: up 35% or more for legacy engines and over 150% for new-generation ones), owners now choose shops on a believable date almost as much as on price. A January 2026 UBS survey found 44% of MRO operators reporting improved turnaround, so the market is starting to separate shops that can deliver from those that can't. The date depends on things the shop doesn't fully control. Parts arrive late. Piece parts go out to repair vendors and component shops in loops of weeks. The test cell is a single shared resource. Inspectors with specific authorisations are scarce. Unlike the workscope, which is won on engineering prediction, this contest is won on scheduling, expediting and honest communication. The induction slot itself is sold months ahead, so the commitment is made before the shop knows what the engine needs.

## Current Tools & Gaps
MRO system work orders and stores modules; generic scheduling tools; email and phone to repair vendors; customer portals at the largest shops. The gaps: no forecast with a range at induction; repair-vendor loops tracked by phone; and customer updates written by hand from stand-up notes.
