# The Hidden Route to a Human

**Niche:** [[niches/customer-support-platforms/customers-who-cannot-self-serve/profile|Customers Who Cannot Self-Serve]]
**Industry:** [[industries/customer-support-platforms|Customer Support Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Reaching a person is made deliberately difficult because every contact costs money, and the customers least able to navigate the obstacles are the ones most likely to need the help.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #hypothesis-testing #confidence-intervals #compliance #worker-facing #quick-win #workflow-orchestration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor that takes this seriously is fighting to get a customer who cannot use the self-service path to a person quickly and in their own language — and whoever stops routing them in circles takes a population the category has optimised against.

## The Problem
A customer needs to speak to someone. The help centre offers articles. The chat widget offers an automated assistant that suggests articles. Asking for a human produces a further suggestion. The phone number is not on the contact page. When found, the menu has no option matching the issue and no obvious path to an operator. Eventually they reach someone, or they give up. The design is deliberate and the rationale is cost, and its effect falls hardest on people who cannot use the alternatives — which means the cost has been shifted onto exactly the customers with the least capacity to absorb it, and shows up in the organisation's metrics as successful deflection.

## Why It's Still Broken
Cost per contact is real and the pressure is genuine, so making the human path harder is an effective lever with an immediately visible return. The cost is borne by customers and is invisible in the organisation's own data, since abandonment and deflection are indistinguishable. And nobody inside the organisation experiences the flow as a customer with a difficult issue, which is why these designs survive internal review — they are tested by people who know where the number is.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Make the path available and measure who uses it. A clear, consistently available route to a person from every channel, which is a design decision rather than a technical one and is the substantive change. Then the measurement that makes it affordable: who takes it, for what, and which of those contacts were genuinely not resolvable otherwise — because the honest finding is usually that a large share of people asking for a person are asking because the automated path failed them specifically, and the fix is the automated path rather than the barrier. Detect repeated failure and offer the human path proactively rather than waiting for the customer to find it, which is better service and, counterintuitively, frequently cheaper because it prevents the multi-attempt journeys that generate several contacts. Report the abandonment rate inside automated flows separately from deflection, which is the number that would show what the barrier is actually doing. And be specific about the populations: time to human for customers using assistive technology, for those writing in unsupported languages, and for those showing distress signals, reported separately — because the aggregate will always look acceptable while these groups are failed.

## Who Feels the Pain
Customers who need a person and cannot find one, disproportionately those who are disabled, older, unwell, in distress or not operating in their first language; agents who receive them at the end of a long journey; and the organisation, whose apparent deflection savings partly consist of people who gave up.

## Impact If Fixed
Separating abandonment from deflection is free and shows what the barrier actually produces. Offering the human path proactively after repeated failure is better service and frequently cheaper once multi-attempt journeys are counted. And reporting time-to-human for the specific populations is the only mechanism that would make their experience visible to the people designing the system.
