# Customers Who Cannot Self-Serve

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/customer-support-platforms|Customer Support Platforms]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor that takes this seriously is fighting to get a customer who cannot use the self-service path to a person quickly and in their own language — and whoever stops routing them in circles takes a population the category has optimised against.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$890M US addressable across accessibility, multilingual support and assisted service
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~6% of support software revenue, against a large and growing share of customers
**Digital Adoption:** Low — this is the population the deflection investment is designed to route around
**Target Buyer:** Support leaders, with regulators and accessibility obligations as the forcing function
**Automation Potential:** High — translation, accessibility and routing are all mature and unevenly applied

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Every investment in the category over the past decade has been aimed at customers who can resolve their own issue through a self-service or automated path. The customers who cannot are the residual, and the system treats them as such: someone with a screen reader navigating a chat widget that was never tested with one, someone whose first language is not among the two the company supports, someone with a cognitive disability trying to follow a multi-step automated flow, an older customer who wants a person, someone in genuine distress whose issue is not a category in any menu. They experience a support function that is structurally organised to prevent them reaching help, because every mechanism between them and a human was built to reduce the number of people who get there. Accessibility obligations apply to these channels and are unevenly met; the rest is not obligated and is simply not built. Machine translation is cheap and good, and most companies support two languages and apologise for the rest — because translation quality is unverifiable at the moment it matters and nobody will risk being wrong in a language they cannot read.

## Current Tools & Gaps
The platforms provide accessibility features of varying quality, translation integrations, and escalation paths. Specialist accessible communication and relay services exist. The gaps: the deflection path is designed and tested for the mainstream case, so a customer using assistive technology encounters an interface that was never exercised that way; multilingual support is limited by an unwillingness to answer in a language nobody on the team can verify, which is a quality assurance problem rather than a translation problem and is solvable; vulnerability and distress signals are not detected, so a customer in difficulty is routed by the same rules as everyone else; and the path to a human is deliberately obscured, which is a design choice with a cost that falls entirely on this population.
