# Teaching, Not Running a Funnel

**Niche:** [[niches/online-course-platforms/the-instructor/profile|The Instructor]]
**Industry:** [[industries/online-course-platforms|Online Course Platforms]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** The person who knows the subject spends their week on pricing experiments and launch sequences.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #automation #evaluation-metrics #large-language-models #revenue-impact #descriptive-statistics #confidence-intervals
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to let someone who knows a subject spend their week teaching rather than on funnels, pricing and platform changes — and whoever removes that overhead keeps the experts the whole catalogue depends on.

## The Problem
An instructor's week contains: producing content, designing the course, setting and testing prices, writing launch emails, running promotions, handling refunds, answering questions, moderating a community, keeping the material current, and reacting to platform changes they learn about from a forum. Almost none of it is teaching. The platform provides a video host and a storefront and leaves the rest, which means the constraint on good instruction is how much business administration a subject expert can tolerate.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
Platforms position themselves as distribution, so everything except hosting and payment was left to the creator — a marketplace that sees itself as a channel does not build the operating system for its suppliers. Instructor-side tooling does not drive enrolment, which is what the platform is measured on. The overhead is distributed across many small tasks and is therefore invisible. And instructors are numerous, so attrition is not felt.

## What to Build
Take the overhead and give back the teaching. Automate the recurring business operations — pricing, promotions, launch sequences, refunds, tax and payout handling — which is the core and is where the week actually goes. Give the instructor teaching feedback for the first time: where learners stall, what they ask, what they fail, which explanations do not land, which is the thing they most want and have never had. Report what their course achieved rather than what it sold, connecting to the measurement work. Warn about platform changes in advance with an explanation, as learning about a ranking change from a forum is a specific and repeated indignity. Handle the support burden, connecting to the Q&A work, since it is unbounded and open-ended. Provide instructional design support rather than assuming expertise transfers, because subject knowledge and teaching skill are different and nobody helps. Prompt maintenance with specifics rather than generic reminders. Show the economics plainly — what each course earns net of platform share, discounting and refunds — since many instructors do not know. Support the instructor who wants to teach rather than to build a business, as that is most of them and the current model selects against them. And measure instructor retention, which is the leading indicator of catalogue quality and nobody tracks it as one.

## Target Customer
Creator and instructor leadership, instructors, learners who benefit from better teaching, and creator platform vendors.

## Impact If Built
A marketplace that sees itself as a channel does not build the operating system for its suppliers, so the expert became a small business operator. Removing the recurring overhead and supplying teaching feedback returns the week to the thing the learner is paying for.
