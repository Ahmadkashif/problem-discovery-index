# Stock & Archival Footage Licensing

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/film-distributors|Film Distributors]]
**Category:** Highly Automatable
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to put a clearable clip of exactly the shot a producer asked for in front of them within hours, and whoever answers the research request first with footage that clears takes the licence.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$200M a year in US licensing of archival and stock footage by libraries that own their collections (estimate; all 512199 employer-firm revenue, including labs and booking agencies, was $426M in 2022)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~5% of US independent distribution and library receipts (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — collections are increasingly digitised; description is sparse keywords typed by staff, and research is done by eye
**Target Buyer:** Archive director or head of licensing at a footage library; head of archival research at a documentary producer
**Automation Potential:** High for search, logging and quoting; low for rights judgement on complex clips

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
512199 names stock-footage film libraries explicitly. These are archives that own historical film, newsreel and news footage and license it by the second, by use, territory and term. Producers Library lists $49 a second for regular documentary use with a $539 minimum, and CONUS starts news footage at $40 a second with a 30-second minimum. Clients are documentary, news and advertising producers working to edit deadlines. This is distinct from [[industries/stock-media-marketplaces|Stock Media Marketplaces]], which resell contributors' newly shot stock. Here the library owns a finite historical collection, its research desk is the sales force, and generative video threatens generic B-roll but not authentic historical footage.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Digital asset management systems with keyword metadata, online preview catalogues, rate cards, and researchers' knowledge of their collection. The gaps: description at reel or story level rather than shot level; no search by visual content or period cues; rights restrictions recorded inconsistently.
