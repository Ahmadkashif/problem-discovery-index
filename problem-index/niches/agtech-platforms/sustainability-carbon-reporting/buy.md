# Reporting Frameworks and Model Implementations Already Published

**Niche:** [[niches/agtech-platforms/sustainability-carbon-reporting/profile|Sustainability & Carbon Reporting]]
**Industry:** [[industries/agtech-platforms|Agtech Platforms]]
**Type:** Buy & Customize (Vertical Adaptation)
**One-liner:** The biogeochemical models that estimate soil carbon and emissions from agricultural practice are published and in several cases open, and each programme runs its own implementation that the grower being paid on it cannot inspect.
**Tags:** #numerical-methods #bayesian-inference #confidence-intervals #monte-carlo-methods #evaluation-metrics #hypothesis-testing #compliance #data-integration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in agricultural sustainability reporting is fighting to let one set of farm records satisfy every programme that wants evidence — and whoever makes a grower's data reusable across programmes takes the enrolment.

## The Problem
A grower's carbon payment depends on a modelled estimate of how much carbon their practice change sequestered. The model is the programme's, the inputs are the grower's data, and the output is a number the grower receives and cannot check. Different programmes using different models on the same field produce different numbers. The models themselves are mostly derived from published science and in several cases are open implementations, which means the grower's inability to verify is a matter of packaging rather than of secrecy.

## What Already Exists
Process-based biogeochemical models for soil carbon and greenhouse gas emissions — DayCent, DNDC and their relatives — are published, well documented and in several cases available as open implementations. Greenhouse gas accounting frameworks and agricultural protocols are public. Uncertainty quantification methods for model outputs are standard. Remote sensing for practice verification, particularly tillage and cover crop detection, is a developed research area with public methods. The scientific and methodological content is largely in the open literature.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to make the estimate inspectable and comparable. It requires: (1) running the published models on the grower's own record so the grower can see what a programme's number is derived from and how sensitive it is to the inputs, which is transparency rather than dispute; (2) uncertainty stated explicitly, since these estimates carry substantial uncertainty that programme communications frequently omit and that a grower deciding on a practice change has a right to know; (3) comparison across methodologies for the same field, which shows where the programmes differ and is information the grower needs before enrolling; (4) remote sensing verification of practice as a complement to attestation, since practice verification is the weakest link in these programmes and satellite detection of tillage and cover is both available and unused at scale; and (5) a strict distinction between what is measured, what is modelled and what is attested, carried through every output — because conflating them is the credibility problem the whole category has.

## Target Customer
Farm management platforms, programme operators seeking credibility, verification bodies, and the growers whose payments depend on numbers they cannot currently examine.

## Impact If Solved
The science is public and the implementations largely exist, so bringing model transparency to the grower is a packaging exercise with substantial effect on a category whose main weakness is credibility. Stating uncertainty honestly is the uncomfortable part and is what would distinguish a serious offering from the current practice of quoting point estimates for quantities that are not known to that precision.
