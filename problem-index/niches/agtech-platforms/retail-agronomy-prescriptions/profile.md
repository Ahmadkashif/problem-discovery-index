# Retail Agronomy — Prescriptions With a Conflict Attached

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/agtech-platforms|Agtech Platforms]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in retail agronomy software is fighting to make a recommendation credible when the adviser's employer sells the product — and whoever can evidence that a prescription paid for the grower takes the relationship.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$600M US spend across retail and co-op agronomy platforms and prescription tools
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~15% of agtech revenue
**Digital Adoption:** Medium-High — prescription writing and recordkeeping are systematised
**Target Buyer:** Agronomy leaders at co-ops and input retailers; the growers on the other side of the advice
**Automation Potential:** High — prescription outcome measurement is achievable and is the capability that resolves the credibility problem

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
The dominant model for agronomic advice in American row crop agriculture is that it is free and the adviser's employer sells the inputs. That is not a scandal — the agronomists are generally competent and conscientious, the model is longstanding, and growers understand it — but it is a structural fact that shapes everything. A grower discounts a recommendation to some degree because of where it comes from. An agronomist's own confidence in a recommendation cannot be distinguished, from the outside, from their employer's margin on the product. And the retailer, which invests heavily in agronomic capability as a differentiator, cannot demonstrate that the advice is worth anything, because nobody measures whether prescriptions paid. That is the contested problem, and it is a measurement and transparency problem before it is an agronomic one — which is why it is a different contest from independent consulting.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Agrian, Agworld, Proagrica and the retailer-specific platforms handle prescription writing, compliance documentation and grower recordkeeping. Variable rate prescription generation from soil sampling and imagery is standard and competent. Integration with application equipment works. The gaps: no prescription is evaluated against what it delivered, so a retailer's agronomic value proposition rests entirely on relationship and reputation; soil sampling, which underlies most prescriptions, is performed on grids or zones whose adequacy nobody tests; and the economic case for a prescription — the expected return against the input cost, on this grower's ground — is presented as agronomic reasoning rather than as a number with an interval, which is the form in which it could be checked.
