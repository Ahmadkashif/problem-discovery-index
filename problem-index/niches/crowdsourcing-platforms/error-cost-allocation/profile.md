# Error Cost Allocation

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/crowdsourcing-platforms|Crowdsourcing Platforms]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Who pays when the task was broken, the instructions were ambiguous or the time estimate was wrong.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$324M — 40% of the task design and quality niche
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~11% of the US microtask and research-participant market
**Digital Adoption:** None — the rule has not changed
**Target Buyer:** Platform leadership; requesters' ethics boards and procurement; worker organisations
**Automation Potential:** Very low — this is a platform rule, not a computation

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

The other half detects the requester's error. This half decides who bears it, and today the answer is entirely the worker.

Unpaid time on a task that turned out to be broken. Rejection for answering an ambiguous item the way a minority did. Approval-rate damage from a requester who rejects indiscriminately. Qualification tests completed for work that never materialised. A time estimate of five minutes on work that takes fifteen. In every one of these the requester's mistake is charged to the person who did the work.

Three things make this a distinct market. It is a rule, not a technology — the platform writes it and could write it differently tomorrow. It runs against the paying customer, whose spend funds the platform and who can post the same batch on a competitor with a more permissive rule. And where it has shifted at all, it has shifted under external pressure — university ethics review boards imposing pay floors and rejection standards on academic requesters — rather than through any platform's product decision.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Rejection rights granted to requesters with few constraints. Approval rate as a qualification gate with no expiry or appeal on most platforms. Bonus mechanisms allowing voluntary additional payment, used rarely. Some platforms restrict rejection or require a reason; the academic-focused platforms are furthest along, under ethics-board pressure.

The gaps are an appeal path, a standard for what may be rejected, compensation for time lost to broken tasks, and any consequence for a requester whose rejection rate is far outside the norm.
