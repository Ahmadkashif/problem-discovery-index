# Pricing & Reference Values

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/online-marketplaces|Online Marketplaces]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to tell a seller what their one-of-a-kind item is actually worth and a buyer whether a price is fair — and whoever does that takes the transaction, because without a reference both sides are guessing and most guesses end in no sale.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.6B US
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~8% of category revenue
**Digital Adoption:** Very Low — better tools exist outside the platform than inside it
**Target Buyer:** Sellers, and the operators losing them to mispricing
**Automation Potential:** Very High — the sold history is the model's training data

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
For a catalogued product there is a market price and everyone can see it. For a one-of-a-kind item there is no reference, and the seller sets a number based on what they paid, what they hope, or what a superficially similar listing is asking — which is itself somebody else's guess, frequently unsold. Overpriced items sit and expire; underpriced items sell instantly and the seller feels cheated. Both outcomes lose a seller. The marketplace holds the complete history of what actually sold, at what price, with what attributes, after how long — which is exactly the training data for a valuation model — and the pricing tools sellers actually use are frequently third-party products built on scraped data from the platform itself.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Suggested price ranges where offered, comparable listing displays that show asking prices rather than sold prices, and third-party pricing tools. The gaps: comparables are drawn from active listings rather than completed sales, which anchors on unsold optimism; no valuation for genuinely unique items; no expression of the price-to-time-to-sale trade-off; no guidance as a listing ages; and no buyer-side signal about whether a price is reasonable.
