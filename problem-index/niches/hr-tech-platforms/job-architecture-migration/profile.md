# Job Architecture & Migration

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/hr-tech-platforms|HR Tech Platforms]]
**Category:** Highly Automatable
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in HCM implementation is fighting to infer an employer's job architecture and employment history from the data rather than rebuilding it from spreadsheets — and whoever shortens time-to-configured most takes the implementation.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.4B US spend on HCM implementation, job architecture design and data conversion
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~6% of HR technology revenue, and a far larger share of implementation cost and timeline
**Digital Adoption:** Low — the work is done with spreadsheets and workshops
**Target Buyer:** VPs of HR Technology, implementation partners, and the consultants who perform the work
**Automation Potential:** Very High — job architecture is inferable from titles, pay, reporting structure and movement history

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
An HCM implementation begins with a structure the employer does not have in usable form. Job architecture — the families, levels, titles and their relationships — exists as an accumulation of job titles invented by hiring managers over a decade, with pay grades that were rationalised twice and abandoned, and a level framework that half the organisation uses. Employment history exists in a legacy system whose data model nobody documented, plus spreadsheets from two acquisitions. The implementation consultant's job is to reconstruct both, through workshops with HR leaders who describe the architecture they wish they had, and through data conversion performed by hand. It is the longest and most expensive part of an HCM programme, it is done from scratch at every employer, and the evidence needed to infer most of it — what people are actually called, what they are actually paid, who they actually report to, and how they have actually moved — is sitting in the data being converted.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Implementation partners have methodologies and accelerators; job architecture consultancies sell frameworks; the HCM vendors provide conversion tooling and templates. Job classification libraries and market data providers supply reference structures. The gaps: nothing infers the architecture that exists from the data, so every engagement starts with a blank framework and a workshop; conversion validation is record counts and totals rather than semantic fidelity, exactly as in the insurance core system case; title normalisation — mapping two thousand distinct titles to a coherent set — is done by hand and is the single largest data task in most programmes; and the architecture designed in the workshop frequently does not match what the organisation does, so it decays as soon as hiring managers resume inventing titles.
