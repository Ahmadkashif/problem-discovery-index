# Revenue From Transactions, Not From Outcomes

**Niche:** [[niches/data-marketplace-brokers/marketplace-outcome-intelligence/profile|Marketplace Outcome Intelligence]]
**Industry:** [[industries/data-marketplace-brokers|Data Marketplace Brokers]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Marketplaces earn a percentage when a transaction closes and nothing when it succeeds, which makes a failed purchase indistinguishable from a good one in every number the business runs on.
**Tags:** #revenue-impact #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #survival-analysis #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #quick-win #compliance
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to turn what the marketplace already sees — evaluations, rejections, churn, field usage — into the quality signal the market lacks, and whoever publishes it defines how datasets are judged.

## The Problem
A marketplace's reported numbers are transaction count and gross value. A purchase that was abandoned after six weeks because the data was unusable contributes exactly as much to both as one that a buyer has renewed for four years. Every incentive in the business — listing growth, conversion optimisation, sales compensation — is oriented toward closing transactions, and none toward whether they worked. The market's reputation problem and the marketplace's incentive structure are the same fact stated twice, and no participant has changed the structure because the first to do so reports worse numbers.

## Why It's Still Broken
Transaction-based fees are simple to administer and standard across marketplaces. Outcome-based pricing requires defining and measuring success, which the marketplace has avoided building. Investors evaluate gross value, so reporting a quality-adjusted figure invites questions. And the buyers who suffer are diffuse while the suppliers who benefit are concentrated and vocal.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Make outcomes count in the numbers the business runs on. Measure retained transaction value — the share of transactions still live after a year — and report it alongside gross value, which is computable from existing billing data and immediately reframes what the business is optimising. Tie some fee to renewal rather than to initial close, so the marketplace's revenue and the buyer's outcome point the same way, which is the structural fix and can be phased in on new listings. Compensate sales and account teams on retained rather than closed value, since that is where behaviour actually changes. Track and publish transaction failure rates, which is uncomfortable and is the number that would drive the whole market's improvement. Offer evaluation-backed purchases with a remedy when delivery does not match the listing, which prices the risk the buyer currently absorbs entirely. Rank listings by retained value, which turns the signal into an incentive suppliers respond to. Report to providers how their listings retain, since many would improve if they knew. And say plainly that a catalogue optimised for listing count is not the same as a market optimised for successful purchases, because conflating the two is what has allowed the current structure to persist.

## Who Feels the Pain
Buyers whose failed purchases count as marketplace successes; good providers competing against listings that convert well and retain badly; and the marketplaces whose growth figures overstate the value they create.

## Impact If Fixed
Retained transaction value is computable from billing data already held and immediately reframes what the business optimises. Tying fees to renewal aligns the marketplace with the buyer, and it can be phased in on new listings without repricing the existing book.
