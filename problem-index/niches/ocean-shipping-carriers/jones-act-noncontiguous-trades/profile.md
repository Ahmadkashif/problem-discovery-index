# Jones Act Noncontiguous Liner Trades

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/ocean-shipping-carriers|Ocean Shipping Carriers]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to deliver an island's weekly freight on the day the island plans around, and whoever lands the most containers on the scheduled arrival day takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$250M spend on scheduling, operations, forecasting and customer-facing systems by carriers in the Hawaii, Alaska, Puerto Rico and Guam trades (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** a small share of this industry's tooling spend (estimate); the trades' own receipts sit in NAICS 483113, outside the $9.9B 483111 figure, so no receipts ratio is given
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — liner systems are modern; arrival forecasts for customers are not
**Target Buyer:** VP Operations / Head of Customer Service at a noncontiguous carrier
**Automation Potential:** Medium — arrival forecasting and customer notice automate; the network is small and fixed

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Island economies run on a weekly ship. Grocery chains, big-box retailers and military exchanges in Honolulu, Anchorage and San Juan plan shelves and staffing around a fixed arrival day, and a late ship empties shelves. A handful of US-built carriers serve each trade — Matson and Pasha in Hawaii, TOTE and Matson in Alaska, Crowley and TOTE in Puerto Rico (recalled), with barge services such as Trailer Bridge left to the queued tug-and-barge-operators industry — with fleets that are expensive and slow to replace. Rate competition is muted by the small number of players, so customers choose on reliability. Hurricane and winter-storm seasons are the hard test.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Carrier liner systems, AIS-based tracking, port operations systems. The gap is a customer-grade arrival forecast and early warning when weather or a port problem will move the arrival day.
