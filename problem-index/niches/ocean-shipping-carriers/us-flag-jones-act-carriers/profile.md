# US-Flag & Jones Act Carriers

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/ocean-shipping-carriers|Ocean Shipping Carriers]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** *Not terminal as stated.* The honest partial sentence is that every serious competitor is fighting to keep US-built or US-flag, US-crewed ships earning despite costs well above foreign tonnage, but the noncontiguous domestic trades are won by landing cargo on the day an island plans around, and the international preference trades by having an eligible ship inside each government tender's loading window. Decomposed into contested sub-niches below.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$400M spend on software, data and outsourced services by US-flag deep-sea operators (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~4% of the spend sized across this industry's niches (estimate); the 483111 receipts of $9.9B (Census SUSB 2022) cover the international half only, since the noncontiguous domestic trades report under 483113
**Digital Adoption:** Low to Medium — the larger operators run modern liner systems; crewing, citizenship and programme compliance run on binders and spreadsheets
**Target Buyer:** COO / VP Operations at a US-flag operator
**Automation Potential:** Medium — schedule forecasting, programme paperwork and crew credentials automate; government relationships do not

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
A small fleet of US-flag deep-sea ships lives under rules no foreign carrier faces. Domestic trades between US ports, including Hawaii, Alaska, Puerto Rico and Guam, are reserved by the Jones Act for US-built, US-owned, US-crewed ships (recalled). Formally those noncontiguous liner trades fall in NAICS 483113. The queue assigns self-propelled deep-sea Jones Act liners in that code to this industry and leaves towboats and barges to the queued tug-and-barge-operators industry. US-flag ships in international trade live on cargo preference — DoD and other government-impelled cargo reserved for US-flag carriers where available at fair and reasonable rates (recalled) — and on the Maritime Security Program, which pays $6.5M per enrolled ship in fiscal 2025–26 under 46 U.S.C. 53106 for a fleet of about 60 ships (search summary). Software built for global lines ignores these rules, and the operators are too few to attract vendors.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Liner and fleet systems shared with foreign carriers (several US-flag operators are subsidiaries of Maersk, CMA CGM and others), USCG credentialing and documentation systems, MARAD programme paperwork, and USTRANSCOM and USDA tender portals. The gaps differ by trade, which is why the niche splits.
