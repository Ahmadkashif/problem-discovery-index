## Why These Niches

Travel tournament teams and rec league operators represent the two dominant organizational models in youth sports — high-commitment, competitive, family-funded travel teams vs. community-based, volunteer-run recreational leagues — with fundamentally different operational needs and price sensitivities. Volunteer coach networks and special needs adaptive leagues serve populations systematically underserved by youth sports technology: volunteers who receive zero tooling or training support, and families of children with disabilities who find that every registration form, scheduling system, and communication tool assumes able-bodied, neurotypical participants. Multi-sport complexes and fundraising operations represent the digitization frontier: complexes managing 10-20 fields/courts with overlapping schedules and tournaments using whiteboards and spreadsheets, and fundraising operations running on cash envelopes and handwritten spreadsheets despite handling $20K-100K per team per year. Field scheduling and registration/draft systems are the two highest-volume, most rule-constrained administrative workflows in youth sports — consuming hundreds of volunteer hours annually that could be automated.

## Why These Pockets

No qualifiers, and the only pocket above 50 is the screening bureau that clears every adult who will be alone with a child — already indexed under HR consultants, and the same pocket that appeared in tutoring centres for the same reason.

The structural finding is that this industry's two most important questions both have answers sitting in commercial systems and neither is asked.

The first is who drops out of sport, when, and why. It is the central policy and philanthropic question in youth athletics, and it is currently answered by household surveys of a few thousand families. The registration platforms hold the actual longitudinal record — millions of children, by sport, by year, with fees paid and years played — and publish registration volume. Below them, the recruiting platforms hold millions of athletes joined to where they actually ended up, which would support an honest probability estimate for any given family, and sell exposure services on a promise whose odds they alone can compute.

The second is what actually hurts children. The evidence is split four ways and never joined. Injury surveillance programmes hold the rare exposure-adjusted incidence data, collected by volunteer athletic trainers on grant funding, covering mostly school programmes rather than the club system where most competitive play now happens. Sports medicine providers see injuries at the moment of occurrence with mechanism attached — exactly what surveillance is trying to reconstruct from reports — behind a health privacy wall. Equipment certification bodies hold decades of laboratory impact results across product generations and have never joined them to field injury data. And the governing bodies change playing rules to reduce injury with no mechanism to measure whether the change worked.

The abuse layer is the sharpest version of the same problem. The insurers hold the only cross-organisation record of which supervision policies and screening practices actually preceded harm, and use it to price a hardening line. The centralised safety body holds the only record of substantiated abuse in organised sport and has jurisdiction over the governing body system alone, not the wider club and recreational sector where most children play.
