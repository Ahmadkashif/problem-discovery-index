# Fix: The Count Rises and the Problem Does Not Shrink

**Niche:** Enforcement Effectiveness Measurement
**Industry:** [[industries/brand-protection-firms|Brand Protection Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Every party in the chain reports takedowns upward, nobody measures counterfeit sales, and the number that justifies the spend is consistent with the problem getting worse.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #descriptive-statistics #hypothesis-testing #revenue-impact #compliance
**Contested on:** Whether anyone can show that enforcement reduced counterfeit sales, or only that it produced takedowns.

## The Problem

The vendor reports four thousand takedowns to the brand protection manager. The manager reports four thousand takedowns to their director. The director reports the programme's activity to the executive team. At each step the number is passed upward as evidence that the programme is working.

Nobody in the chain has measured whether counterfeit sales fell. The vendor does not, because they are paid on notices. The manager does not, because they have no access to the measurement and no mandate to produce it. And the executive receives a large number that sounds like progress.

The number is genuinely consistent with several very different realities. The counterfeit problem could be growing, with detection growing alongside it. Operators could be cycling accounts faster, producing more removals from the same underlying supply. Detection could have improved while the population is unchanged. Or the problem could be shrinking. Nothing in the report distinguishes them.

What makes this structural rather than dishonest is that no party has an incentive to look. The vendor's revenue depends on the count. The manager's programme is justified by it. And the executive has no basis to ask for anything else because nothing else is on offer anywhere in the market.

## Why It's Still Broken

**Everyone in the chain is served by the count.** The vendor, the manager and the reporting line all benefit from a number that rises, and the only party harmed is the brand's understanding of its own problem.

**No alternative is available to buy.** A brand wanting outcome measurement cannot procure it, because no vendor offers it, so the count is not merely the preferred option but the only one.

**The measurement sits across functions.** Warranty, customer service, legal and sales hold the brand-side signals. The brand protection manager may have access to none of them.

**Prevalence sampling is nobody's job.** It is straightforward and costs a little money and belongs to neither the vendor nor the manager.

**Attribution difficulty is used as a reason not to start.** Because a prevalence change cannot be attributed perfectly to enforcement, the conclusion drawn is that measuring is pointless — rather than that a trend is informative even when attribution is partial.

**Nobody is measured on counterfeit sales.** It is not a metric anyone in the chain owns, which is why nobody produces it.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Sample prevalence, once, and see.** One category, one platform, a random sample assessed for infringement. Repeat quarterly. This is a modest piece of work that would produce the first trend line anyone in this chain has seen, and it does not require the vendor's cooperation.

**Join the brand's own signals.** Warranty claims on units that turn out not to be genuine, customer complaints about fakes, returns of non-genuine goods, seizure volumes. All exist inside the brand, in other functions, and none is joined to enforcement.

**Measure consumer exposure.** For the brand's highest-intent searches on each major platform, what fraction of the first page is infringing. Cheap, directly meaningful, and closer to the harm than any population count.

**Ask the vendor for re-emergence data.** What proportion of actioned sellers reappeared within thirty days. Vendors who can answer will; those who cannot have told you something about what they track.

**Report the count as activity and label it as such.** Takedowns are an operational metric and belong in an operational report. The executive summary should carry the outcome proxies, however imperfect, and say plainly what the count does and does not establish.

**Give someone the outcome question.** A named person responsible for answering whether counterfeit sales are falling, with access to the functions holding the signals. Currently the question has no owner, which is why it has no answer.

**Ask for it at renewal.** A brand requiring an outcome measure as a condition of renewal would change what vendors build, and it is a question any brand can ask at the next contract.

## Who Feels the Pain

The brand, spending substantially on a programme whose effect is unmeasured and unmeasurable from what it receives.

The brand protection manager, holding a number they suspect does not mean what everyone above them takes it to mean, with nothing better to offer.

Consumers, who buy counterfeits from operations that a rising takedown count did not disrupt.

And the vendors doing genuinely effective work, who are indistinguishable from those producing volume, because volume is the only comparable metric.

## Impact If Fixed

A single quarterly prevalence sample, run by the brand, produces the trend line this chain has never had — and requires no vendor to agree to anything.

Joining the brand's own warranty, complaint and seizure signals costs a conversation between internal functions and bounds the real problem from a direction nobody currently looks.

And asking for re-emergence data at renewal would change what vendors measure, because a question asked by enough brands is what determines what the industry builds.
