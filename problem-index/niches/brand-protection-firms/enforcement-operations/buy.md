**Contested on:** Whether the enforcement lever is chosen because it works, or because it is the easiest one available.

## The Problem

Disrupting a criminal enterprise rather than prosecuting its individual acts is a well-developed discipline, and financial crime enforcement arrived at it the hard way.

The lessons are consistent. Targeting individual transactions achieves nothing because the enterprise replaces them. Targeting the money — payment rails, correspondent relationships, cash-out mechanisms — is far more disruptive than targeting the activity. Network analysis identifies the enterprise behind the accounts. Coordinated simultaneous action prevents the network re-forming around the removed node. And cooperation between the institutions that touch the money is what makes any of it work.

Brand protection removes listings, which is the transaction-level intervention that financial crime abandoned. The money — payment processing, the mechanism by which a counterfeit operation actually collects revenue — is the lever least used. Network analysis is not performed. Action is sequential rather than coordinated. And the parties who touch the money are barely involved.

## What Already Exists

Financial crime: anti-money laundering programmes, suspicious activity reporting, network analysis over transaction and entity graphs, correspondent banking controls, and the coordinated action structures between institutions and authorities.

Payment ecosystem controls: card scheme rules on illegal and brand-infringing merchants, acquirer obligations, merchant monitoring programmes, and the mechanisms by which a merchant account is terminated.

Sanctions and designation regimes: the practice of targeting an entity rather than its transactions, with the network mapping that supports it.

Platform integrity: coordinated takedowns of abuse networks by large platforms, executed simultaneously across surfaces, which is the closest existing model.

Brand protection: per-listing notices, some payment processor reporting, and customs recordation.

## The Customization Gap

**The money lever is available and underused.** Card scheme rules prohibit merchants selling counterfeit goods, and acquirers have obligations. Brand protection reports to processors occasionally and unsystematically, where this is the intervention that removes the operator's revenue.

**No entity graph exists.** Financial crime analysis is built over a network of entities and relationships. Brand protection has listings. Constructing the operator graph is the prerequisite for everything financial crime does.

**Action is sequential and should be simultaneous.** Removing an operator from one surface while they continue on four teaches them where the monitoring is. Coordinated simultaneous action is standard in network disruption and rare here.

**Institutional cooperation is absent.** Financial crime works because banks, processors and authorities share structured information. Brand protection firms compete, brands do not share, and the payment ecosystem is barely engaged.

**Evidence standards for the money lever are higher.** A processor terminating a merchant needs more than a visual match, which is why the lever is hard to use and why better determination is a prerequisite.

**Disruption measurement exists there and not here.** Financial crime measures whether an enterprise was disrupted, however imperfectly. Brand protection measures notices filed.

## Target Customer

Payment processors and acquirers, who have rule-based obligations regarding counterfeit merchants and limited systematic intelligence about which merchants are counterfeit operations.

Brand protection firms, adopting network-first disruption thinking, for whom the payment lever is the most consequential underused channel.

Brand coalitions, since the institutional cooperation that makes financial crime enforcement work has no equivalent here and would require brands to share operator intelligence they currently do not.

## Impact If Solved

An enforcement discipline that abandoned transaction-level intervention decades ago offers a direct template to an industry still doing it.

The payment lever is the one that touches the operator's economics and is the least used, largely because the evidence and the relationships to use it have not been built.

And coordinated simultaneous action across surfaces, standard in network disruption, would replace sequential removal that currently functions as reconnaissance for the operator.
