# The Brand's Own Manager

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/brand-protection-firms|Brand Protection Firms]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Whether the person accountable for the brand protection programme can say what it achieved, or only what it did.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$280M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~7%
**Digital Adoption:** Low — a monthly dashboard to interpret
**Target Buyer:** Brand protection managers, IP counsel, brand leadership
**Automation Potential:** Moderate — the reporting automates, the accountability does not

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

Inside the brand there is usually one person, sometimes two, who own the programme. They are frequently in legal or IP, sometimes in commercial, and they are the point at which the vendor's activity meets the organisation's expectations.

Their position is difficult in a specific way. They receive a monthly report of four thousand takedowns and must present it upward as evidence the programme is working, knowing it does not establish that. They field the internal consequences when an authorised reseller or a repair partner is suspended by the vendor they manage. They are asked by commercial colleagues whether counterfeits are actually falling and have no answer. And at budget time they must justify a substantial spend with an activity number.

They also sit across functions they do not control. The evidence that would establish outcome — warranty claims, customer complaints, seizure data, sales analysis — is held by customer service, legal, supply chain and finance. The authorised seller list that would prevent wrongful actions is maintained by commercial. And the vendor relationship is theirs while the consequences land across the business.

This is an underserved audience because every tool in the category is built for the vendor's workflow and none is built for the person accountable for the programme's result.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Vendor dashboards reporting takedown counts by platform and category. Periodic business reviews with the vendor. Internal reporting templates. Access to legal and commercial colleagues for specific questions. Test purchase programmes where budget permits.

The gaps are about answering the question they are actually asked. Nothing reports outcome, so the upward presentation is activity. Nothing joins the brand's own signals — warranty, complaints, seizures — to the programme. Vendor performance cannot be compared on anything but volume and price. The authorised seller list they depend on is maintained elsewhere and is frequently stale. Wrongful actions against channel partners arrive as internal complaints rather than as vendor-reported errors. And nothing helps them make the budget case beyond the count.
