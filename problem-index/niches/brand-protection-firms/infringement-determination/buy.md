## The Problem

Notice-based enforcement against online content is not new, and copyright went through this first.

Copyright takedown has statutory structure. A notice must contain specified elements including a good-faith belief statement made under penalty of perjury. The affected party has a counter-notice right with a defined process and a restoration timeline. There are penalties, at least in principle, for knowing misrepresentation. Large platforms publish transparency reports showing notice volumes and outcomes. And two decades of litigation and research have documented the over-enforcement problem in detail, including the systematic targeting of fair use.

Trademark enforcement on marketplaces operates largely outside that structure. Notice requirements are set by each platform rather than by statute. Counter-notice equivalents vary and are frequently weak. There is no meaningful penalty for a wrong notice. Transparency reporting on trademark notices is thinner than on copyright. And the same over-enforcement dynamic that copyright documented is operating with less scrutiny.

The lessons are available and the apparatus is not.

## What Already Exists

Copyright notice regimes: the statutory notice-and-counter-notice structure, the good-faith belief requirement, misrepresentation liability, and the substantial case law on its limits.

Transparency reporting: platform reports on notice volumes, actioned rates and counter-notices, and the aggregated research built on them.

Over-enforcement research: a well-developed body of study documenting notice error rates, fair-use targeting and the chilling effect on legitimate speech and commerce.

Platform trademark processes: marketplace IP programmes with their own notice forms, seller appeals of varying quality, and repeat-infringer policies.

Emerging regulation: platform accountability regimes in several jurisdictions imposing notice-and-action requirements, redress rights and reporting obligations — which is the direction of travel.

## The Customization Gap

**The lawful defences differ and are harder.** Copyright has fair use and fair dealing; trademark has exhaustion, nominative use, repair and comparative advertising. The trademark defences are arguably more commercially common on marketplaces — resale and repair are enormous categories — and are less well handled.

**No good-faith statement with consequences.** Copyright notices carry a penalty-of-perjury element. Trademark notices on most platforms carry an assertion with no meaningful consequence for being wrong, which removes the only external discipline on notice accuracy.

**Counter-notice is weak or absent.** Copyright's restoration mechanism has a defined timeline. Marketplace seller appeals vary enormously and frequently favour the rights holder, and a suspended seller loses income while appealing.

**Transparency reporting is thin.** Copyright notice data is published and studied. Trademark notice volumes, actioned rates and reversal rates are largely unpublished, which is why the error rate is unknown.

**No research base exists.** Copyright over-enforcement is documented extensively. The equivalent study of trademark enforcement on marketplaces is comparatively sparse, which means the harm is asserted from anecdote rather than measured.

**Regulation is arriving and is unanticipated.** Platform accountability regimes imposing redress and reporting obligations will apply here, and the industry has not prepared for notice accuracy becoming a reportable metric.

## Target Customer

Platforms, who could adopt copyright-style structure for trademark notices — a good-faith statement with consequence, a defined counter-notice path, and published notice statistics — and who face regulatory pressure in exactly that direction.

Regulators, for whom the copyright precedent provides a well-studied template and a documented set of failure modes to avoid.

Brand protection firms, for whom anticipating notice-accuracy reporting is considerably cheaper than being subjected to it — and for whom a demonstrable precision record would become a commercial asset.

## Impact If Solved

Two decades of learning about notice-based enforcement, including its documented over-enforcement failure, applies to a regime operating with less structure and less scrutiny.

A good-faith statement with actual consequence is the single structural change that would introduce a cost for wrong notices, which is the missing incentive underneath every problem in this niche.

And published notice and reversal statistics would produce the error rate this industry does not have, which is the number that would let brands choose firms on accuracy rather than on volume.
