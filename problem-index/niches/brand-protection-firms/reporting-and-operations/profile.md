# Reporting & Account Operations

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/brand-protection-firms|Brand Protection Firms]]
**Category:** Highly Automatable
**Contested on:** Whether the monthly report answers the client's question or restates the count the contract was priced on.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$80M

**Target Buyer:** Firm operations, customer success, account management
**Automation Potential:** Very high — assembly and delivery are mechanical

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

Around the detection and enforcement work sits the account layer: monthly reporting, quarterly business reviews, rights recordation management across platforms, scope and category configuration, escalation handling, renewal preparation and billing.

Most of it is mechanical. The monthly report is assembled from the same data every time. Recordation of trademarks with each platform's rights programme is administrative and must be maintained across dozens of platforms and jurisdictions. Scope configuration is set at onboarding and rarely revisited. And renewal is prepared by pulling together the year's numbers.

The contest, such as it is, is whether the reporting says anything. The report is the client's entire window into the programme, it is the artefact that justifies the spend, and it is built around the metric the vendor is paid on. A report that answered the client's actual question would be a different product and would require the measurement the industry does not have.

It is the smallest niche in the industry and the one where the gap between what is produced and what is needed is most obvious.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Client dashboards with takedown counts by platform, category and time. Monthly report generation. Quarterly business review decks. Rights recordation tracking. Escalation logging. Billing against contracted volumes or subscriptions.

The gaps are about content rather than mechanics. The report contains activity and no outcome, so the client cannot answer the question they are asked. Channel-partner errors are absent, so the vendor's most damaging failures do not appear in their own reporting. Re-emergence is not shown, so repeated action against the same operator looks like productivity. Recordation lapses are discovered when a notice is rejected rather than tracked proactively. And scope is not revisited against where the problem has moved, so monitoring persists on surfaces the operators have left.
