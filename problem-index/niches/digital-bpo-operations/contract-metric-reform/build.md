# Build: An Outcome-Based Commercial Model That Both Sides Can Audit

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-bpo-operations/contract-metric-reform/profile|Contractual Metric Reform]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-bpo-operations|Digital BPO Operations]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Construct a contract priced on resolved issues rather than handled contacts, with attribution for what the vendor does not control and an audit basis both parties accept.
**Tags:** #causal-inference #hypothesis-testing #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #compliance #descriptive-statistics #revenue-impact #survival-analysis
**Contested on:** Whether a vendor can be paid for an outcome it only partly controls.

## The Problem

The commercial model is the reason the metrics do not change. A contract priced per contact with handle time targets makes speed the vendor's economic interest, and every quality initiative sits on top of that as a constraint rather than an objective.

Moving to outcomes is obviously right and genuinely hard. Resolution depends on the client's product, its policies, its knowledge base, its systems and the contact types it routes — a vendor cannot resolve an issue the client's policy forbids resolving, and it cannot answer from a knowledge base the client maintains badly. A vendor priced purely on resolution would be underwriting the client's own decisions.

So the industry stays on operational metrics, which are fully within the vendor's control, precisely measurable, and measure the wrong thing.

## Why Nobody Has Built This

The first-mover dynamic is decisive. Procurement evaluates bids on comparable unit prices. A vendor proposing an outcome-based structure is asking a procurement function to evaluate something its scorecard cannot score, against competitors offering a lower number in the cell the scorecard has.

Attribution is the real technical obstacle inside a commercial one. Nobody has built a defensible way to separate the vendor's contribution to resolution from the client's, and without it neither party will sign — the client fears paying for the vendor's luck, the vendor fears being penalised for the client's product.

And both sides' internal incentives favour the status quo: the vendor's operations leadership is measured on the metrics in the contract, and the client's vendor manager is measured on cost per contact.

## What to Build

An attribution model and a contract structure built on it.

**Decompose resolution into what each party controls.** Contact types where resolution is fully within the vendor's authority. Types where it depends on a client policy. Types where the answer must come from the client's knowledge base. Types requiring a system the client operates. This taxonomy, agreed up front per contact type, is the foundation — and producing it is a joint exercise that has value even if no contract changes.

**Price the controllable portion on outcome and the rest on effort.** A blended structure where vendor-controllable contact types carry a resolution-based rate and the remainder stays on a handled basis is far more achievable than a wholesale change and captures most of the incentive benefit.

**Build the attribution evidence.** Where a contact was not resolved, the reason is usually determinable from the transcript: the policy did not allow it, the knowledge base had no answer, the system was unavailable, the agent did not identify the issue, the agent identified it and handled it poorly. Classifying unresolved contacts by cause, automatically and at full coverage, is what makes the commercial split auditable. It also gives the client something valuable — a ranked list of their own policies and knowledge gaps costing them resolution.

**Define the audit basis jointly.** Both parties must be able to verify the resolution measure independently, which means an agreed rubric, an agreed repeat-contact definition, access to the evidence, and a dispute mechanism. Without this the contract is unsignable.

**Model the vendor's exposure before proposing it.** Simulate revenue under the proposed structure against historical performance, including the variance introduced by client-side factors. A vendor that has quantified its downside can negotiate the floor and the collar; one that has not will not propose it at all.

**Start as a gainshare overlay.** Keep the existing per-contact base and add a resolution-based bonus and penalty band. This is signable, it aligns the incentive at the margin, and it produces the operating history that makes a fuller restructuring possible later.

## Target Customer

BPO commercial leadership at vendors with a resolution measurement already working, for whom this is the only durable differentiation available in a price-competitive market. Also client-side vendor management dissatisfied with cost-per-contact outcomes, who are the party that can actually change the procurement scorecard.

## Impact If Built

The vendor's economic interest aligns with the outcome the client is buying, at least at the margin. Unresolved contacts get attributed, which tells the client which of their own policies and knowledge gaps are costing them. And the industry's central tension gets confronted commercially rather than managed operationally.
