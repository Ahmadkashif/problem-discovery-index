# Fix: Nobody Asks the People Who Left

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-bpo-operations/hiring-and-attrition/profile|Hiring, Onboarding & Attrition]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-bpo-operations|Digital BPO Operations]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A third of the workforce leaves every year and the exit interview is a form that half of them never complete and nobody reads in aggregate.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #workflow-orchestration #quick-win #worker-facing
**Contested on:** Whether an industry losing a third of its people annually will ask them why and read the answers.

## The Problem

Exit interviews exist on paper in most operations. They are conducted by whoever is available, often by the team leader the person may be leaving because of, in a short conversation at the end of a notice period, recorded as free text in an HR system.

Completion is partial — people who leave abruptly, which is common, complete nothing. Candour is limited, because someone may want a reference or may return. And the results are aggregated, if at all, into a pie chart of reasons that has looked the same for five years and prompts nothing.

Meanwhile tens of thousands of people a year leave with a specific, articulable reason, and nobody assembles them into a picture anyone acts on.

## Why It's Still Broken

Attrition is treated as structural, so its causes are treated as known: the pay, the work, the industry. Investigating further would imply doing something, and the recruiting machine already absorbs the flow.

The mechanics are also poorly designed. An interview conducted by the person's own manager at the end of a notice period is close to the worst possible configuration for candour. And free-text exit notes across thousands of departures are unreadable without analysis nobody was doing.

And the people who leave fastest — early attrition, which is the most expensive — are the ones least likely to have any exit process at all, because they simply stop coming.

## What a Fix Looks Like

Ask properly, ask everyone, and read the answers in aggregate.

Ask independently. Not the team leader, not HR in the building, but a neutral channel — a short anonymous survey, or a third-party interview — with an assurance that responses are aggregated. Candour rises sharply.

Ask short and ask early. A five-question survey sent on the last day and again two weeks later, when the person has no stake and is more honest than they were during notice. Two weeks out is where the useful answers are.

Catch the fast leavers. People who leave in the first ninety days are the most expensive and the least surveyed. A specific early-attrition survey, sent regardless of how the departure happened, covers the population that currently produces no data at all.

Analyse the free text properly. Thousands of open responses, clustered by theme, ranked by frequency and cross-tabbed by site, programme, shift and tenure. This is exactly what language models are good at and it is a week of work over data that in many operations already exists unread.

Cross the results with the operational data. If people leaving a particular programme cite contact difficulty and the load measurements confirm it, that is a finding with two independent sources. This is far more persuasive internally than either alone.

And report it to someone accountable, with a stated action, at a defined cadence. An exit analysis that goes into a deck is a slightly better version of nothing.

## Who Feels the Pain

Agents, leaving for reasons that recur for years because nobody assembled them. The ones who stay, working conditions that the departures were about. Team leaders, rebuilding teams continuously. And the BPO, funding a recruiting and training machine sized to replace a third of its workforce annually, against causes it has never seriously examined.

## Impact If Fixed

The industry's largest cost gets its causes documented from the people who experienced them, independently and at scale. Early attrition — the most expensive and least surveyed — enters the data for the first time. And the free text that has accumulated unread for years becomes a ranked list of specific, site-level, programme-level causes that someone can be made accountable for.
