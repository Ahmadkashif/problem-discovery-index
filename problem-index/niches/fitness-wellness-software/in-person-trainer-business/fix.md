# The Premium Slot Given Away on First-Come

**Niche:** [[niches/fitness-wellness-software/in-person-trainer-business/profile|In-Person Training — Schedule Density and Roster Retention]]
**Industry:** [[industries/fitness-wellness-software|Fitness & Wellness Software]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Early morning and evening slots are the scarce resource in a trainer's week and are allocated to whoever asked first, years ago, while the midday hours that nobody wants stay empty and are priced identically.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #optimization-fundamentals #revenue-impact #quick-win #worker-facing
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor selling to in-person trainers is fighting to fill the hours a trainer can physically work and keep a roster where every departure hurts — and whoever raises paid hours per available hour takes the account.

## The Problem
A trainer's 6am, 7am and 6pm slots have been held by the same clients for three years. Their 11am and 1pm are empty most days. New enquiries who can only do early mornings are turned away or put on an indefinite list; enquiries who could come at midday are rare. Every hour is priced the same. The trainer is simultaneously capacity-constrained at the times people want and idle at the times they do not, which is a textbook case for differential pricing and is never applied, in a profession where every practitioner has this exact problem.

## Why It's Still Broken
Differential pricing feels uncomfortable in a relationship business — charging a long-standing client more for their 6am than a new client pays for a 1pm seems like penalising loyalty, and trainers are reluctant to have the conversation. The tooling does not support it either: packages are priced per session with no notion of time-of-day. And the alternative framing, which is a discount for the unpopular hours rather than a premium for the popular ones, occurs to relatively few people and is much easier to introduce.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Measure the demand asymmetry, then price the off-peak rather than the peak. The trainer's own enquiry history and booking pattern show which hours are oversubscribed and which are never requested, which most trainers know qualitatively and have never quantified. Introduce an off-peak rate for the midday hours, framed as an option rather than as a change to anyone's existing arrangement — which fills hours that currently generate nothing, attracts the clients whose schedules are flexible, and requires no difficult conversation with anyone. Support it in packages and booking so it is a setting rather than an exception. Report the effect, since the point is to find out whether cheaper midday sessions actually attract anyone, and the answer varies by market and by clientele. For the genuinely oversubscribed slots, a waitlist with a stated policy is fairer and more useful than the indefinite one that exists now.

## Who Feels the Pain
Trainers idle at midday and turning away evening enquiries; clients on informal waitlists for popular hours with no idea where they stand; and the business, which is capacity-constrained and underutilised simultaneously.

## Impact If Fixed
Off-peak pricing fills hours that currently earn nothing, at no cost to existing arrangements, and is the single least confrontational revenue change available to an in-person trainer. Quantifying the demand asymmetry first is what makes the pricing decision evidence-based rather than a guess, and the data is already in the booking history.
