# Arguing From Nothing

**Niche:** [[niches/product-design-studios/the-designer-defending/profile|The Designer Defending a Decision]]
**Industry:** [[industries/product-design-studios|Product Design Studios]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The client says users will not scroll, the designer knows that is not true, and neither can show anything.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #quick-win #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #data-integration #tacit-knowledge-ml #confidence-intervals #workflow-orchestration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to give the designer something other than their own judgement to bring to the sixth round of the same argument — and whoever supplies that takes the account.

## The Problem
Design reviews are full of confident claims about user behaviour that nobody can check: people will not scroll, nobody reads, that many fields will kill the form, our customers are not like that. Some are true, some are folklore, and some are true generally and false for this product. The designer disagrees, has no evidence, and the claim stands because it was asserted by the client. The client's own analytics frequently contain the answer.

## Why It's Still Broken
Nobody looks at the client's own data — the answer to most of these claims is in an analytics tool the client already pays for, and nothing in the review process prompts anyone to open it. Assertions are cheap. The designer has no access. And the review moves on.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Check the client's own numbers before arguing about anyone's. Ask for read access to the client's analytics at kickoff, which is the fix and is granted far more often than it is requested. Answer behavioural claims from the client's own product where possible, since their data beats any general finding in their own room. Look up the specific claim rather than arguing the general principle, which is faster and more persuasive. Keep a short list of the recurring claims and what the evidence generally says, as the same half-dozen arrive on every project. Distinguish claims that are checkable from those that are not, and say which is which. Propose a test for the genuinely uncertain ones rather than continuing to argue. Record what was found so the next project starts with it. Ask the client where their claim comes from, which is often revealing and is rarely asked. Give the designer the numbers before the review rather than during it. And accept the client's constraint when it is a business requirement rather than a belief about users, since conflating the two wastes everyone's time.

## Who Feels the Pain
Designers conceding decisions they were right about; clients acting on folklore about their own users; the product, which is shaped by the most confident assertion; and the designer's standing, which erodes with every concession.

## Impact If Fixed
The answer to most behavioural claims is in an analytics tool the client already pays for, and nothing prompts anyone to open it. Read access at kickoff turns an argument about belief into a lookup.
