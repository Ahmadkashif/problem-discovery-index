# The Estimate Somebody Remembered

**Niche:** [[niches/product-design-studios/discovery-scoping/profile|Discovery Scoping & Pricing]]
**Industry:** [[industries/product-design-studios|Product Design Studios]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The number in the proposal came from someone's recollection of a project they ran two years ago.
**Tags:** #quick-win #descriptive-statistics #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #workflow-orchestration #data-integration #hypothesis-testing
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to price fixed-fee discovery work that by definition does not know what it will find — and whoever prices it honestly and still wins takes the account.

## The Problem
Proposals are priced from memory. A senior person recalls a similar engagement, adjusts for perceived difference, and produces a figure. Nobody checks what that earlier engagement actually cost, because timesheets and estimates live in different systems and have never been compared. The firm has run dozens of these and has no idea whether it systematically underestimates, by how much, or on what kind of work.

## Why It's Still Broken
The two numbers are never joined — an estimate in a proposal and hours in a timesheet that are never compared produce no learning, however many projects go by. Nobody owns estimating. Comparing implies judging the estimator. And margin absorbs it quietly.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Compare the estimates to the actuals you already recorded. Put every past engagement's quoted days against its actual hours in one table, which is the fix and takes a day and changes the conversation permanently. Break the overrun down by phase, since it usually concentrates in discovery or in revision rounds. Segment by engagement type and client size, as the differences are typically large. Identify the estimator-level pattern privately, which is uncomfortable and is where most of the improvement is. Compute the average contingency actually required, which the firm can price with immediately. Record the assumptions each proposal rested on so overruns can be attributed to scope or to estimating. Count scope-change conversations and what caused them, which is a separate and equally informative series. Apply the finding at the next proposal rather than waiting for a model. Keep the comparison going as part of project close. And include the projects that came in under, since they are being lost on price and that is the other half of the problem.

## Who Feels the Pain
Studios absorbing overruns out of margin; commercial leads pricing from anecdote; delivery teams working to a number nobody checked; and clients in a scope conversation halfway through.

## Impact If Fixed
An estimate in a proposal and hours in a timesheet that are never compared produce no learning, however many projects go by. One table of quoted against actual gives the firm its first real contingency number.
