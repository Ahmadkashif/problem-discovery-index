# The Stakeholder Who Was Not in the First Four Reviews

**Niche:** [[niches/product-design-studios/review-and-alignment/profile|Review & Stakeholder Alignment]]
**Industry:** [[industries/product-design-studios|Product Design Studios]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A senior person joins at round five, objects to something settled in round two, and the work goes back a fortnight.
**Tags:** #quick-win #workflow-orchestration #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #compliance #worker-facing #automation #data-integration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to get a design decision made when the objections are preferences and neither side has evidence — and whoever makes that process work takes the account.

## The Problem
The most expensive event in a design engagement is a late arrival with authority. Someone senior who has not attended reviews sees the work for the first time near the end, raises an objection to a decision made weeks earlier, and the team reopens it. The original reasoning is not written down, the people who made the decision may not be in the room, and the fastest resolution is to do what the new person wants.

## Why It's Still Broken
The reasoning was never recorded — a decision that exists only as a change in a file cannot be defended weeks later, so the person who arrives last effectively decides. Attendance is not managed. Nobody defined who decides. And the studio has no standing to refuse.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Write down the decisions and name the approvers at the start. Keep a running decision log with the reason and the approver for each, which is the fix and takes minutes per review. Identify every stakeholder with authority at kickoff and secure their attendance or their delegation, since the late arrival is the predictable failure. Circulate decisions to absentees after each review rather than at the end, which gives them the chance to object cheaply. Require new information rather than a new opinion to reopen a decision, and state that convention at kickoff. Show the schedule cost of reopening at the moment it is proposed, which resolves a good share of them. Have the client's own decision-maker respond to a late objection rather than the studio, which is both more effective and correctly placed. Keep the log visible to the client throughout rather than producing it defensively. Record what was considered and rejected, since late objections are frequently options already examined. Bound reopening to a defined point in the schedule. And treat the log as a client deliverable, which makes maintaining it obviously worthwhile.

## Who Feels the Pain
Designers reworking settled decisions; producers whose schedule absorbs a fortnight; clients paying for the same decision twice; and the design, which ends up assembled from whoever spoke last.

## Impact If Fixed
A decision that exists only as a change in a file cannot be defended weeks later, so the person who arrives last effectively decides. A running decision log with reasons and approvers costs minutes per review.
