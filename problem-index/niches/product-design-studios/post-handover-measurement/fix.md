# Asking the Client What Happened

**Niche:** [[niches/product-design-studios/post-handover-measurement/profile|Post-Handover Measurement]]
**Industry:** [[industries/product-design-studios|Product Design Studios]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Three months after launch nobody from the studio has asked how it went, and the client would have said.
**Tags:** #quick-win #workflow-orchestration #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #automation #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #data-integration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to get one engagement's outcome back from a client whose contract has closed — and whoever makes that routine takes the account.

## The Problem
The simplest version of the problem is that nobody asks. The relationship is good, the client is reachable, and a short message three months after launch asking what the numbers did would be answered. It does not happen because the project closed, the team moved on, and following up is not in anyone's role. The information is available for the cost of an email and is left uncollected on every engagement.

## Why It's Still Broken
The follow-up belongs to no role — a task that sits outside every job description after a project closes will not happen, however cheap and however obviously useful. The engagement is invoiced and complete. Asking feels like it needs a pretext. And nobody has counted what is being lost.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Schedule the conversation at close and keep it short. Book the follow-up at project close rather than relying on anyone remembering, which is the fix and is a calendar entry. Ask two or three specific questions rather than sending a survey, since specificity is what gets a reply. Ask what the client measured and what it did, which is the whole substance. Have the account holder ask rather than the designer, which suits both roles. Record the answer in a consistent form so the answers accumulate. Ask about what did not work as well as what did, because the studio learns more from that and clients respect being asked. Frame it as improving the studio's work rather than as seeking a testimonial. Use it as the natural opening for a follow-on conversation, which is where it pays commercially. Do it on every engagement rather than the flagship ones. And accept a qualitative answer, since even an impression is more than the studio currently has.

## Who Feels the Pain
Studios with no evidence about their own work; designers who never learn whether they were right; clients who would have shared and were not asked; and every proposal argued on portfolio alone.

## Impact If Fixed
A task that sits outside every job description after a project closes will not happen, however cheap and obviously useful. A scheduled three-question follow-up is a calendar entry that starts the whole loop.
