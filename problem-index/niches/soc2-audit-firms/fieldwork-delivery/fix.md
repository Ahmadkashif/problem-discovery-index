# Fix: Everyone's Year End Is the Same Month

**Niche:** Fieldwork Delivery
**Industry:** [[industries/soc2-audit-firms|SOC 2 & Attestation Audit Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Clients pick a reporting period by convention, most pick the same one, and the firm's entire capacity is oversubscribed for a quarter and idle for another.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #time-series-forecasting #confidence-intervals #workflow-orchestration #worker-facing #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Whether fieldwork is spread across the period or compressed into a window.

## The Problem

A company setting up its first SOC 2 picks a reporting period. Nobody advises them particularly. They choose the calendar year, or their fiscal year, because that is what seems normal.

So does almost everyone else. The result is that audit demand concentrates into the months following the common period ends, and a firm's capacity is oversubscribed for a quarter and underused for another.

The consequences are the ones everyone in the industry recognises. Associates work extreme hours during the peak. Quality varies with fatigue. Reviews queue because managers are also on engagements. Deadlines slip, which matters because clients need the report for a deal. And attrition follows the peak reliably enough that firms plan hiring around it.

The period end is a choice. Very few clients have any real constraint on it — a SOC 2 period is defined by the engagement, not by an external calendar. A client whose period ended in a quieter month would get a faster audit, more senior attention and frequently a lower fee, and would lose nothing.

Nobody tells them. The period is set in the first conversation, by convention, and is then fixed for years because changing it requires a stub period and a conversation nobody initiates.

## Why It's Still Broken

**Nobody advises on the choice.** The period is agreed in scoping as an administrative detail, and the firm's own capacity interest is not raised.

**Clients assume it must align to something.** Finance year ends, board cycles and fiscal reporting create an assumption that the SOC 2 period should match, which it need not.

**Changing later requires a stub period.** Moving an established period means a short transitional report, which is a minor inconvenience treated as a blocker.

**Firms compete for the peak work.** A firm declining peak engagements loses them to a competitor, so nobody wants to be the one shaping demand.

**No pricing incentive exists.** Almost no firm offers a discount for an off-peak period, which is the obvious mechanism and would work.

**The peak's cost is absorbed by associates.** Because the overrun lands as hours rather than as a cost line, the firm's own numbers do not show the problem clearly.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Advise on the period at scoping, deliberately.** Tell first-time clients that the period is theirs to choose, that an off-peak end means faster turnaround and more senior attention, and that nothing requires alignment to the fiscal year. Most would move if asked.

**Price off-peak periods lower.** A discount for periods ending in quiet months costs the firm nothing net and moves demand. It is the standard mechanism in every industry with a seasonal peak and essentially nobody in this one offers it.

**Offer the stub period free.** The transition cost is the barrier for existing clients, and a firm absorbing it would move a substantial share of its own book out of the peak within two years.

**Unbundle what does not need the period to have ended.** Walkthroughs, description review and control design assessment can happen months before, which spreads the work without the client noticing anything.

**Measure the peak's real cost.** Overtime hours, review turnaround, deadline slippage and post-peak attrition. The number would very likely justify a significant investment in demand shaping.

**Manage the review bottleneck.** Manager review is the constraint during the peak and is performed by people who are themselves on engagements. Protecting review capacity separately is a scheduling decision that would address the most acute delay.

**Tell clients what the peak costs them.** A client in the peak receives slower turnaround and less senior attention than one outside it, and they have never been told.

## Who Feels the Pain

The associates, working extreme hours for a quarter every year, in a pattern the industry treats as normal and that drives its attrition.

The clients in the peak, who receive slower turnaround and less senior attention and do not know that a different period end would have avoided it.

The managers, whose review queue is the bottleneck and who are reviewing while running their own engagements.

And the firm, which sizes for the mean, absorbs the peak in overtime, and loses people afterwards.

## Impact If Fixed

Advising clients on the period choice costs nothing, would move a meaningful share of demand, and is not currently offered because nobody has thought of the period as a variable.

An off-peak discount and a free stub period are straightforward commercial mechanisms that would reshape a firm's own book within two years.

And measuring the peak's cost — overtime, slippage, attrition — would give the industry a number for a problem it currently discusses entirely in anecdote.
