# Fix: The Review Comes Back Four Days Later

**Niche:** The Audit Associate
**Industry:** [[industries/soc2-audit-firms|SOC 2 & Attestation Audit Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** An associate documents twenty tests the same way, the review comes back days later, and the same correction applies to all twenty.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #automation
**Contested on:** Whether the entry-level role is professional training or evidence checking at high utilisation.

## The Problem

An associate completes a control test and documents it. They complete the next one the same way, and the next, working through a section over several days.

The manager reviews at the end of the section, or at the end of the week, because they are running three engagements and reviewing is what happens when there is a gap. The review comes back with a comment: the documentation does not evidence the reperformance adequately.

It applies to every test in the section. The associate now reworks twenty workpapers, having repeated the same error twenty times because nobody told them after the first.

This happens continuously. The feedback loop in audit fieldwork is measured in days, so errors propagate across a body of work before they are caught, and the associate learns the correction after they have already embedded the mistake.

The cost is rework during the busiest period of the year, and it is also the learning experience — an associate who receives feedback four days late learns slowly and experiences the work as a cycle of doing things and being told they were wrong.

## Why It's Still Broken

**Managers review when they can.** They are running several engagements and reviewing is squeezed between them, so the queue is a function of their load rather than of the associate's need.

**Review is structured by section, not by item.** Workpaper platforms present review as a batch, which makes reviewing the first item separately an unnatural act.

**The peak makes it worse.** Review latency is longest exactly when the volume is highest, which is when the propagation cost is greatest.

**Nobody measures review turnaround.** Its effect on rework and on learning is not tracked, so it is not managed.

**The rework is absorbed by the associate.** It lands on their hours rather than on the engagement's cost, so the firm does not experience it as a problem.

**Early review feels inefficient to the manager.** Reviewing one test at a time seems like more work than reviewing twenty, which is true for the manager and false for the engagement.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Review the first test in every section immediately.** One item, reviewed within hours of completion, catching a systematic error before it is repeated. This is the fix, it costs a manager minutes, and it saves an associate days.

**Set a review turnaround expectation.** Hours rather than days for the first item, with the rest batched. Making it an expectation rather than a courtesy is what makes it happen during the peak.

**Automate the documentation standards check.** Whether a workpaper contains the required elements is a rule, checkable automatically, catching a large share of review comments before a human sees them.

**Show the associate examples.** A reviewed, accepted workpaper for the same control type from a previous engagement, available before they start, prevents the error rather than correcting it.

**Track review latency and rework.** How long reviews take and how much rework results. The number would justify protecting review capacity, which is currently the first thing squeezed.

**Protect manager review time explicitly.** Review is a distinct activity from running engagements and is currently done in the gaps. Scheduling it is a resourcing decision.

**Aggregate the review comments.** The same comments recur across associates and engagements, and turning the recurring ones into standards, templates or automated checks would remove them permanently.

## Who Feels the Pain

The associate, repeating a mistake twenty times before learning of it, reworking during the busiest period, and experiencing the work as correction rather than as learning.

The manager, reviewing a body of work containing a systematic error they could have caught in one item, and dealing with the rework that follows.

The engagement, which absorbs the delay and the rework at the point in the year when it can least afford either.

And the firm's retention, since the experience of repeated late correction during an extreme workload is a recognisable part of why people leave.

## Impact If Fixed

Reviewing the first test in every section within hours costs a manager minutes and prevents a systematic error from propagating across twenty workpapers.

Automated documentation standards checking would remove a large share of review comments before a human sees them, which frees the review for the judgement questions it should be about.

And aggregating recurring review comments into standards and templates would permanently eliminate errors that are currently corrected individually, associate by associate, engagement after engagement.
