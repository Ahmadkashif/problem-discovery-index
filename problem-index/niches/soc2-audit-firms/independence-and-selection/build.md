# Build: Visible Selection and Rotation

**Niche:** Auditor Independence & Selection
**Industry:** [[industries/soc2-audit-firms|SOC 2 & Attestation Audit Firms]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Make auditor changes, tenure and predecessor communications visible in the report, so the selection history a reader currently cannot see becomes part of what they can evaluate.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #compliance #descriptive-statistics #revenue-impact #workflow-orchestration
**Contested on:** Whether the party selecting and paying the auditor can be the party the audit is about.

## The Problem

A company changes auditors. The previous firm had pushed on scope and identified two control failures. The new firm's engagement begins with a client who knows exactly what happened and a fee negotiated accordingly.

The report the following year is clean and looks identical to any other. The reader does not know there was a change. They do not know the tenure of the current firm, whether a predecessor was replaced, or whether the previous report was qualified.

Every one of those is a signal about the audit, and none of them appears anywhere the reader can see. A company on its fourth auditor in five years and one with a ten-year relationship produce indistinguishable artefacts.

The information exists. The company knows. The incoming auditor knows, or should, through predecessor communication. And in financial audit, auditor changes are disclosed publicly for exactly this reason — the pattern of changes is informative and the market uses it.

The structural conflict cannot be removed while the audited party pays. Making the selection history visible does not remove it and does make it observable, which is the achievable step.

## Why Nobody Has Built This

**The report has nowhere for it.** Attestation report format does not include auditor tenure or predecessor information, and adding it requires the standard to change.

**Disclosure is against the client's interest.** A company that changed auditors after a difficult engagement does not want that visible, and the company commissions the report.

**Nobody in the transaction wants it.** The client does not, the auditor does not want to raise it, and the relying party is not present to ask.

**Predecessor communication is weak.** The requirement exists in various forms and in practice a departing firm may say little, so even the incoming auditor is not well informed.

**Rotation would reduce firm revenue.** Mandatory rotation caps relationship length, which every incumbent firm has a reason to resist.

**No regulator owns it.** Financial audit disclosure requirements come from securities regulation. Attestation has professional standards and no equivalent mandate.

## What to Build

**Disclose auditor tenure in the report.** How long this firm has issued the opinion. One line, and it is a signal a reader can use immediately.

**Disclose predecessor changes.** Whether the previous period was audited by a different firm, and whether the predecessor's opinion was qualified. Financial audit discloses changes because the pattern is informative, and it is equally informative here.

**Strengthen predecessor communication.** A defined handover including scope history, prior exceptions and reasons for change, provided to the incoming auditor. This protects the incoming firm as much as it informs anyone.

**Adopt voluntary rotation and say so.** A firm rotating its engagement partner on a defined cycle, or a company rotating firms periodically, can state it. Voluntary disclosure is available now and requires no standard change.

**Let relying parties see the selection.** Where a large customer's requirement is driving the audit, they could be given a say — even a list of acceptable firms. This inverts the selection problem partially and is entirely achievable between two commercial parties.

**Publish firm-level statistics.** Qualification rates, client retention and average tenure, by firm. This is the market signal that does not exist, and a firm with nothing to hide benefits from it.

**Relying parties should ask.** How long has this firm audited you, and was there a change — two questions a vendor risk team can ask about any report, immediately.

## Target Customer

Relying parties and enterprise vendor risk teams, who are the beneficiaries of the audit, are excluded from its governance, and have commercial leverage over their suppliers.

Standards bodies, who own the report format and could require tenure and change disclosure with a small amendment.

Audit firms with long stable relationships and good qualification records, for whom published statistics would be an advantage rather than a risk.

## Impact If Built

Auditor tenure and change disclosure are one-line additions that give a reader a signal financial audit has considered informative for decades.

Letting relying parties have a say in selection — even a list of acceptable firms — partially inverts the structural conflict and is available between commercial parties today without any standard changing.

And two questions asked by relying parties, about tenure and about changes, would surface the selection history that currently sits entirely outside what anyone outside the transaction can see.
