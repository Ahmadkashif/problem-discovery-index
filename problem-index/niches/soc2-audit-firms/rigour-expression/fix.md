# Fix: The Party Who Wants Rigour Is Not in the Room

**Niche:** Rigour Expression
**Industry:** [[industries/soc2-audit-firms|SOC 2 & Attestation Audit Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The company buying the audit wants it clean and fast, the customer relying on it wants it searching, and only one of them is a party to the engagement.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #compliance #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #worker-facing #descriptive-statistics
**Contested on:** Whether a report can convey how searching the audit was.

## The Problem

A company needs a SOC 2 report because three enterprise prospects require one. They select an auditor on price and timeline, because those are the attributes they can compare and because the report is a gate to a deal closing this quarter.

The enterprise customers who required the report want something else entirely. They want the audit to have been thorough, the scope to have covered the systems that actually process their data, and the auditor to have pushed back where the description was convenient. They are relying on the opinion to make a supplier decision.

They are not in the transaction. They did not select the auditor, do not pay the fee, cannot specify the scope, and receive the report as a finished artefact whose production they had no part in.

So the auditor is selected by the party whose interest is a clean report and relied upon by the party whose interest is a searching one. The market's entire quality signal is therefore produced under the first party's preferences.

This is the classic structure and it is not solvable by any individual actor inside it. The company cannot pay more for rigour it cannot demonstrate. The auditor cannot invest in rigour that is invisible. And the relying party cannot express a preference it cannot detect.

## Why It's Still Broken

**The structure is inherent to the model.** Somebody must pay for the audit, and the audited company is the only party with a transactional relationship to it.

**Relying parties process at volume.** An enterprise vendor risk team reviewing hundreds of supplier reports cannot engage with each audit's quality, so they accept the artefact as binary.

**The artefact is binary by design.** A clean opinion is what the standard produces, and it is what makes the report usable in a vendor risk process at scale.

**No firm can move alone.** A firm that made rigour visible would be disclosing against competitors who do not, for a buyer who does not want it disclosed.

**The purchaser's interest is legitimate.** A company that runs good controls and wants an efficient audit is not doing anything wrong, and the problem is not bad actors but a missing signal.

**Nobody has tried the demand side.** The relying parties have collective leverage over their suppliers and have never used it on this.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Relying parties should specify what they require.** A large enterprise stating that it will accept reports only where testing depth and scope exclusions are disclosed changes what its suppliers commission. This is available now, requires no standard, and is the fastest route.

**Coordinate among relying parties.** A group of large buyers requiring the same disclosure creates a market-wide expectation without any regulator or standards body acting.

**Ask the specific questions.** Which systems were excluded from scope and why; what proportion of each control's population was tested; whether the auditor required changes to the system description. Three questions a vendor risk team can ask about any report they receive.

**Treat scope as the first thing to examine.** The boundary is where most of the variation lives, it is stated in the report, and it is the part relying parties least examine.

**Value the auditor's identity properly.** Where a relying party does have informal views about which firms are thorough, recording and sharing them within the organisation is better than the current state of individual impression.

**Companies should buy the audit their customers want.** A supplier whose customers are large and sophisticated has a commercial reason to commission a rigorous audit and to be able to say so, and almost none think about it that way.

**Standards bodies should close it properly.** The durable fix is a required disclosure, and the demand-side pressure is what would prompt it.

## Who Feels the Pain

The enterprise customer, making supplier decisions on an artefact whose informational content varies enormously and is not expressed.

The rigorous audit firm, whose additional work is invisible and unrewarded, competing against firms whose reports look identical.

The well-run supplier, whose genuinely strong controls produce the same report as a competitor's weak ones.

And the market as a whole, which competes on price for a product whose quality nobody can observe — the classic outcome of exactly this structure.

## Impact If Fixed

The demand side is where this is winnable. A handful of large enterprise buyers requiring disclosure would change what the whole supplier base commissions, immediately, without waiting for any standard.

Three specific questions asked of every report received — scope exclusions, testing depth, description amendments — would give a vendor risk team more signal than they currently get from the entire document.

And examining the scope boundary first is the highest-return change a relying party can make, because that is where most of the variation between audits actually sits.
