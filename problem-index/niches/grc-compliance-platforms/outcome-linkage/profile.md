# Outcome Linkage

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/grc-compliance-platforms|GRC & Compliance Platforms]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Whether what actually happened to organisations — incidents, breaches, claims, regulatory findings — can be joined to their control state at all.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$720M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~8%
**Digital Adoption:** Very low — the data is in three places and joined nowhere
**Target Buyer:** Cyber insurers, regulators, research institutions, platform leadership
**Automation Potential:** Moderate — the obstacle is access and governance, not technique

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

This is the dependent variable, and it lives outside the platform entirely.

Customers do not volunteer incidents; disclosure is reluctant, partial and legally fraught. Insurers hold claims data and treat it as competitive. Public breach notification captures only the largest events, with selection bias so severe that any analysis built on it alone would be misleading. Regulators hold findings that are not shared. Every source is incomplete, biased differently, and held by a party with reasons not to share.

That is what separates this from its sibling. [[niches/grc-compliance-platforms/control-state-normalisation/profile|🎯 Control State Normalisation]] is internal, immediate and improves the product on its own terms. Outcome linkage requires institutional arrangements with parties the platform does not control, returns nothing for years because serious incidents are rare per organisation, is confounded by everything, and ends in a finding that would tell a certification business which of its certifications do not matter.

The contest is over whether the join can be constructed at all — through insurer partnership, confidential registry, voluntary reporting under governance, or public data used carefully with its biases modelled. Whoever assembles it credibly holds the only evidence base security compliance has ever had.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Public breach disclosure under notification laws, which captures large events at large organisations. Industry incident reports describing what happened without any denominator of control state. Cyber insurance underwriting questionnaires collected at policy inception, and claims data held internally by carriers. Regulatory enforcement actions, published selectively. Threat intelligence describing attacker behaviour rather than defender state.

The gaps are institutional rather than technical. No registry exists where control state and outcomes are held together under governance that would make organisations willing to contribute. Insurers hold the best outcome data and have no structured access to control state beyond self-assertion. Voluntary incident reporting has no confidentiality architecture that would make it safe. Public disclosure has no denominator, so it cannot support any rate calculation. And the near-miss data — incidents that were detected and contained — is far more plentiful than breaches and is collected by nobody.
