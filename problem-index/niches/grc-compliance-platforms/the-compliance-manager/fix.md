# Fix: Responsible for Everything, Authority Over Nothing

**Niche:** The Compliance Manager
**Industry:** [[industries/grc-compliance-platforms|GRC & Compliance Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The person accountable for the certificate commands none of the teams whose work produces it, and their only lever is an escalation that works less well every time they use it.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #compliance #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Whether one person running several certification programmes has any leverage over the organisation whose cooperation the whole thing depends on.

## The Problem

The compliance manager is accountable for the audit outcome. Almost everything that determines it is done by people who do not report to them.

Engineering supplies evidence and remediates findings. IT manages device compliance and access. HR handles onboarding and training records. Legal owns contracts and subprocessor terms. Each has their own priorities, their own manager, and no accountability for the certificate.

The manager's tools are asking nicely, the audit deadline, and escalation. The first works while goodwill lasts. The second is an external constraint that people respond to and increasingly resent. The third — going to a leader to ask them to direct a team — is powerful, expensive, and degrades with use. A manager who escalates twice a quarter is seen as unable to manage their own programme, and one who never escalates does not get the work done.

So they spend their capacity on relationship maintenance and careful chasing, which is a reasonable adaptation and is not a system. When it fails, it fails at the audit, and the accountability lands on the person who had no authority.

## Why It's Still Broken

**Nobody constituted the role.** The compliance function grew because a certificate was needed for a deal. It was given a person and an objective, and the organisational question of how the work gets done across teams was never addressed.

**Certification is treated as a compliance problem.** Leadership regards the certificate as compliance's deliverable rather than as an organisational commitment requiring engineering, IT and HR time. Framing it that way makes the resourcing question disappear.

**There is no agreed capacity commitment.** No team has agreed to allocate time to compliance work. Every request is therefore a negotiation against work they have committed to, and the manager loses most of those negotiations on merit.

**The cost is invisible.** Nobody knows what compliance consumes in engineering hours, so nobody has ever decided to allocate it, so it is taken ad hoc from whoever cooperates.

**Escalation is the only lever and is self-limiting.** Its effectiveness declines with use and its use signals a problem with the manager rather than with the arrangement.

**The manager is junior.** Frequently the first compliance hire, without the seniority to set expectations with engineering leadership, reporting to someone for whom compliance is one item among many.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Get an explicit capacity commitment from each team.** A stated allocation — an engineer-day a month per team, or a named person with a defined share of their time — agreed with engineering leadership at the start of the certification year. This converts every individual request from a negotiation into a draw against an agreed budget, which is the whole difference.

**Make the certificate an organisational objective, not compliance's.** If the certificate gates revenue, it belongs in the leadership team's objectives with named contributions from engineering, IT and HR. Compliance coordinates; it does not own the outcome alone.

**Give the manager a standing forum.** A brief recurring slot with engineering and IT leadership where outstanding work is reviewed. Routine visibility replaces episodic escalation, which is what makes escalation expensive in the first place.

**Count the hours and publish them.** What compliance actually costs each team, measured. This is the number that makes the capacity conversation concrete and it currently does not exist anywhere.

**Name a counterpart in each function.** One person in engineering, IT and HR who owns their side. The manager then coordinates with four peers rather than chasing forty individuals.

**Escalate on a schedule, not on frustration.** A monthly report of what is outstanding and what it blocks, sent routinely to leadership. Making it a standing artefact removes the political cost of raising it and means nothing needs to become a crisis before it is visible.

**Give the role appropriate seniority or appropriate cover.** Either the manager has the standing to set expectations with engineering leadership, or someone who does is explicitly accountable alongside them. The current arrangement asks a junior person to direct senior teams by persuasion.

## Who Feels the Pain

The compliance manager, accountable for an outcome produced by people they cannot direct, spending their capacity on persuasion, and blamed if it fails.

Engineering teams, receiving unbudgeted requests that were never planned into their capacity because nobody ever agreed the capacity.

The organisation, whose revenue-gating certificate depends on an arrangement held together by one person's relationships.

And the next compliance manager, who inherits the goodwill balance the last one spent and no structure whatsoever.

## Impact If Fixed

An agreed capacity commitment per team is the fix, and it costs a conversation at the start of the year. Every request afterwards becomes a draw against an agreed allocation rather than an argument.

Making the certificate an organisational objective with named contributors puts the accountability where the work is, instead of concentrating it on the one person with no authority.

And a standing monthly report removes the political cost of visibility, which is what currently forces the manager to choose between letting things slide and spending a limited escalation budget.
