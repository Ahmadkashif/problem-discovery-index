# Multiples on Earnings That Did Not Transfer

**Niche:** [[niches/ecommerce-aggregators/acquisition-underwriting/profile|Acquisition Underwriting]]
**Industry:** [[industries/ecommerce-aggregators|Ecommerce Aggregators]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Aggregators paid multiples on trailing earnings for brands whose performance rested on things that did not transfer with the sale, and the sector's contraction is the consequence of a prediction nobody made well.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #confidence-intervals #causal-inference #hypothesis-testing #revenue-impact #evaluation-metrics #time-series-forecasting
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to predict whether an acquired brand's revenue survives the change of ownership — and whoever does that takes the market, because the sector's contraction was the consequence of that prediction being made badly at scale.

## The Problem
A brand earns steadily for three years and is bought at a multiple of that. Eighteen months later it earns a third as much. The reasons were visible before the sale: the seller had a fifteen-year relationship with a factory that gave them pricing nobody else got, they had built ranking on a review velocity that stopped when they stopped soliciting, three competitors had entered the category in the previous year with better products, and the seller personally adjusted prices and advertising daily in a way a portfolio operator cannot. None of that appeared in the financial statements, all of it was observable, and the underwriting model was a multiple.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
The sector grew during a period when capital was cheap and speed won auctions, which rewarded fast underwriting over accurate underwriting. The predictive features require marketplace data analysis rather than accounting, and the diligence teams were financial. The multiple was a market convention and deviating from it meant losing deals. And by the time the outcomes were known, the capital had been deployed.

## What to Build
Underwrite the persistence, not the earnings. Model revenue persistence directly from observable pre-acquisition features — ranking stability and its trajectory, review velocity and its sources, competitive entry in the category, price elasticity and discount dependence, supplier concentration, breadth of the product range, the seller's personal operating cadence — which is a well-posed problem the moment a firm has a dozen realised outcomes, and is the whole build. Separate transferable earnings from seller-dependent earnings explicitly, and price only the former, which is the underwriting discipline the sector skipped. Assess the supplier relationship as a distinct risk, since supplier pricing is frequently the largest single unstated dependency and is knowable through diligence questions nobody asked. Model competitive vulnerability from category entry rates and the defensibility of the product, since a commodity product ranking well is a position rather than an asset. Price the migration disruption explicitly, since it is predictable and is currently treated as an operational detail rather than as a cost of acquisition. Report a distribution of outcomes rather than a base case, because the variance is the whole risk and a point estimate hides it. Feed realised outcomes back into the model after every acquisition, which the corpus niche develops and which is how the underwriting compounds. And be willing to walk from auctions the model prices below the clearing price, which is the discipline that makes any of it matter.

## Target Customer
Acquirers and their investment committees, the debt providers financing them, and the sellers whose businesses are being valued by a convention.

## Impact If Built
The sector deployed capital on an accounting figure while the drivers of persistence were observable and unanalysed. Separating transferable from seller-dependent earnings is the discipline that was skipped, and pricing the migration disruption turns a predictable loss into an underwritten cost.
