## The Problem
Finding consolidation opportunities across a fragmented spend base is the founding exercise of strategic sourcing, and procurement built the whole apparatus for it: spend cubes that classify every line of spend into a common taxonomy, category strategies, supplier rationalisation programmes, should-cost modelling, and total cost of ownership rather than unit price. Every one of those is directly applicable to an aggregator's problem and this sector uses a spreadsheet and a sourcing manager's memory.

## What Already Exists
Spend analysis with automated classification into a common taxonomy; category strategy methodology; supplier rationalisation and consolidation programmes; should-cost and clean-sheet modelling; total cost of ownership frameworks; and supplier risk and concentration assessment.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to a spend base assembled by acquisition rather than grown. It requires: (1) classification of products rather than of spend lines, since procurement classifies what was bought from invoices and the relevant grouping here is by how the thing is made — which makes the product attributes the classification input and is the specific analytical change; (2) manufacturability clustering rather than category taxonomy, because the opportunity crosses categories; (3) should-cost modelling for small-volume consumer goods, where the classic approach assumes engineering drawings and here the specification frequently does not exist in writing; (4) the demand-side risk of a supplier change modelled alongside the saving, which procurement does not have to consider and which is the largest hidden cost here; and (5) a cadence that matches acquisition, since the spend base changes every time a brand is bought and a periodic category review is the wrong rhythm.

## Target Customer
Aggregator sourcing functions, procurement software vendors for whom this is an adjacent market, and the strategic sourcing profession.

## Impact If Solved
Procurement's apparatus for exactly this exercise is decades mature and unused here. Classifying by manufacturability rather than by spend category is the analytical change, and modelling the demand-side risk of a supplier change is the cost procurement never has to consider.
