# The Supplier Relationship That Left With the Seller

**Niche:** [[niches/ecommerce-aggregators/supply-and-sourcing-operations/profile|Supply & Sourcing Operations]]
**Industry:** [[industries/ecommerce-aggregators|Ecommerce Aggregators]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A brand's margin frequently rested on pricing a seller earned through a decade of personal relationship with a factory, and the purchase agreement transferred a supplier list.
**Tags:** #revenue-impact #confidence-intervals #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #compliance #hypothesis-testing #quick-win #data-integration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this sub-niche is fighting to deliver a lower landed cost across products that share nothing — and whoever does that proves the thesis, because supply consolidation is the synergy the whole model was underwritten on.

## The Problem
A brand's gross margin was forty-five percent because the seller had bought from the same factory for eleven years, visited annually, paid on time and had earned pricing and priority nobody else got. The acquisition transferred the supplier's contact details. The new owner is one of forty accounts managed by a sourcing team, orders in the same quantities at list pricing, and finds the margin is thirty-four percent — a change large enough to invalidate the acquisition price, discovered in the first order cycle. The dependency was the business and it was documented nowhere.

## Why It's Still Broken
Diligence examined financial statements, and a relationship does not appear in one. Sellers do not volunteer that their margin depends on goodwill, and buyers do not ask because the question sounds naive. The supplier has no obligation to extend the same terms and often reprices immediately. And the loss appears as a cost of goods variance rather than as an underwriting error.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Test the dependency before paying for it. Ask the supplier directly during diligence, with the seller's cooperation, what terms would apply to a new owner ordering the same volumes — a question that costs a phone call and settles the largest single unstated risk in the transaction, which makes it the fix. Benchmark the brand's unit costs against market quotes for comparable production, since a margin far above the benchmark is evidence of a relationship rather than of a product advantage. Structure the deal to carry the risk: a seller transition period, an introduction obligation, an earn-out contingent on maintained supply terms, or a price adjustment if terms change. Retain the seller in a supplier-facing role for a defined period, which is cheap and preserves the relationship through the transition. Document the relationship's specifics — who is spoken to, what was agreed informally, what the payment and priority arrangements are — since the knowledge is in one person's head and leaves on completion. Build the new relationship deliberately rather than transactionally, since the sourcing team's account management model is what erodes it. Report supplier-dependent margin as a risk line in the underwriting. And measure realised cost of goods against the underwritten assumption per acquisition, so the pattern is visible across deals rather than absorbed one at a time.

## Who Feels the Pain
Acquirers whose margin assumption fails in the first order cycle; sourcing teams inheriting a relationship they cannot recreate; and investors whose returns include a variance nobody underwrote.

## Impact If Fixed
The largest unstated risk in the transaction is settled by a phone call to the supplier during diligence. Benchmarking unit costs against market quotes distinguishes a product advantage from a relationship, which is the difference between an asset and a departure.
