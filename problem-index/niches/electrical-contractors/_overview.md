## Why These Niches

Electrical contracting is not one market — it fragments along project type (service vs. new construction vs. maintenance), voltage class (120V residential vs. 480V industrial), setting (residential vs. commercial vs. industrial), and business function (field operations vs. estimating vs. compliance). These 8 niches cover the full span: the two largest revenue segments (residential service and commercial new construction), the two most digitally neglected (industrial maintenance and solar/EV integration), the two most underserved by existing tools (small/rural electricians and multi-family property management), and the two highest-ROI automation targets (panel inspection diagnostics and estimating/job costing). Excluded: utility-scale power distribution (a distinct industry), low-voltage/data cabling (specialized sub-segment), and fire alarm systems (governed by separate NFPA standards and licensing).

## Why These Pockets

The Pass 1 analysis names two tacit-knowledge crises in this trade: the master electrician's diagnostic pattern recognition, and labour estimating. The sweep finds that the second has an entire industry above it selling the answer, and the first does not.

Four qualifiers, and they form an unusually clean stack — one organization writes the rules, another prices the work, a third finds the work, and a fourth does the pricing for the 80% of firms too small to employ an estimator. The code body is the most distinctive of them: its publication is adopted into law, its clock is a fixed three-year cycle with statutory adoption following, and its proprietary asset is a development record spanning decades. Its central weakness is structural — the code is a dependency graph published as a numbered document, so a change accepted in one panel can silently alter a provision in another and the safeguard is the memory of long-serving members.

The recurring gap across the other three is the same one this sweep has found in every estimating and lead business: the outcome exists, is observable, and is not collected. Labour unit publishers set installation times by study while contractors record actual hours against every job. Lead publishers assert that a project will bid and their own researchers later observe whether it did, overwriting the prediction rather than scoring it. Estimating bureaus produce thousands of bids a year into a market that publishes the winning number on every public job — a free, complete calibration set nobody uses.

Nine pockets logged without qualifying, and the most pointed is the last. The body responsible for transferring the trade's tacit knowledge to the next generation — the national apprenticeship curriculum function, in an industry the Pass 1 note describes as facing a genuine knowledge-transfer crisis — employs fewer than twenty researchers nationally and fails the gate.
