# Value Before Configuration

**Niche:** [[niches/contract-lifecycle-platforms/mid-market-clm/profile|Mid-Market CLM]]
**Industry:** [[industries/contract-lifecycle-platforms|Contract Lifecycle Platforms]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Every CLM product delivers value after an implementation project, and the companies that most need contract management are exactly the ones with nobody available to run one.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #bert #transfer-learning #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #automation #compliance
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor here is fighting to deliver contract management value to a company with no legal operations function and no implementation budget — and whoever does that takes a market the category has never reached, because everyone else requires a project first.

## The Problem
A company with three hundred employees has perhaps six hundred executed agreements in a shared drive, an office manager who files them, no lawyer on staff, and an auto-renewal they will miss this year for the third time. They evaluate a CLM product. The demonstration is impressive and the implementation plan describes a clause library, a playbook workshop, a workflow design phase and a data migration. They have nobody to do any of it. They buy nothing and remain on the shared drive, which does not work and requires no project.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
The category's product architecture assumes configuration because its first customers had configuration teams, and moving down-market has meant selling the same architecture at a lower price rather than removing the requirement. Vendors also expect the customer to supply the legal content — templates, playbooks, positions — because supplying it themselves edges toward providing legal advice, which is a boundary they have preferred not to approach. And the deal sizes at this tier do not support a sales motion built around an implementation conversation, so the go-to-market does not work either.

## What to Build
Value on the first day, from what the customer already has. Point it at the shared drive and extract the estate — parties, dates, renewals, notice periods, values and the handful of terms that matter — with no schema to configure, since the terms that matter are broadly the same for every company at this size and the product should simply know them. Produce the outputs that justify it immediately: a renewal and notice calendar, a list of agreements that auto-renew within ninety days, the counterparties with the most exposure, and the agreements with no liability cap — all derived, none configured. Supply the legal content rather than expecting it: standard templates and a default position set for common agreement types, drawn from what the vendor's corpus shows is ordinary, with the boundary drawn at describing what is standard rather than advising what to do. Let the playbook emerge from use rather than from a workshop, learning positions from what this company actually agrees to. And design for a buyer who will decide in a week: the first useful output within an hour of pointing it at the drive, because a product that needs a month to become useful will not survive contact with this customer.

## Target Customer
Companies of roughly one hundred to two thousand employees with contract estates and no legal operations function, and the CLM and e-signature vendors seeking a market below the enterprise.

## Impact If Built
The failure to reach this market is architectural rather than commercial, which means a lower price on the same product will not fix it. Deriving the estate from a shared drive with no configuration, and supplying the legal content rather than requesting it, are the two changes that make the category reachable at this tier.
