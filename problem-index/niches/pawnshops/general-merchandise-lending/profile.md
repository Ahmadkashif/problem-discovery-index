# General Merchandise Lending

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/pawnshops|Pawnshops]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to price a used branded item — a drill kit, a games console, a guitar, a laptop — from its exact model and condition against what that model actually resells for locally, in seconds at the counter, and whoever prices it closest to its realised resale takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$2–3B of US pawn revenue from loans on, and sales of, tools, electronics, instruments, gaming and other goods (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~35–45% of revenue and most tickets by count (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Low — clerks look up eBay sold listings on their phones; the POS records a description, not a valuation
**Target Buyer:** Owner-pawnbroker; Director of Lending at a chain
**Automation Potential:** High for identification and comparables, Medium for condition

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Unlike gold, general merchandise has no public price and it depreciates. Value is model-specific and moves with product cycles: a console loses much of its pawn value when its successor launches, a phone is worthless if activation-locked, a cordless tool kit holds value by brand and battery platform. The same item is worth different amounts in different towns. Each ticket is small, so the clerk's research time per item is the binding cost.

## Current Tools & Gaps
eBay sold listings and Terapeak, PriceCharting for games, Reverb's price guide for instruments, manufacturer lookups for model and serial, device blacklist checks for phones (recalled), and the chain's lending percentages by category. The gaps: no local realised prices from the store's own sales; no consistent condition grade recorded at intake; asking prices mistaken for sold prices; and no feedback from what a forfeit fetched to the clerk who lent on it.
