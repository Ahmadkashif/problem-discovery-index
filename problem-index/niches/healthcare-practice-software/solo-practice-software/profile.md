# Solo & Two-Provider Practice Software

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/healthcare-practice-software|Healthcare Practice Software]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor selling to solo practices is fighting to get a physician live, charting and billing without an implementation consultant ever touching the account — and whoever makes unassisted go-live reliable takes the segment.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.1B US software spend across solo and two-provider ambulatory practices, including the direct primary care and cash-pay segment
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~6% of ambulatory software revenue spread across the largest number of individual buyers in the category
**Digital Adoption:** Low-Medium — adoption is high in name and shallow in practice; many solo practices use a fraction of what they pay for
**Target Buyer:** The physician-owner, who is also the buyer, the implementer, the administrator and the biller
**Automation Potential:** Very High — this segment cannot afford staff for anything, which makes every administrative task a candidate

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
The defining constraint is that there is nobody else. A solo physician evaluating software has no IT staff, no practice manager to run an implementation, and no capacity to attend eight configuration calls. The enterprise-descended products are priced for a per-provider model that works and delivered through a services motion that does not: a six-week guided implementation costs the vendor more than the account is worth and costs the physician clinic time they cannot recover. So the segment is served either by products that are cheap and thin, or by products that are capable and never fully configured. Meanwhile the segment is growing, because direct primary care, cash-pay psychiatry, concierge practice and part-time practice are all expanding, and every one of those practices is a software buyer with an afternoon.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Practice Fusion, Tebra, Elation, SimplePractice, Charm and CarePaths anchor the low end, and the market is genuinely competitive on price. The gaps are in onboarding and in the parts of practice operations that assume staff. Credentialling and payer enrolment — the thing that actually decides when a new practice can bill — is almost universally outsourced to a service and is invisible inside every product, so a physician who is live in the EHR on Monday may be unable to submit a claim for four months and frequently does not understand why. Billing modules assume a biller. Eligibility, prior authorisation and patient collections all assume somebody at a front desk. Self-serve configuration exists and is abandoned at high rates, which vendors observe and rarely instrument.
