# The Physician Who Is Also the Biller

**Niche:** [[niches/healthcare-practice-software/solo-practice-software/profile|Solo & Two-Provider Practice Software]]
**Industry:** [[industries/healthcare-practice-software|Healthcare Practice Software]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Solo practice billing modules are scaled-down versions of tools designed for a professional biller, so the physician-owner is handed a denial worklist, an eligibility queue and an aging report they have neither the training nor the hours to work.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #confidence-intervals #automation #workflow-orchestration #worker-facing #revenue-impact #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor selling to solo practices is fighting to get a physician live, charting and billing without an implementation consultant ever touching the account — and whoever makes unassisted go-live reliable takes the segment.

## The Problem
A solo physician finishes clinic at six and opens the billing module. There is a denial worklist with forty items, an eligibility queue, an aging report, and a set of unposted remittances. Every one of these is a professional biller's tool with the professional biller removed. The physician does not know what CO-97 means, does not know which of the forty denials is worth twenty minutes, and does not know whether the aging report is normal. So they work the ones they understand, ignore the rest, and write off what ages past appeal. The practice's collection rate is several points below what it should be, and the physician cannot see the gap because the product reports activity rather than money left on the table.

## Why It's Still Broken
The products are built down from the group-practice version, and removing features is easier than rethinking the user. Vendors also know that the honest answer for many solo practices is to outsource billing, and several of them sell exactly that service, which makes a genuinely self-sufficient billing experience a competitor to the vendor's own higher-margin offering. And the segment is diffuse and price-sensitive, so nobody has invested in the design work that a genuinely different interface would require.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Stop presenting a worklist and start presenting a shortlist with money attached. Rank every outstanding item by expected recovery — probability of success times amount times the practice's own history — and show the top five, with a plain-language statement of what went wrong and a drafted correction or appeal ready to approve. Everything below the line is handled automatically where it safely can be, or explicitly marked as not worth the physician's time, which is a legitimate answer nobody is willing to give. Add the one report the segment never gets: what this practice is losing per month to unworked denials and untimely filings, in dollars, against what comparable practices lose. Most physicians in this segment have never seen that number and it is computable from their own data today.

## Who Feels the Pain
Physician-owners doing billing at nine at night with no training in it; their patients, who receive statements generated by a process nobody supervised; and the vendors, whose support queue absorbs the confusion.

## Impact If Fixed
Ranking by expected recovery typically shows that a handful of items carry most of the recoverable money, which turns a two-hour evening task into fifteen minutes and raises net collections by several points. The lost-revenue report is the cheapest part and the most persuasive: it converts an invisible leak into a number, which is the only form in which a busy physician will act on it.
