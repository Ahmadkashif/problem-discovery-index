# Specialty EHR Platforms

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/healthcare-practice-software|Healthcare Practice Software]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** *Not terminal as stated.* The honest sentence is that every serious competitor is fighting to make the specialty's own evidence chain arrive in the chart already structured for the claim — but that chain is a different artefact in each specialty, so the niche is decomposed into contested sub-niches below.

## Profile
**Market Size:** $4B across the specialty-vertical ambulatory EHR market
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~22% of ambulatory software revenue, concentrated in ophthalmology, dermatology, orthopaedics, cardiology and OB/GYN
**Digital Adoption:** High — these practices bought early and switch rarely, because the switching cost is the loss of specialty-specific templates and device integrations
**Target Buyer:** Founders and product leads at specialty vendors; the practice-side buyer is a physician-owner rather than an administrator
**Automation Potential:** High but specialty-shaped — the automatable work is the evidence chain, and its shape differs entirely by specialty

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Specialty practices are the segment that generic ambulatory platforms lose. A dermatology practice running a horizontal EHR spends its day defeating the software: templates written for primary care, no place to record a lesion map, a pathology result that arrives as a scanned PDF. The specialty vendors exist because that friction is worth paying to avoid, and they hold their accounts for a decade. What makes the niche analytically awkward is that its members look alike from outside and are not alike from inside. Ophthalmology, dermatology and orthopaedics are all "specialty EHR," all sold to physician-owners, all defended by templates — and the capability each one's competitors are actually fighting over is different, because the physical evidence each specialty generates is different.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Nextech, ModMed, Compulink and Eyefinity anchor the specialty market; Epic and athenahealth pursue it with configuration rather than verticalisation. Every vendor in the niche competes on template libraries and on how many device or lab interfaces it ships, both of which are countable and therefore commoditised. The gap is the same in every specialty and takes a different form in each: the evidence the physician relies on — an image, a slide, a measurement, a range-of-motion figure — enters the record as an attachment rather than as data, so it cannot drive the coding, cannot be searched, and cannot close its own loop. Which piece of evidence, and which loop, is exactly what separates the sub-niches.
