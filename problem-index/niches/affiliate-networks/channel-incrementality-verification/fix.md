# The Return on Spend That Cannot Go Down

**Niche:** [[niches/affiliate-networks/channel-incrementality-verification/profile|Channel Incrementality Verification]]
**Industry:** [[industries/affiliate-networks|Affiliate Networks]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The channel's headline metric is attributed sales divided by commission on those same sales, which is the commission rate inverted and is reported as performance.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #hypothesis-testing #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #quick-win #compliance #causal-inference
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to tell a merchant whether affiliate produced any sales at all — and whoever runs that test credibly decides whether the channel's reported return survives contact with evidence.

## The Problem
A merchant pays ten percent commission. The affiliate report shows a return of ten to one. Next year they pay eight percent and the return becomes twelve and a half to one, and someone presents this as an improvement in channel performance. The metric is arithmetically determined by the commission rate and contains no information about whether the channel produced anything. It appears in board packs, is compared against paid search and display figures that at least have a denominator of real spend on unattributed impressions, and it wins that comparison every time by construction.

## Why It's Still Broken
The metric was inherited from a period when affiliate was small and nobody scrutinised it, and it has the form of a performance measure, which is enough for most reporting purposes. Everyone presenting it benefits from it. Finance teams take marketing metrics on trust. And the alternative requires an experiment, which is a project rather than a report.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Stop reporting a tautology. Show the commission rate alongside the return, which makes the relationship between the two immediately obvious and is the fix — one extra column ends the confusion for anyone who looks. Report incremental return as the headline and attributed return as a secondary figure, since the second is an accounting statement and only the first is a performance measure. Break the number down by partner type, which reveals that the channel average is a blend of genuinely productive partnerships and interceptions. Compare against a properly measured alternative channel rather than against that channel's own inflated figure, so the comparison is like for like. Report total sales in the presence and absence of the channel from the holdout, which is the only figure a finance function should accept. State the method with every number, since an unqualified ratio in a board pack acquires an authority it has not earned. Show the trend in commission rate alongside the trend in return, which exposes the arithmetic relationship over time. Educate the finance function, because they are the audience most likely to act on this and least likely to have examined the construction. Adopt the reporting change even before the experiment, since it costs nothing and is honest. And push for an industry reporting standard, because a merchant whose affiliate team reports honestly will lose budget to one whose team does not.

## Who Feels the Pain
Finance functions allocating budget on a tautology; channels measured honestly and losing the comparison; and merchants who have never known what affiliate produced.

## Impact If Fixed
The headline metric is the commission rate inverted, and lowering the rate raises it while nothing changes. Showing the commission rate in the next column costs nothing and ends the confusion for anyone who looks at the two together.
