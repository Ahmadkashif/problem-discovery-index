# Commitment Pacing & Liquidity Planning

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/pension-funds|Pension Funds]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to forecast the programme's capital calls, distributions and NAV under stress well enough to set next year's commitment budget, and whoever keeps the plan inside its allocation ranges through a drawdown without forced sales of liquid assets takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$300M a year of pacing studies, liquidity analytics and consultant time on commitment planning for US pension plans (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~2% of pension funds' non-investment-management operating spend (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — models exist, mostly as consultant spreadsheets refreshed annually
**Target Buyer:** CIO / Head of Private Markets
**Automation Potential:** Medium — the forecast automates, the risk appetite does not

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
A pension sets a target allocation to private markets, but it controls only commitments. Calls and distributions follow the GPs' timing, and the NAV moves with markets. When public markets fall, private NAVs lag, so the private allocation jumps above its range (the "denominator effect"). Distributions slow just as benefit payments must still be made. The plan has to set this year's commitments to hit a target four to six years out without breaching ranges or selling liquid assets at the bottom. The contest is forecasting under stress. It is a different skill, buyer and clock (the annual pacing plan and the board's allocation review) from document reconciliation.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Consultants and private markets advisers produce annual pacing studies, typically on Takahashi–Alexander-style models. Monitoring platforms and benchmark providers sell cash-flow analytics. Large plans build in-house models. The gaps: models are calibrated to generic vintage curves rather than each GP's observed pacing; they are refreshed annually rather than as markets move; and they are rarely tied to the plan's own benefit cash flows.
