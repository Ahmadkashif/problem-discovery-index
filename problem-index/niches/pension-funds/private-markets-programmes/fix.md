# The Re-Up Memo That Records Only the Reasons to Approve

**Niche:** [[niches/pension-funds/private-markets-programmes/profile|Private Markets Programmes at Public Plans]]
**Industry:** [[industries/pension-funds|Pension Funds]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A memo written for a public board meeting argues for the commitment, and the reservations that would make it gradeable are left out.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #workflow-orchestration #quick-win #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** *Not terminal as stated.* The honest partial sentence is that every serious competitor is fighting to own the plan's private markets record, but it splits into two contests with different winners: reconciling what GPs report each quarter to custodian records and partnership terms before the board's review, and forecasting calls and distributions well enough to set next year's commitments without breaching allocation ranges in a drawdown. Decomposed into contested sub-niches below.

## The Problem
Re-up memos follow a template: track record, team, strategy, terms, recommendation. They are public documents prepared for a vote, so they argue a case. The officer's real uncertainties, such as "watch the new co-head" or "marks look a quarter behind", are said in the investment committee meeting and lost.

## Why It's Still Broken
Writing reservations into a public document feels like creating a record against yourself. No workflow asks for them in a structured way, and nothing later compares them with outcomes, so there is no reward for recording them.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Add a short internal section to every recommendation, kept in the investment office's working files under the plan's records rules. It lists the top three concerns from a fixed category list, the signal behind each, and the officer's conviction. A language model drafts it from the officer's notes for one-minute confirmation. Two years in, the plan can start reading which concerns recurred and which preceded a weak fund.

## Who Feels the Pain
Investment officers who inherit relationships without history; CIOs asked by boards why a manager was re-upped; boards approving commitments on one-sided memos.

## Impact If Fixed
A few minutes per recommendation turns a decade of re-up decisions into a dataset the plan can learn from, at almost no cost in staff time.
