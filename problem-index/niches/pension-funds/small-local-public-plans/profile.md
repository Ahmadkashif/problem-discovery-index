# Small Local Public Plans

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/pension-funds|Pension Funds]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to run a small board's whole compliance calendar, from census data for the actuary to GASB 67/68 schedules, state filings and investment monitoring, without the plan hiring staff, and whoever keeps the most small plans off the state regulator's late-filing and non-compliance lists takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$500M a year of administration, actuarial, investment consulting, audit and legal fees paid by small locally administered public plans (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~3% of pension funds' non-investment-management operating spend (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Low — member records in spreadsheets or a municipal payroll system, board packets in email, filings prepared by outside firms
**Target Buyer:** Board chair or part-time plan administrator, often the town's finance director
**Automation Potential:** Medium — the filings and data preparation automate; the board's fiduciary decisions do not

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
The Census counts 4,632 locally administered public pension systems against 304 state-administered ones. Most of the local ones are small: a town's police plan, a fire district's plan, a county's plan with a few hundred members. They have the same obligations as a statewide system. There is an annual actuarial valuation, GASB 67 and 68 reporting for the plan and the employer, state filings in states that oversee local plans, investment policy monitoring, and trustee education requirements. But they meet them with volunteer or part-time trustees and no staff. The statewide tools and vendors are priced and designed for systems with hundreds of employees. Several states have responded by consolidating local plans' assets, so the audience is shrinking in places but remains large.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Small plans buy everything from outside firms: an actuary (see [[niches/municipal-services/public-pension-actuarial/profile|🔍 Public Pension Actuarial Services]]), an investment consultant, an auditor, a lawyer and sometimes a plan administration firm. The town's payroll system holds member data. The gaps: no single calendar of obligations; census data rebuilt by hand for each valuation; GASB schedules assembled from the actuary's and auditor's work by the finance director; and trustee decisions recorded in minutes no one can search.
