# The Buyer Who Wanted to Comply

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-goods-marketplaces/licence-expression-and-enforcement/profile|Licence Expression & Enforcement]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-goods-marketplaces|Digital Goods Marketplaces]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The buyer read the licence, intended to follow it, and breached anyway, because eighteen months later nothing in their project recorded which asset came with which terms.
**Tags:** #compliance #workflow-orchestration #automation #quick-win #evaluation-metrics #data-integration #descriptive-statistics #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to make a licence something the delivery path can read and check rather than prose in an agreement — and whoever does that lets compliance happen by default instead of by goodwill.

## The Problem
The overwhelming majority of licence breaches in this category are committed by people who intended to comply. They bought a personal licence for something that later became a client project. They downloaded a font in one job and reused it in another. They handed a project folder to a colleague along with its assets. At no point did they decide to breach; they simply lost track, because tracking was left entirely to human memory across eighteen-month projects and dozens of purchases. The platform treats the result as an enforcement problem, when it is a records problem it created by not keeping any.

## Why It's Still Broken
The licence is delivered as a document at purchase and never appears again. Files carry no indication of their terms once they are in a folder. The category assumes the buyer will maintain a register, which nobody does. And it is easier to pursue a breach than to prevent it, so prevention has no owner.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Keep the record so the buyer does not have to. Maintain a per-buyer licence register — every asset, its terms, and where it was used — which is the fix and is entirely within the platform's existing data; the buyer's memory was never the right place for it. Embed the identifier and a plain terms summary in the delivered file, so the terms are present wherever the file ends up rather than in an email from two years ago. Show the register inside the buyer's tools, since a record they must go and look up is a record they will not consult. Prompt at the moments where scope changes — a new project, a new client, a handover — which is when almost every inadvertent breach is committed and where a single question prevents it. Let the buyer declare a use and get an immediate answer on whether it is permitted, which is the question they actually have and currently cannot ask anyone. Offer the upgrade in that moment, since a buyer told they need a commercial licence while they are working will usually buy one, and the same buyer discovered a year later will argue. Support team-wide registers, because assets are shared within organisations and individual entitlements do not reflect that. Warn on handover that assets in a delivered folder carry terms, which is a common and entirely inadvertent breach. Make the register exportable for the buyer's own compliance records, which agencies genuinely need and cannot produce. And measure prevented breaches and upgrade conversions rather than enforcement actions, because the first is worth more to everyone including the creator.

## Who Feels the Pain
Buyers who wanted to comply and could not; creators whose terms are exceeded by customers rather than by pirates; and platforms adjudicating disputes their own record-keeping caused.

## Impact If Fixed
Most breaches are committed by people who intended to comply and lost track, because the platform left the record to human memory across eighteen-month projects. A per-buyer register surfaced in the tools, plus a prompt at scope changes, prevents the breach and converts it into an upgrade.
