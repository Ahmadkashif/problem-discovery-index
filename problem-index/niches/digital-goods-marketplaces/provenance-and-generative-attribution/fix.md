# The Buyer Whose Client Asked Where It Came From

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-goods-marketplaces/provenance-and-generative-attribution/profile|Provenance & Generative Attribution]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-goods-marketplaces|Digital Goods Marketplaces]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** An agency is asked to confirm no generated content was used in a campaign, has eleven purchased assets, and cannot confirm anything about any of them.
**Tags:** #compliance #workflow-orchestration #evaluation-metrics #quick-win #automation #data-integration #descriptive-statistics #confidence-intervals
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to establish what an asset was made from before it is listed for sale — and whoever can answer that decides what a marketplace is allowed to sell.

## The Problem
A client's legal team asks the agency to confirm the provenance of every asset in a campaign. The agency bought eleven items from four marketplaces. Each purchase page said something vague or nothing. The creators are unreachable or unsure. The agency cannot answer, so they either give an assurance they cannot support, or strip the assets and redo the work, or lose the account. The requirement is becoming routine in advertising, publishing, broadcast and games, and the marketplaces supplying the assets have no mechanism to answer a question their customers are now being asked constantly.

## Why It's Still Broken
Marketplaces sell to the creator's customer and have not noticed that customer's customer is now imposing requirements. Provenance was framed as a policy compliance matter rather than as a buyer-facing product feature, which put it with legal instead of with product. Retrofitting statements onto an existing catalogue requires admitting what is unknown. And the requirement is new enough that nobody has been penalised yet.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Give the buyer something they can hand over. Issue a provenance statement with every purchase, stating what is known, what is declared and what is unverified, which is the fix — an honest three-state statement is immediately usable where silence is not. Record the creator's declaration in a structured, durable, attributable form rather than as a checkbox result, so there is something to stand behind. Let buyers request provenance on past purchases, since the requirement arrives after the fact and the entire current problem is retrospective. Route the request to the creator with a structured question, which is far more likely to produce an answer than a buyer emailing a stranger. Mark the catalogue's unknown portion honestly instead of implying verification that has not happened, because an overstated assurance is worse for the buyer than an acknowledged gap. Offer a verified tier for creators who record provenance at creation, which gives buyers something to filter on and creators a commercial reason to participate. Support the buyer's own downstream statement by composing the provenance of every asset in a project, which is the artefact the client actually asks for. Track which industries and clients are imposing requirements, so the platform can prioritise. Warn buyers before purchase when an asset has no provenance record, which is far cheaper than discovering it at campaign sign-off. And measure what share of sales can be accompanied by a usable statement, because that figure will determine which marketplaces enterprise buyers are permitted to use at all.

## Who Feels the Pain
Agencies unable to answer a routine client question; creators losing enterprise sales for want of a record; and marketplaces quietly becoming unusable to the highest-value buyers.

## Impact If Fixed
Marketplaces have not noticed their customers' customers now impose provenance requirements, and the whole problem is retrospective. An honest three-state statement with every purchase is immediately usable where silence is not, and composing them across a project produces the artefact the client asks for.
