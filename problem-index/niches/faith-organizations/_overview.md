## Why These Niches

Megachurches and small rural congregations together represent the vast majority of US religious attendance and giving — megachurches through concentrated resources and rural churches through sheer volume (250,000+ congregations under 100 members). Mosques and church-run preschools are digitally neglected: mosques lack faith-specific software designed for Islamic practices (zakat calculations, Ramadan scheduling, Friday khutbah management), while church preschools operate in a regulatory gray zone between ministry and licensed childcare with inadequate tools for either. Online worship and facility rental represent underserved use cases that exploded post-COVID but lack purpose-built platforms. Tithing/pledge management and volunteer scheduling are the highest-volume automatable workflows — every congregation does both, and most do them manually. Excluded: denominational headquarters (enterprise operations), parachurch organizations (different funding model), and seminary/theological education.

## Why These Pockets

**No qualifiers**, the third industry in the sweep to produce none, and the reason is different again from dental (compliance) and estate planning (scale).

Faith organizations are a $130B sector that is almost entirely invisible as data. Congregations are largely exempt from federal information filing, so the nonprofit data infrastructure that covers the rest of the sector has a hole precisely here. Nothing in the chain is structured to sell insight, because almost nothing in the chain is structured to sell anything: denominational research offices, academic centres, and the translation organizations are funded by denominations, grants, and donors respectively, and every one of them publishes free by design.

What that produces is a sector where the best data belongs to whoever happens to be transacting with congregations for another reason. Church management platforms hold giving and attendance across tens of thousands of congregations — effectively the only substantial dataset on the industry — and sell software subscriptions. Specialty insurers hold claim experience across hundreds of thousands of congregations, which is the only real record of congregational risk, and sell insurance. Capital campaign firms hold feasibility studies joined to what congregations actually raised, which would make campaign forecasting evidence-based, and are paid for running campaigns.

Two findings are worth recording beyond the score. The most consequential research in the sector — abuse prevention risk accreditation, which insurers increasingly require before writing coverage — rests on case material too sensitive to work with freely, which is the correct constraint and a real one. And the single most irreplaceable dataset encountered anywhere in this entire sweep sits here: field linguistics organizations employ up to three thousand people documenting minority languages, in many cases producing the only record that will ever exist, and the organization is constituted to give it away.
