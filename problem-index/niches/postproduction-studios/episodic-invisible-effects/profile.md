# Episodic & Invisible Effects

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/postproduction-studios|Postproduction Studios]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this sub-niche is fighting to turn around hundreds of cleanup, screen-replacement and set-extension shots per episode inside a weekly post schedule at a known cost per shot, and whoever prices those shots closest to their real effort keeps the series.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.0B in US-billed VFX for scripted series and smaller features (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~16% of US postproduction revenue (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** High — shot tracking is universal, bidding is spreadsheets
**Target Buyer:** VFX producer and owner at a boutique vendor (20–200 artists); show-side VFX supervisor and producer
**Automation Potential:** High — roto, paint, tracking and screen replacement are the most automatable VFX work

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
A streaming drama can carry ~100 to 400 VFX shots an episode (estimate), most of them "invisible": removing modern signage from a period street, replacing phone and monitor screens, painting out rigs and crew reflections, extending a set, adding muzzle flashes and blood. Each shot is small, the volume is large, and the schedule is weekly, with episodes locking in sequence. Boutique vendors win series on turnaround and price per shot, often bidding a flat rate by shot category. Their margin depends on how many shots in each category turn out harder than the category assumed.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Nuke, Mocha, Silhouette, Resolve Fusion and machine-learning roto and paint tools; ftrack or Flow Production Tracking; Excel bid sheets by category; Frame.io for review. The gaps: shot categories hide wide effort variance; client notes add scope that is never priced; and prep work is outsourced or automated without measuring which shots the automation actually saved.
