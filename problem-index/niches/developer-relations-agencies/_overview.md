# Niche Analysis — Developer Relations Agencies

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/developer-relations-agencies|Developer Relations Agencies]]

## Niche Selection

Developer relations makes a claim that is almost certainly true — credible technical advocacy drives adoption — and has never demonstrated it in a form that survives a budget review. The evidence exists in pieces that nobody joins, and the function reports talks given and community members instead. The eight niches below follow that: what the function can honestly claim, what its community and content assets actually do, and who carries the work.

## Why These Niches

Attribution and community health are where the function's credibility is decided: one is the question every budget review asks, and the other is a set of metrics that rise as a community fills with unanswered questions from people who leave. Sample maintenance and event economics are the two places where substantial spend produces unmeasured and frequently negative returns — a developer's first experience being a tutorial that no longer works, and a conference programme nobody has costed against anything. The two underserved audiences are the advocate whose credibility is simultaneously their employer's asset and their own, and the community manager running an unofficial support desk with no support tooling. The last two are mechanical: operating the community, and distributing the content.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight are terminal — each names one contest that every serious competitor is fighting over, and decomposing further would produce features rather than markets.

**Adoption attribution** is not. It names the field's central problem, and there are two routes to it with different methods, different risks and different buyers. Cohort-level measurement asks what can be established without identifying anybody — comparing adoption in markets, segments and time periods with and without advocacy activity, using aggregate exposure and holdout designs — which sacrifices precision for defensibility, requires no linking of individuals, and is the version that would survive both a budget review and a privacy review. Identity linking and privacy design asks how to connect the same pseudonymous developer across a conference badge, a community handle, a content read and a product signup, which would produce far stronger evidence and raises questions the field is right to be careful about; it is a data engineering and governance problem with a genuine chance of being the wrong thing to build. A firm can do the first this quarter and never attempt the second, and the two fail in entirely different ways — one for lack of statistical power, the other for lack of consent.

Two adjacent candidates were rejected as belonging elsewhere: **the documentation the advocacy points at** belongs to [[industries/technical-content-agencies|Technical Content Agencies]], and **paid acquisition of developers** sits with [[industries/performance-marketing-agencies|Performance Marketing Agencies]].
