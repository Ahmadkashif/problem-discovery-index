# Household & Micro-Employer Payroll

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/payroll-platforms|Payroll Platforms]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in household payroll is fighting to make a private individual a compliant employer without an accountant — and whoever gets a family from hiring to filed and paid without professional help takes the segment.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$740M US household and micro-employer payroll and tax services
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~2% of payroll revenue, against a very large number of employing households
**Digital Adoption:** Low — a substantial share of household employment is paid in cash with no filings at all
**Target Buyer:** Households employing nannies, carers and domestic staff; the workers themselves have the most at stake
**Automation Potential:** Very High — the obligations are fixed and the population has no expertise whatsoever

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
A family that employs a nanny, a home carer or a housekeeper is an employer in law, with obligations that include withholding, employer taxes, unemployment registration in their state, workers' compensation in many states, year-end forms, and increasingly domestic worker protections that specify written agreements, rest periods and overtime. The employer is a private individual with no payroll knowledge, no HR function and frequently no awareness that any of this applies. The consequence of non-compliance falls asymmetrically: the family risks penalties and, occasionally, public embarrassment; the worker loses unemployment eligibility, social security credits, disability and workers' compensation coverage, and any documented employment history — which affects their ability to get credit, housing or a mortgage, and their retirement income decades later. A very large share of household employment is paid off the books, and the party who bears most of the cost is the worker.

## Current Tools & Gaps
A small number of specialist household payroll services exist and do the job well; the mainstream payroll providers serve this segment thinly because the economics are poor; tax preparers handle year-end for families who ask. The gaps: onboarding requires a household to understand that they are an employer, which is the real barrier and is an information problem rather than a product one; state registration — unemployment insurance, workers' compensation, new hire reporting — differs by state and is the step at which most well-intentioned families abandon; domestic worker protection laws, where they exist, are unknown to nearly all household employers; and nothing serves the worker's side at all, so a domestic worker has no way to establish their own employment record if the employer does not.
