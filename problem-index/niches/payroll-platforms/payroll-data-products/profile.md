# Payroll Data Products

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/payroll-platforms|Payroll Platforms]]
**Category:** Highly Automatable
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor sitting on payroll data is fighting to turn the highest-frequency wage panel in the country into a product people pay for, without compromising employer or worker confidentiality — and whoever publishes credibly takes a market nobody currently serves.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$980M US addressable across labour market data, income verification and wage benchmarking
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~3% of payroll revenue today, against a far larger unrealised opportunity
**Digital Adoption:** Low — the data is comprehensive and the products built on it are marketing indices
**Target Buyer:** Providers themselves as the builder; economists, lenders, policymakers and employers as the market
**Automation Potential:** Very High — the data is already structured, current and complete

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Payroll providers hold the highest-frequency, highest-coverage economic dataset in the country: actual wages paid, hours worked, employment starts and ends, by jurisdiction, industry and employer size, updated every pay period across tens of millions of workers. Federal employment and earnings statistics are published monthly from surveys with substantial subsequent revisions; the providers know the answer in near real time and with far better coverage. What they publish is, at most, a quarterly index issued as marketing. This is the clearest instance in the vault of what Stage A called the declined join: the data is complete, in one place, and the measurement is simply not made and published, because nobody's objective function includes it. The constraints are real — employer confidentiality, worker privacy, competitive sensitivity and a legitimate reluctance to become a data business — and every one of them has an established answer in adjacent industries.

## Current Tools & Gaps
The large providers publish employment indices and occasional research; income and employment verification is a functioning business built on the same data with a long history and a mixed reputation on consumer fairness; compensation survey providers sell benchmarking assembled from employer submissions, which is slower and less accurate than payroll data would be. The gaps: no credible, methodologically documented, high-frequency labour market series is published from payroll data despite the providers being the only parties able to produce one; wage benchmarking sold to employers is survey-based when it could be actual; the verification businesses have historically operated with limited consumer transparency about what is disclosed and to whom; and no provider has established the privacy architecture — aggregation thresholds, differential privacy, secure computation — that would let them publish confidently rather than cautiously.
