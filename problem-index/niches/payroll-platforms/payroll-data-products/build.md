# The Wage Panel, Published

**Niche:** [[niches/payroll-platforms/payroll-data-products/profile|Payroll Data Products]]
**Industry:** [[industries/payroll-platforms|Payroll Platforms]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Payroll providers measure American wages and employment in near real time with coverage no survey can approach, and publish a quarterly index as marketing.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #time-series-forecasting #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #hypothesis-testing #compliance #revenue-impact #data-integration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor sitting on payroll data is fighting to turn the highest-frequency wage panel in the country into a product people pay for, without compromising employer or worker confidentiality — and whoever publishes credibly takes a market nobody currently serves.

## The Problem
Policymakers set interest rates, lenders price credit, employers set pay and researchers study labour markets using employment and earnings statistics produced from surveys, published monthly with a lag and revised substantially afterwards. The payroll providers know, by the Monday after any pay period, what actually happened to wages and hours across tens of millions of workers, by state, by industry and by employer size. They do not publish it in any form a serious user could rely on. This is not a capability gap and not a data gap; it is a decision nobody has made, and it is the widest such gap in this entire vault.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
Three genuine constraints and one absent incentive. Employer confidentiality — a series that reveals a large employer's payroll changes is a problem, and the answer is aggregation thresholds, which is well-understood statistical practice. Worker privacy — individual records must not be inferable, and the answer is established disclosure control. Competitive sensitivity — providers do not want to reveal their own customer composition, which constrains how the panel is described rather than whether it can be published. And the absent incentive: the data business is small relative to the processing business, the risk of getting it wrong is reputational, and no product leader's objective function includes publishing labour statistics. That last one is the actual reason.

## What to Build
A methodologically serious labour market series with published methods and known limitations. Employment levels and flows, hours, earnings and wage growth, by geography, industry and employer size, at pay-period frequency, with the sample's composition characterised honestly — because a payroll provider's client base is not a random sample of American employers and a credible series must say so and correct for it. Disclosure control through established methods with the thresholds published. Revision policy stated in advance. Benchmark comparison against the official statistics, which is how the series earns trust and is also genuinely useful — a high-frequency series that leads a monthly survey by three weeks is valuable precisely because the relationship between them can be characterised. And the derived products follow: wage benchmarking for employers from actual pay rather than survey submissions, which is a better product than what exists; and research access for economists, which is both a public good and the fastest route to methodological credibility.

## Target Customer
The payroll providers themselves as the only possible builders; economists, central banks, lenders, policymakers and employers as the market.

## Impact If Built
A credible high-frequency wage and employment series would be a genuinely useful public and commercial instrument, available to nobody else, and the providers have declined to build it for reasons that are mostly about organisational attention rather than about capability or risk. The employer benchmarking product alone — actual pay rather than survey — is a better version of an existing market, and it makes the pay equity and compression work elsewhere in this vault far more accurate.
