# Moving the Threshold After the Call

**Niche:** [[niches/payment-fraud-vendors/the-risk-strategist/profile|The Risk Strategist]]
**Industry:** [[industries/payment-fraud-vendors|Payment Fraud Vendors]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A merchant complains on Tuesday, the threshold moves on Wednesday, and nobody records what happened next.
**Tags:** #quick-win #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #workflow-orchestration #confidence-intervals #automation #worker-facing #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to let a strategist see both sides of a threshold move before they make it — and whoever gives them that turns a reactive craft into a measured discipline.

## The Problem
Policy changes are made in response to whatever arrived most recently: an escalated merchant call, a loss spike, an account manager's request. The change is made, and then nothing. No baseline was recorded, no expected effect was stated, no review date was set, and no outcome was attributed. Six months later the threshold has been moved eleven times by four people and nobody can describe the cumulative effect or defend any individual move.

## Why It's Still Broken
Changes are made under pressure to resolve an immediate complaint, so recording a hypothesis feels like ceremony — and an action taken to stop a phone call is not naturally accompanied by a measurement plan. The effect is delayed and confounded by everything else changing. Nobody owns the change log. And the strategist is rewarded for responsiveness.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Record the hypothesis and check it later. Log every policy change with its reason, its expected effect and a review date, which is the fix and takes a minute at the moment of change. Capture the baseline automatically before the change takes effect, since comparing afterwards is impossible without it. Review at the set date and record what actually happened, as that single habit turns eleven undocumented moves into an evidence base. Report the cumulative effect of all changes over a period, which nobody currently sees and which will frequently be surprising. Attribute changes to their trigger — merchant complaint, loss spike, planned work — because the proportion that are reactive is itself a finding. Roll changes out on a traffic slice where volume allows, so the comparison is concurrent rather than sequential. Flag changes that were never reviewed, which will be most of them initially. Keep an owner per change even as people move on. Show the change history on the threshold itself, so anyone looking sees how it got there. And report policy stability, since a threshold moved eleven times in six months is itself a signal about the process.

## Who Feels the Pain
Strategists defending decisions they cannot reconstruct; merchants experiencing policy that oscillates; data teams whose model evaluation is confounded by unlogged changes; and leadership with no view of cumulative policy drift.

## Impact If Fixed
An action taken to stop a phone call is not naturally accompanied by a measurement plan, so the hypothesis is never recorded. Logging the reason, the expected effect and a review date turns a reactive sequence into an evidence base.
