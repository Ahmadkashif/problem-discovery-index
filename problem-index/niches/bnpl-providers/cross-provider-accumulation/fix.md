# The Product That Does Not Furnish

**Niche:** [[niches/bnpl-providers/cross-provider-accumulation/profile|Cross-Provider Accumulation]]
**Industry:** [[industries/bnpl-providers|BNPL Providers]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The longer interest-bearing instalment products furnish to the bureaus and the short pay-in-four plans mostly do not, which is exactly backwards relative to where the accumulation risk sits.
**Tags:** #compliance #data-integration #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #quick-win #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #graph-theory
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to make concurrent obligations across providers visible to somebody before the regulator makes it visible to everyone — and whoever assembles that sharing defines the sector's terms rather than receiving them.

## The Problem
Furnishing practice across the sector is uneven, and the pattern is unhelpful: the longer-term interest-bearing products, which look most like conventional credit, are more likely to be reported, while the short pay-in-four plans, where a consumer can accumulate several in a day, largely are not. So the reporting covers the obligations that are already visible in other ways and omits the ones that are invisible everywhere else. The gap in the data is precisely aligned with the gap in everyone's knowledge, and it arose from each product being treated according to how closely it resembles traditional credit rather than according to where the risk is.

## Why It's Still Broken
Furnishing decisions followed product resemblance rather than risk, because the question asked was whether this looks like reportable credit rather than whether reporting it would help — the framing determined the outcome and nobody reframed it. Reporting a six-week obligation into a monthly-cycle format is awkward and the awkwardness became a reason. A consumer's short plan appearing on their file has consequences the sector is cautious about. And no provider wants to be first.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Furnish where the risk is. Report the short instalment products, which is the fix and directly closes the gap that matters rather than the one that is convenient. Agree a format for six-week obligations with the bureaus, since the format problem is real and solvable and is currently doing duty as an excuse. Report the obligation during its life rather than only its outcome, because an obligation discharged before the next reporting cycle never appears and those are exactly the ones accumulating. Coordinate the timing across providers, since a staggered adoption leaves the same blind spot in a smaller form. Address the consumer impact honestly, since a short plan appearing on a file has consequences that should be designed for rather than avoided by not reporting. Report positive repayment as well as delinquency, which benefits the consumers who repay and is a strong argument for participation. Start with the accumulation-relevant fields rather than a full furnishing specification, since a minimal timely report is worth more than a complete slow one. Move collectively through an industry body so no provider is first alone. Measure what the furnishing reveals once it exists, because the sector's own exposure is currently unknown to it. And publish the sector's aggregate position, since the alternative is a regulator publishing an estimate from worse data.

## Who Feels the Pain
Consumers accumulating invisible obligations; providers underwriting into a gap they created; and the sector, whose least defensible fact is that its reporting omits precisely what matters.

## Impact If Fixed
Furnishing followed product resemblance rather than risk, so the reporting covers what was already visible and omits what is invisible everywhere. Reporting short instalment obligations during their life closes the gap that matters rather than the one that is convenient.
