# A Boundary Held by Individual Judgement

**Niche:** [[niches/open-source-commercial-vendors/dual-audience-support/profile|Dual-Audience Support]]
**Industry:** [[industries/open-source-commercial-vendors|Open Source Commercial Vendors]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Nobody has written down what paid support actually provides that community support does not, so every support engineer decides it themselves, differently, in every interaction.

**Contested on:** Every serious competitor that takes this seriously is fighting to let one answer serve both a paying customer and a public community without the support engineer holding the boundary personally — and whoever does that takes the support organisation, because that boundary is the job's defining discomfort.

## The Problem
A support engineer is asked, in a public issue, a question they have just answered for a customer. What is the policy? There is none. The contract describes response times and escalation paths and says nothing about whether the knowledge is exclusive. So the engineer decides, and their colleague decides differently, and the community experiences inconsistent generosity, and the paying customer occasionally sees their answer published for free, and the engineer carries the discomfort of an undefined boundary they are personally enforcing.

## Why It's Still Broken
The commercial boundary was defined in contract terms, which are about service levels, and the operational question of what to say in public was never derived from it. Writing the policy down is uncomfortable, because an explicit statement that some knowledge is withheld sounds worse than an implicit practice of withholding it — even though the implicit version is the one causing the harm. And the people affected are support engineers, whose discomfort is not a metric anybody reports.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Write the policy, and make it one the engineers can defend. Define the entitlement as service rather than as knowledge: guaranteed response times, named escalation, dedicated engineers, private context handling, advisory time and priority on fixes — all of which are genuinely valuable, genuinely exclusive and entirely defensible, and none of which requires withholding an answer. State explicitly that technical answers are public by default, which is what most engineers do anyway and what the community expects, and removes the discomfort by removing the ambiguity. Identify the narrow exceptions — anything containing customer context, anything under embargo, anything tied to a commercial feature — as a short and specific list rather than as a judgement call. Publish the policy to the community as well as internally, since the unstated boundary causes as much frustration outside as inside. Set response expectations on the public side explicitly, because the absence of a stated expectation is what produces the entitled behaviour the maintainer niche describes. And measure consistency, since a policy nobody follows is the situation being replaced.

## Who Feels the Pain
Support engineers making the same uncomfortable judgement repeatedly; community members receiving inconsistent help; and paying customers unsure what they are actually buying.

## Impact If Fixed
Defining the entitlement as service rather than as knowledge is a policy statement that costs nothing and removes the discomfort entirely, because it describes what the company actually sells. Publishing it externally addresses the frustration on the community side at the same time.
