# The Community Reimplementation Nobody Was Watching

**One-liner:** A paid feature is rebuilt in the open by a frustrated community, which is the open-core model's characteristic failure, and the vendor learns about it when it is already widely adopted.

**Contested on:** Every serious competitor here is fighting to draw a line between free and paid that customers accept and the community tolerates — and whoever can locate that line from evidence takes the model, because it is currently redrawn every two years by argument.

## The Problem
A commercial feature is genuinely useful and is priced at a level that excludes smaller users. One of them builds an open equivalent as a plugin and publishes it. It is worse initially, improves over eighteen months, gains contributors who are also frustrated, and eventually becomes good enough that mid-sized prospects mention it in sales conversations. The vendor becomes aware of it at that point. The entire trajectory — the initial publication, the early adoption, the accelerating contribution — was public from the first commit and was watched by nobody.

## Why It's Still Broken
Competitive monitoring at these companies is oriented to commercial competitors, and a community project is not filed as one until it is. The ecosystem around a popular project is large enough that nobody can watch all of it manually. The signals are public and scattered across repositories, package registries and discussions. And there is a mild institutional reluctance to look, since a community reimplementation is evidence that the boundary is wrong and nobody enjoys collecting that.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Watch the ecosystem systematically, which is entirely public. Monitor the project's plugin and extension ecosystem for anything that overlaps a commercial feature, which is detectable from descriptions, dependencies and code, and is a search rather than an investigation. Track each candidate's trajectory — contributors, adoption, release cadence, issue activity — since the useful signal is acceleration rather than existence and most such projects go nowhere. Read the issue and discussion corpus for the demand that precedes a reimplementation, since these projects are almost always preceded by a thread asking why the feature is commercial. Treat an accelerating reimplementation as a boundary signal rather than as a competitive threat, because it is evidence that the line is in the wrong place and the productive responses are to move the line, to contribute, or to improve the commercial version — and treating it as a threat produces the worst of the available responses. Report it early enough to have those options, which requires watching the trajectory rather than the outcome. And record what happened, since each instance is evidence about where the boundary can and cannot be held.

## Who Feels the Pain
Vendors surprised by an ecosystem project that eliminates a revenue line; communities whose frustration produced a duplication of effort; and companies whose boundary decision was wrong and who learned it two years late.

## Impact If Fixed
The entire trajectory is public from the first commit and monitoring it is a search rather than an investigation. Treating an accelerating reimplementation as a boundary signal rather than a threat is what preserves the options that still exist at that point.
