## Why These Niches

The category's defining problem is that adoption is invisible and conversion is unmeasurable. The companies deriving the most value from the software are frequently ones the vendor has never heard of, and every commercial decision is made from download counts that conflate a build cache with a production cluster. Reconstructing who is actually running the software is the largest contested surface and the precondition for every other decision in the category.

Commercial models **failed the filter as one niche**. Open core and licensing is contested on where the line between free and paid should sit and whether it can be held, against the community edition itself and against forks, and is bought by platform teams choosing whether to pay for features. Hosted service is contested on operating the software better and cheaper than a hyperscaler offering the same project, and is bought by teams who do not want to operate it at all. Different customers, different competitors, different definitions of winning. Decomposed below.

The two underdigitised areas are enterprise adoption and the unfunded base. Getting open-source software through enterprise procurement, legal review and security approval is handled by spreadsheets and lawyers. And the long tail of critical dependencies — the software everything depends on and nobody funds — remains the category's most-discussed and least-addressed structural problem.

The two underserved constituencies are the maintainer, who carries an unbounded queue from a community far larger than themselves and leaves, and the support engineer who must answer the same question in a contracted ticket and a public issue while maintaining a distinction that is commercially necessary and feels indefensible.

The automation niches are the triage machinery that has not reduced any queue, and the public corpus — issues, discussions, public code, job postings, conference talks — which is abundant, textual, scattered and unread.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. **Commercial Models** is not: it names the monetisation question rather than a contest, and the two answers to it are fought in completely different arenas. Open core and licensing is won by drawing a line that the paying customer accepts and the community tolerates, against the free edition and the possibility of a fork, and is decided by a platform team weighing features against price. Hosted service is won by operating the project better, cheaper or more conveniently than a hyperscaler offering the same software, and is decided by a team that has already chosen not to operate anything. The licence change phenomenon sits between them and belongs to the first. Decomposed into two contested sub-niches.

Two candidates were rejected. *Developer relations and community growth* was folded into Community Corpus Intelligence and Adoption Visibility, since its contest is measurement rather than activity and standing alone it would restate them. *Foundation governance* was rejected as a structural and legal arrangement rather than a capability anyone is competing to build.
