# Every Investigation Starts From the Scene, Not From the Archive

## The Problem
Forensic engineering is a pattern recognition discipline built on volume — the value of a large practice is that somebody there has seen this failure before. Whether that value is realized depends entirely on whether the expert on the current matter happens to know who that somebody is. Matters are archived by client and number, reports are documents, and the investigative substance — the failure mechanism, the evidence that distinguished it from alternatives, the analytical approach, the conclusion — is not indexed in any way that lets an expert find structurally similar prior work. So investigations are re-derived, the firm's collective experience is inaccessible, and the practical index is the memory of senior staff.

## Why It's Still Broken
Matters are confidential and the instinct is to wall them off entirely, when the transferable content is the technical pattern rather than the client's facts. Reports are produced as documents for external audiences, so the structure that would make them searchable was never captured. And expertise in this field is regarded as personal — an expert's credibility is individual and testified to individually — which has left the collective asset unbuilt even in firms of a thousand technical staff.

## What a Fix Looks Like
A technical index over the matter archive, separated from client identity: failure mechanism, subject system or component, materials, environment, the evidence that supported the conclusion, the analytical methods used, and the outcome. Built by structuring existing reports and maintained as matters close, with access controls that expose the technical pattern without exposing the engagement. An expert opening a new matter then sees the firm's prior work on structurally similar failures — what was concluded, what evidence proved decisive, and where an approach was challenged. Across the archive, the accumulated patterns support things no individual expert can do: recognizing that a mechanism is recurring across an industry or a product line, which is the earliest possible signal of a systemic defect and a genuinely valuable finding for manufacturer and insurer clients alike.

## Who Feels the Pain
Experts re-deriving investigations the firm has already done; junior staff learning by apprenticeship because there is no accessible body of worked cases; clients paying for analysis the firm has effectively performed before; and the practice, whose scale advantage is theoretical as long as the archive is unreachable.

## Impact If Fixed
Realizes the only real advantage a large forensic practice has over a solo expert, which is accumulated pattern exposure. Cross-matter mechanism detection is also a new product — systemic defect identification is what manufacturers and insurers most want and what a firm seeing thousands of failures is uniquely placed to provide.
