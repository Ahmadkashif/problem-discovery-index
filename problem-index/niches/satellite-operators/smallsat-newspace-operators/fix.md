# The Licensing Sequence That Slips the Launch

**Niche:** [[niches/satellite-operators/smallsat-newspace-operators/profile|Small Satellite & NewSpace Operators]]
**Industry:** [[industries/satellite-operators|Satellite Operators]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A startup discovers months before launch that its FCC licence, NOAA remote-sensing licence, ITU filing and launch provider's paperwork depend on each other in an order nobody wrote down.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #compliance #workflow-orchestration #automation #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to take a startup's satellites from licence to steady operations without the startup building its own ground network or 24/7 operations team, and whoever bundles ground contacts, mission operations and the regulatory paperwork into one dependable service takes the startup's ground and operations contract.

## The Problem
An imaging startup needs an FCC space station licence (or a foreign administration's), a NOAA commercial remote-sensing licence, ITU advance publication through its administration, earth-station authorisations for every ground station it uses, orbital debris mitigation showings including five-year disposal, and the launch provider's own compliance documents. Each agency's review takes time and some require others to be in hand.

## Why It's Still Broken
Founders do it once, usually for the first time; counsel bills by the hour; and agency guidance is written per agency, not as a sequence.

## What a Fix Looks Like
A licensing plan generated from the mission's parameters (orbit, bands, imaging resolution, ground stations, launch date) that lists every filing, its dependencies, typical review times and the latest safe submission date, tracked against the launch schedule.

## Who Feels the Pain
Founders and CTOs; launch providers holding manifest slots; investors watching a launch slip for paperwork.

## Impact If Fixed
Launches stop slipping for licences that could have been filed months earlier, and counsel's time goes to the hard questions.
