# The Correlation Study Presented as a Ranking Factor

**Niche:** [[niches/seo-tooling-vendors/causal-attribution-of-change/profile|Causal Attribution of Visibility Change]]
**Industry:** [[industries/seo-tooling-vendors|SEO Tooling Vendors]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The annual study finds that pages with more of something rank higher, the industry treats it as a ranking factor, and a decade of practice is built on associations nobody tested.
**Tags:** #causal-inference #hypothesis-testing #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #quick-win #compliance #monte-carlo-methods
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to answer whether what a site did worked or whether the algorithm moved — and whoever does it owns the only dataset capable of settling the customer's recurring question.

## The Problem
A vendor publishes a study: pages in the top three have on average more words, more links and more of some measured property than pages in positions eight to ten. The industry reads this as guidance and starts adding words and links. The finding is a correlation across a heavily confounded sample — better sites do more of everything — and says nothing about whether adding any of it to a given page would change its position. These studies are the foundation of a substantial part of professional practice, they are published annually, and the causal question is not addressed in any of them.

## Why It's Still Broken
Correlation studies are cheap to produce, generate attention, and demonstrate the size of the vendor's dataset, which is the real purpose — they are marketing artefacts that the industry adopted as evidence. The audience mostly cannot distinguish correlation from cause and the framing rarely helps them. A causal study might find that a popular practice does nothing, which is commercially awkward. And nobody has been held to account when the advice fails.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Say what the study establishes. Label correlational findings as correlational, prominently, which is the fix, costs nothing, and is the minimum any vendor publishing to a non-technical audience owes it. Report the obvious confounders explicitly, since site quality and authority drive nearly all of these associations and naming them changes how the finding reads. Run the causal version where the corpus permits, comparing sites that made a change against matched sites that did not, which is available and is what the data is for. Report effect sizes with intervals rather than rank correlations, since a statistically detectable association across millions of pages can be practically meaningless and the current presentation hides that. Publish negative results, which are more useful than positive ones in a discipline full of unexamined practices and which nobody currently has an incentive to release. Show the distribution rather than the average, because averages across positions conceal enormous variance and imply a precision that is not there. Test the practices the industry already believes in, which is the highest-value study available and has never been run properly. Make data and method available for others to check, which builds the credibility the category lacks. Stop presenting these as ranking factors, since that framing is the specific error that turns a correlation into a decade of practice. And measure whether acting on published guidance actually helped, which is the honest test and which no vendor has attempted.

## Who Feels the Pain
Practitioners building strategy on unexamined associations; businesses investing in practices that may do nothing; and the discipline, whose evidence base is annual correlation studies.

## Impact If Fixed
These studies are marketing artefacts the industry adopted as evidence, and the causal question is not addressed in any of them. Labelling findings correlational, naming the confounders and running the matched comparison the corpus supports would replace a decade of folklore with evidence.
