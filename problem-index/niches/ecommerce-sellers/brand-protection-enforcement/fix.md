# Takedowns Are Counted and Recurrence Is Not

**Niche:** [[niches/ecommerce-sellers/brand-protection-enforcement/profile|Brand Protection & IP Enforcement Providers]]
**Industry:** [[industries/ecommerce-sellers|E-Commerce Sellers]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The industry reports how many listings it removed and nobody reports how many came back, which means the central claim — that enforcement reduces infringement — has never been evidenced.
**Tags:** #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #change-point-detection #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #causal-inference #descriptive-statistics #data-integration #revenue-impact #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
Every provider in this segment reports takedown volume, and rights holders buy on it because there is nothing else to buy on. It is close to meaningless. A programme that removes fifty thousand listings a year while the same operators relist continuously is indistinguishable, in that metric, from one that removes ten thousand and shuts the operators down. The data needed to tell them apart — whether a removed listing reappears, how quickly, from the same operator, and what happened to the client's own sales and pricing in the affected categories — is either in the firm's own monitoring or obtainable, and it is not assembled. So the industry's whole value proposition rests on an activity count.

## Why It's Still Broken
Recurrence measurement requires stable operator identity, which the firm does not maintain, so the two gaps reinforce each other. Contracts and pricing are also structured around volume — many are priced per takedown — which makes a metric that would reduce volume commercially awkward to introduce unilaterally. And rights holders have not demanded it, because they have never been shown that the alternative exists.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Outcome measurement as the reported deliverable. Removed listings are monitored for reappearance, matched against the operator graph so a relist under a new account is recognized as recurrence rather than counted as a new detection. Time-to-relist becomes the headline enforcement metric, because it is the one that actually distinguishes a deterred operator from a briefly inconvenienced one. Where a client's own sales data is available, the analysis extends to what a rights holder ultimately cares about — whether infringing supply in a category fell, and whether the client's pricing power recovered. Reporting shifts from volume to a small set of outcome measures with honest uncertainty attached, including explicit statement of what cannot be attributed. Pricing can follow, since a provider that can demonstrate suppression is selling something a volume competitor cannot match.

## Who Feels the Pain
Rights holders funding enforcement programmes with no evidence of effect; analysts working cases whose durability nobody tracks; the provider's own product organization improving detection with no outcome signal; and the segment's credibility, which is one sceptical procurement analysis away from being challenged on exactly this point.

## Impact If Fixed
Replaces an activity metric with an effectiveness metric in a market where every competitor sells the same activity metric. It also directs enforcement effort at the operators that actually persist rather than at whichever listings are easiest to find, which is where the volume-based incentive currently points it.
