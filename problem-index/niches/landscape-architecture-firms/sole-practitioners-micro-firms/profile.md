# Sole Practitioners & Micro-Firms

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/landscape-architecture-firms|Landscape Architecture Firms]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor serving this niche is fighting to give a one- to four-person practice the second reviewer and the production capacity of a ten-person firm on every stamped set, at a price a sole practitioner can carry, and whoever does takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1B a year in revenue across self-employed landscape architects and firms of one to four people, mostly residential, small commercial and sub-consultant work (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~10% of revenue including non-employer practices (~$1B of a ~$9.6B total; estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Low-Medium — CAD is used, but at the minimum licence set; QA, estimating and business administration are informal
**Target Buyer:** The licensed principal, who is also designer, drafter, CA lead and bookkeeper
**Automation Potential:** High for production and checking; the stamp and client relationship stay human

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
BLS counts 17% of landscape architects as self-employed, and IBISWorld's count of more than 48,000 businesses against ~5,000 employer establishments (estimate) implies a very large tail of one-person practices and unlicensed designers. A sole practitioner stamps grading and planting plans with nobody to check them, prices residential and small commercial projects at fees of a few thousand dollars, and often lacks a topographic survey on small sites. Software priced and designed for 50-person firms — AutoCAD plus add-ons, enterprise project accounting — is a large share of their overhead.

## Current Tools & Gaps
AutoCAD LT or Vectorworks Landmark, SketchUp, Land F/X where affordable, QuickBooks, and a phone. Gaps: no second reviewer for grading and code compliance, no cheap topographic data for small sites, and business tools that assume hourly billing and project managers.
