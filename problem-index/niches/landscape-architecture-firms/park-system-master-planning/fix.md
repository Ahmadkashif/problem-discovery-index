# Level-of-Service Maps Rebuilt for Every Plan

**Niche:** [[niches/landscape-architecture-firms/park-system-master-planning/profile|Park System Master Planning & Engagement]]
**Industry:** [[industries/landscape-architecture-firms|Landscape Architecture Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Every park plan rebuilds the same walk-access and facility service-area maps from the agency's inconsistent inventory, and half the time goes into cleaning the inventory.
**Tags:** #graph-theory #feature-engineering #data-integration #automation #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to turn a city's contested community input into a park plan the council adopts and the capital programme funds, and whoever produces adopted, defensible plans fastest takes the account.

## The Problem
Level-of-service analysis — which households are within a 10-minute walk of a park, which areas lack a playground, a court or a trail — is central to every system plan. The analyst starts from the agency's park and amenity inventory, which is usually a spreadsheet of uneven quality, and a street network that ignores fences, highways and missing sidewalks.

## Why It's Still Broken
Agency inventories are maintained by operations staff for maintenance, not planning; amenity types and counts drift. Each firm cleans them for its plan and the clean version is not returned to the agency in a maintained form.

## What a Fix Looks Like
A standard amenity schema, an inventory audit step using aerial imagery to confirm amenities, a pedestrian network that respects barriers, and service-area analysis that runs in minutes — with the cleaned inventory delivered back to the agency as a maintained dataset.

## Who Feels the Pain
GIS analysts and planners at park planning firms; agency staff who get a different count of their own playgrounds from every consultant.

## Impact If Fixed
Weeks of analysis time cut to days per plan, and an inventory the agency can keep using after the plan is adopted.
