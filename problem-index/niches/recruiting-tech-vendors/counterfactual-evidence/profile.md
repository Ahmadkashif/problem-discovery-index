# Counterfactual Evidence

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/recruiting-tech-vendors|Recruiting Tech Vendors]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Whether an employer will hire some of the people its screen rejected in order to find out whether the screen works.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.30B — 40% of the screening and matching niche
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~11% of US recruiting technology spend
**Digital Adoption:** None — no employer runs this at scale
**Target Buyer:** Large employers with research capability; academic labour economists; regulators
**Automation Potential:** Low — the mechanism is an experiment, not a computation

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

This is the half that would settle the question and that nobody runs. The counterfactual is structurally unavailable because rejected candidates generate no performance data — so the only way to obtain it is to advance some candidates the screen would have rejected and observe how they do.

Done properly this means randomisation at the margin of the screening threshold: among candidates near the cut, some proportion are advanced regardless of the screen's verdict, and their subsequent outcomes are compared to those of candidates above the cut. That design produces genuine evidence about screening validity and it is the only design that does.

The obstacles are what make it a distinct market. It requires an employer to deliberately hire people its process rejected, which is a decision at the level of a chief executive rather than a recruiting leader. The sample takes years to accumulate. Randomising employment prospects raises ethical and legal questions nobody wants to be first on. And the finding may be that the screen adds nothing, which is a conclusion with consequences for a large budget.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Academic labour economics has run audit studies and field experiments in hiring, principally on discrimination rather than on screening validity, and those designs are the closest existing precedent. Some employers run limited pilots — a blind-screening trial, a relaxed-requirement requisition — without randomisation or measurement. Several published studies have found that relaxing specific requirements does not reduce performance.

The gaps are that no employer runs a continuing randomised marginal-admission programme, that no vendor supports one, and that the ethical and governance framework for doing it responsibly has not been written.
