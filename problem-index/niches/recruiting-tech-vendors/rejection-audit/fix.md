# Fix: Nobody Has Ever Looked at the Rejections

**Niche:** [[niches/recruiting-tech-vendors/rejection-audit/profile|Rejection Audit]]
**Industry:** [[industries/recruiting-tech-vendors|Recruiting Tech Vendors]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Ninety-eight percent of applicants are rejected and not one of those decisions has ever been reviewed by anybody.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #hypothesis-testing #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #workflow-orchestration #quick-win #compliance #worker-facing
**Contested on:** Whether anyone will read fifty rejected applications.

## The Problem

A requisition receives six hundred applications. Twelve are interviewed. The other five hundred and eighty-eight are rejected, most of them by a filter or a ten-second scan, and nobody ever looks at any of them again.

This is not a measurement gap requiring a methodology. It is that nobody has read them. A recruiting leader who spent two hours reading fifty rejected applications from a role they are struggling to fill would learn more about their screening than any dashboard has told them, and almost none has ever done it.

The reason the finding would be striking is that screening failures are systematic rather than random. A filter discarding a job title synonym, a knockout question catching legitimate candidates, a requirement nobody meant literally — each of these discards a category of people, consistently, and reading fifty applications surfaces the pattern immediately.

## Why It's Still Broken

Rejections are the end of a workflow. The system moves them to a status and they leave the working view, and every incentive points forward to the next requisition.

The volume also feels prohibitive — five hundred and eighty-eight is a lot — which obscures that fifty is not, and that fifty is enough.

And the finding is unwelcome in the ordinary way: a recruiting leader who discovers their screen is discarding good candidates has a problem they did not have that morning.

## What a Fix Looks Like

Read some of them. Make it routine.

Read fifty from the hardest-to-fill requisition, personally, at the leadership level, once a quarter. No methodology, no blinding, no statistics — just reading. This costs two hours and is the highest-information activity available to a recruiting leader, and its absence is remarkable.

Do it for the roles where the fill is failing. If a requisition has been open five months, the hypothesis that the screening is discarding suitable candidates is worth two hours of testing, and it is tested far more often by adding sourcing spend.

Look at what the filters removed specifically. Candidates knocked out by a single question, by a years-of-experience threshold, by a degree requirement, by a location filter. Each of these is a list the ATS can produce and each will contain people the hiring manager would want to see.

Ask the hiring manager to read ten. Their judgement about who should have reached them is the criterion, and getting it on ten specific rejected applications is far more useful than another conversation about the profile.

Then build the sampling and the blinding if the reading finds something, which it will.

And write down what you find as the requirements, because most of what this exercise surfaces is that the stated requirements do not describe what the manager actually wants.

## Who Feels the Pain

Candidates discarded by a filter nobody has examined, systematically, for reasons that would not survive a manager's glance. Recruiters, blamed for slow fills on pipelines their own screen is emptying. Hiring managers, who never see the candidates they would have chosen. And the organisation, spending on sourcing to fill a funnel it is rejecting the top of.

## Impact If Fixed

Two hours of reading surfaces the systematic screening failures that no dashboard reports and that will not be found any other way. Filter-driven exclusions become visible as lists of specific people. And the requirements get rewritten to describe what the manager actually wants, which is the underlying fix for most of it.
