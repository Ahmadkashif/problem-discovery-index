# Water & Sewer Capacity Commitments

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/land-developers|Land Developers]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to secure binding water and sewer service commitments for a tract's planned lots before the land closes, and whoever obtains binding commitments covering the highest share of planned lots before closing takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1B a year of utility engineering, capacity studies, utility-agreement legal work and capacity fees handled by developers (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~6% of receipts (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Low — capacity is negotiated by letter and meeting; the utility's models are not visible to the developer
**Target Buyer:** VP of land development, or the developer's utility engineer
**Automation Potential:** Low-Medium; screening which providers have capacity can be automated, the negotiation cannot

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
In much of the fast-growing Sunbelt the land is already zoned, or sits in an unincorporated county with little zoning, and what stops it becoming lots is water and sewer. The developer needs a binding commitment ("will-serve" letter, utility service agreement, capacity reservation) from a city, a private utility or a district. In Texas that often means a certificated provider's service area or a new MUD. In the Phoenix area, Arizona stopped issuing new groundwater-based assured water supply certificates in 2023 (recalled). The counterparty is a utility with its own plant capacity and politics, profiled from the city side under Municipal Services at [[niches/municipal-services/water-sewer-utilities/profile|Water & Sewer Utilities]], not a council voting on land use, and the consultants are utility engineers and water lawyers. That makes it a different contest from a rezoning.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Utilities run hydraulic and capacity models (InfoWater, WaterGEMS) internally. State commissions publish service-area maps. Developers' engineers request capacity studies one tract at a time. Gaps: no view of which providers near a tract have uncommitted capacity, how long commitments have taken, or which letters expire before the plat.
