# Four Customers and an Independence Claim

**Niche:** [[niches/ai-model-evaluation-firms/frontier-lab-assurance/profile|Frontier Lab Assurance]]
**Industry:** [[industries/ai-model-evaluation-firms|AI Model Evaluation Firms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A firm whose revenue comes from four labs publishes independent assessments of those labs, and the concentration is never disclosed alongside the finding.
**Tags:** #compliance #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #worker-facing #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this sub-niche is fighting to find capability or risk that the lab's own evaluation team did not — and whoever does that takes the contract, because a lab with a large internal team buys external evaluation for exactly one reason.

## The Problem
The customer base for frontier model assurance is a handful of organisations. A firm serving them derives most of its revenue from a few contracts, each renewable, each held by a party whose model the firm is assessing. Nobody in this arrangement is behaving badly and the incentive is nonetheless obvious to everyone outside it. The reports are cited by policymakers and journalists as independent findings, and the commercial relationship behind them is not stated. The field is building its credibility on an arrangement it has not disclosed.

## Why It's Still Broken
The concentration is a commercial fact nobody wants to advertise, and disclosing it weakens the report's rhetorical force even when the work is impeccable. There is no norm requiring it because the field has no professional body to set one. The labs have no reason to ask. And the firms are small and growing, so the concentration is expected to resolve itself, which is a reason to wait rather than to disclose.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Disclose the relationship with the finding. State revenue concentration and the commercial relationship in every published report — banded rather than exact, which addresses the confidentiality objection while conveying the material fact, and is a change any firm can make unilaterally this quarter. Publish the engagement terms: who chose the scope, who could veto publication, whether the fee depended on any outcome, and what the firm may not disclose, since those four facts determine how a reader should weigh the report and none are currently stated. Separate the assurance practice from advisory work for the same customer, because a firm advising a lab on its safety approach and then assessing it is the conflict the audit profession spent decades legislating against. Establish a publication right in the contract, so a firm can report a finding the customer dislikes, which is the difference between assurance and consulting. Report engagements that were declined or terminated in aggregate, which is a strong credibility signal and costs nothing to publish. Push for a shared disclosure standard across firms, since unilateral disclosure disadvantages the honest one and a common norm removes that. And be plain that concentration is structural in this market rather than a temporary condition, because pretending otherwise is what makes the eventual disclosure look like a revelation.

## Who Feels the Pain
Policymakers and the public treating these reports as independent; the firms whose genuinely rigorous work is undermined by an undisclosed structure; and the labs, whose good-faith engagement is read cynically once the arrangement surfaces.

## Impact If Fixed
Banded revenue concentration disclosed with each report conveys the material fact without breaching confidentiality, and any firm can adopt it unilaterally. Publishing the engagement terms — scope authorship, veto rights, publication rights — is what separates assurance from consulting.
