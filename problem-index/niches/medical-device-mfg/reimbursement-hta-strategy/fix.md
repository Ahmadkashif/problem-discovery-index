# Payer Behaviour Knowledge Retires With the Consultant

**Niche:** [[niches/medical-device-mfg/reimbursement-hta-strategy/profile|Reimbursement & Health Technology Assessment Strategy]]
**Industry:** [[industries/medical-device-mfg|Medical Device Manufacturing]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Knowing which medical director at which plan responds to what argument is the whole service, and it is stored in people.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #large-language-models #graph-ml #worker-facing #data-integration

## The Problem
Published policies say what a payer covers. They do not say how the payer got there or what would move it. A consultant who has worked coverage for fifteen years knows which plans require a randomized trial and which accept registry evidence, which medical directors respond to budget impact and which to clinical outcomes, which plans follow the national programme and which set their own line, how long a policy typically takes to move after a guideline endorsement, and which societies' endorsements actually carry weight with which payers.

That knowledge routes every engagement. It decides which payers to approach first, what evidence to lead with, and what the realistic timeline is.

The engagement record contains the dossier submitted and the coverage decision. The approach taken, the arguments made, the objections raised, and what the consultant learned about that payer are nowhere structured. So the next consultant working the same plan starts over, and the firm's differentiating asset walks between competitors with the people who hold it.

## Why It's Still Broken
Engagements are filed by client and are confidential, along the one axis nobody needs to search — every practitioner question is about a payer, not a client.

The market access profession is also relationship-heavy, and knowledge of a payer's behaviour is partly knowledge of individuals, which people are careful about writing down and which is a personal asset in a competitive talent market.

And the feedback loop is long. A coverage strategy plays out over two to four years, so nobody attributes an outcome to a decision cleanly enough to build a record from it — unless they decide to record the decisions as they happen.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Attach knowledge to payers, not to engagements.

**Payer profiles, maintained and dated.** Evidence standards, decision process and cadence, who influences policy, historical responsiveness to different argument types, and observed lag behind national determinations or guideline changes.

**Engagement interaction records, client-anonymized.** The approach taken, the evidence led with, the objection raised, and the outcome. The reusable fact is about the payer, and it carries no client identity.

**Link to the policy corpus.** Where a policy changed after an engagement, connect the two. Over several years this is the only evidence anyone in this field has about which arguments actually move coverage.

**Retrieval at strategy.** A consultant planning payer sequencing should see the firm's accumulated experience with each plan rather than asking whoever has worked it recently.

**Record the prediction.** What the consultant expects each payer to do and on what timeline, recorded at the outset. Checking those against outcomes is how the firm learns whose judgment is reliable and about what.

## Who Feels the Pain
Consultants, rebuilding payer knowledge colleagues already have. New market access professionals, who need years to be credible. Practice leaders, whose capability is a small number of careers in a mobile market. And device companies, whose coverage timeline — and therefore commercial viability — depends on which consultant they drew.

## Impact If Fixed
Coverage determines whether a cleared device has a market, and coverage strategy runs on individual memory of payer behaviour. Making that knowledge institutional compresses the years it takes to develop a market access consultant, makes payer sequencing evidence-based, and builds the one asset in this practice that cannot be hired away a person at a time.
