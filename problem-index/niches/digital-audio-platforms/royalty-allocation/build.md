# Computing the Argument

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-audio-platforms/royalty-allocation/profile|Royalty Allocation]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-audio-platforms|Digital Audio Platforms]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Every quantity needed to settle the industry's central economic argument sits in the platform's own logs and has never been published.
**Tags:** #monte-carlo-methods #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #descriptive-statistics #revenue-impact #compliance #hypothesis-testing #causal-inference
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to decide how a subscription fee is divided and whether the streams it is divided by were real — and the contest splits cleanly enough that it is not terminal.

## The Problem
The platform knows exactly what every subscriber listened to, for how long, and in what context, and then divides the money by a formula that deliberately discards most of that information. What each artist would earn under a user-centric allocation, what the fraud rate actually is, what the minimum threshold policies reallocated and to whom — all of these are computable from existing logs. The argument has run for a decade on assertion because nobody has been willing to compute and publish the numbers.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
The pro-rata pool was a licensing convenience that became an entrenched settlement, so changing it requires renegotiating with every licensor at once — an arrangement that is expensive to change is defended by everyone who benefits and by the difficulty itself. Publishing the alternative allocation would show who gains and who loses, which is precisely why it has not been published. Licensors' agreements constrain what platforms may disclose. And the parties who lose from transparency are the ones with the leverage.

## What to Build
Compute the alternatives and detect the manipulation. Compute what each rights holder would earn under user-centric and other allocations, which is one half and is arithmetic over data the platform already has. Detect and quantify stream manipulation, which is the other half and is what determines whether the pool is being divided against real listening at all. Publish the comparison with methodology, since the number is the argument and its absence is why the debate is unresolved. Measure what the threshold policies actually reallocated, because they moved real money and the effect was asserted rather than reported. Quantify the fraud rate and its redistribution effect, as fraud in a fixed pool is a transfer from identifiable other artists. Model the transition costs honestly, since a change in allocation has losers and pretending otherwise discredits the case. Make the calculation auditable by a third party, because a self-computed number in a contested settlement carries limited weight. Report the distributional effects by artist size and genre, which is what the argument is actually about. Separate the allocation question from the integrity question, as the decomposition below, since conflating them muddles both. And accept that the answer may be uncomfortable, because that is why it has not been computed.

## Target Customer
Executive and rights leadership, artists and their representatives, licensors and collecting bodies, and policymakers examining streaming economics.

## Impact If Built
An arrangement that is expensive to change is defended by everyone who benefits and by the difficulty itself. Computing and publishing what each allocation would pay converts a decade of assertion into a settled set of numbers.
