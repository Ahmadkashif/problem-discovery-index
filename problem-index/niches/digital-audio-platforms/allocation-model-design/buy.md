## The Problem
Evaluating a rule for dividing resources is what mechanism design and policy analysis do: assess the incentives the rule creates, model the distributional consequences, check for manipulability, and compare candidate rules on stated criteria. The methods are standard and are routinely applied to auctions, taxation, matching markets and subsidy allocation. The rule dividing eighteen billion dollars of music revenue was chosen because it was easy to administer and has never been analysed this way in public.

## What Already Exists
Mechanism design criteria and incentive analysis; distributional impact assessment; manipulability and strategyproofness analysis; simulation of candidate rules; and policy evaluation methodology.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to a rule already entrenched in bilateral contracts. It requires: (1) a mechanism that cannot be changed unilaterally, since it is embedded in licensing agreements with every major rights holder — the analysis therefore has to persuade rather than prescribe, which is the substantive difference; (2) manipulability that is not theoretical but an active industry, so strategyproofness is a first-order criterion rather than an elegance concern; (3) data held by the interested party, which makes independent analysis dependent on disclosure; (4) distributional consequences across hundreds of thousands of rights holders with very different bargaining power; and (5) an outcome that is a commercial negotiation rather than a policy decision.

## Target Customer
Rights and policy leadership, licensors, artist bodies, regulators, and academic economists.

## Impact If Solved
Economics evaluates allocation rules as a matter of routine and this one was never subjected to it. Manipulability is a first-order criterion here rather than a theoretical nicety, which is itself a strong argument the analysis would surface.
