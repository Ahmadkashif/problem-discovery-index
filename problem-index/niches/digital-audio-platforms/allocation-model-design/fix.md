# A Policy Change With No Numbers

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-audio-platforms/allocation-model-design/profile|Allocation Model Design]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-audio-platforms|Digital Audio Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A minimum stream threshold was introduced, real money moved, and the only account of its effect is the announcement that preceded it.
**Tags:** #quick-win #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #compliance #hypothesis-testing #automation
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to establish what each allocation model would actually pay each rights holder — and whoever computes and publishes it first moves an argument that has run on assertion for a decade.

## The Problem
A policy is announced — a minimum annual stream count before a track earns, a minimum play duration for certain audio — with a rationale about fraud or fairness. It takes effect. Money that previously went to one group now goes to another. No measurement of the actual reallocation is published, so the affected artists learn about it from their statements and the sector debates the change on the basis of the press release that preceded it.

## Why It's Still Broken
The announcement precedes the change and nothing requires a report afterwards, so the rationale is the only account — a policy with a stated intention and no measured outcome is evaluated on its intention forever. Measuring it would name the winners and losers. Nobody has asked for the report. And the affected artists individually have too little visibility to construct it.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Report what the change actually did. Measure the reallocation by artist segment after the change, which is the fix and is a straightforward before-and-after over the platform's own payouts. Report how much money moved and to whom, since that is the question everyone is asking and nobody is answering. Test the stated rationale, as a threshold justified by fraud reduction should be shown to have reduced fraud. Report the effect on the artists it was expected to affect, because the claim that the amounts were trivially small is checkable. Publish before the next change, so the sector can evaluate a proposal rather than react to a result. Tell affected rights holders individually what changed for their catalogue, which is what they most want and is per-account arithmetic. Distinguish the policy's effect from ordinary variation, since artists currently attribute every change to the policy. Model the proposal before implementing it and share the projection, which is the standard anyone else would be held to. Invite independent verification, as a self-reported effect on a self-chosen policy carries limited weight. And commit to reporting on future changes, which is what makes the next debate evidential.

## Who Feels the Pain
Artists whose income changed for reasons nobody quantified; distributors explaining a change they cannot measure; the sector debating on press releases; and platforms whose stated rationale is untested and therefore disbelieved.

## Impact If Fixed
A policy with a stated intention and no measured outcome is evaluated on its intention forever. A before-and-after over the platform's own payouts is straightforward and turns a contested announcement into a reported effect.
