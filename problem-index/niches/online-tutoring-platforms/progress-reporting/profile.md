# Progress Reporting to Parents

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/online-tutoring-platforms|Online Tutoring Platforms]]
**Category:** Low Digitized
**Contested on:** Whether a parent paying for months of tutoring is told anything about what happened in it beyond a charge on their card.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$720M — 9% of US online tutoring
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~9%
**Digital Adoption:** Low — a note the tutor writes unpaid, or nothing
**Target Buyer:** Parents; platform product teams; tutors
**Automation Potential:** Very high — the source material is a transcript the platform already holds

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

A parent buying tutoring is buying something they cannot observe. The sessions happen between their child and a stranger on a video call, and what the parent receives is a monthly charge and whatever the tutor volunteers.

Good tutors write progress notes. It takes fifteen minutes per student per week, is unpaid, and is among the first things dropped when a tutor is busy — so the parents of the busiest tutors' students get the least information. The platform holds the transcript of every session and generates none of it.

The niche is distinct because it is the industry's main trust mechanism and it runs on unpaid goodwill. It is also the single most automatable valuable thing in the industry: a good progress summary is a summarisation task over a transcript, which is precisely what current models do well.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Session notes fields in most platforms, optional and inconsistently used. Some platforms email a summary after each session containing the duration and the topic the tutor selected from a dropdown. Curriculum-based platforms report progress against their own content. Parent-tutor messaging exists and is the main channel in practice.

The gaps are consistency and substance. Where reporting exists it is a topic label rather than an account of what happened. Nothing tracks progress across sessions in a way a parent can see. And nothing connects the tutoring to the thing the parent actually cares about, which is school performance — because nobody asked for the grades.
