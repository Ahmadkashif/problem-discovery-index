# Session Instruction Analysis

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/online-tutoring-platforms|Online Tutoring Platforms]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Whether what a tutor actually does in a session can be observed and scored against what the teaching research says works.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.34B — 60% of the tutor effectiveness niche
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~17% of US online tutoring
**Digital Adoption:** Very low — recordings are stored and not analysed
**Target Buyer:** Platform quality and product teams; tutors themselves
**Automation Potential:** Very high — transcription and discourse analysis are mature

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

This is the half that stays inside the product. The platform records the sessions. What happens in them — who talks and how much, whether the tutor asks before explaining, how long they wait after a question, whether a wrong answer is diagnosed or simply corrected, whether the student does the work or watches it being done — is fully observable in the recording, needs nobody's permission, and maps onto a large and settled body of research about what makes teaching effective.

The distinguishing property is self-sufficiency. An instructional quality score is useful the moment it exists, as feedback to the tutor and as an input to quality review, whether or not anyone ever measures a learning outcome. It requires no assessments, no school relationship, no causal claim and no waiting for a semester to end.

It also has an immediate constituency: tutors themselves want this. Nobody working alone in one-to-one sessions ever sees their own teaching from the outside, and specific feedback on questioning and wait time is the kind of professional development this workforce has no access to at all.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Video infrastructure with recording is standard. A few platforms sample recordings for manual quality review, which is expensive and covers a negligible fraction. Transcription is cheap and widely available. Classroom discourse analysis exists as a research method and in a small number of teacher-coaching products aimed at schools.

The gap is that no tutoring marketplace analyses its own recordings systematically. The corpus is extraordinary — millions of hours of one-to-one teaching with the same students returning over months — and its only current use is dispute resolution. Automated instructional feedback, which is the obvious product, exists for classroom teachers and not here.
