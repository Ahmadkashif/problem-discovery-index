# Fix: Doing the Job Well Costs the Tutor Money

**Niche:** [[niches/online-tutoring-platforms/the-tutor/profile|The Tutor]]
**Industry:** [[industries/online-tutoring-platforms|Online Tutoring Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Preparation, marking, parent messages and progress notes are what makes tutoring work and none of them are paid, so the tutor who prepares earns less per hour than the one who improvises.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #workflow-orchestration #worker-facing #quick-win #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Whether the work that makes tutoring effective will be recognised in the price of it.

## The Problem

A per-session rate pays for the session. Everything that makes the session worth having happens outside it: reviewing the student's last piece of work, planning a sequence, finding or making a resource, marking what was set, writing the parent a note, replying to the parent's question.

A conscientious tutor spends twenty to forty minutes per session on these. A tutor who does not spends zero. Both are paid the same, so the conscientious one earns thirty to forty percent less per hour worked, and the rating system does not reliably tell them apart — a well-prepared session and a warm improvised one both get five stars.

The market therefore selects against preparation. This is not a subtle incentive problem; it is the central economic fact of the job, and every tutor knows it.

## Why It's Still Broken

Session-based pricing is what parents understand and what the platform's booking and payment infrastructure is built around. Pricing the preparation means either raising the session rate, which reduces bookings, or adding a line item, which parents resist because they cannot verify it.

The platform also does not observe the preparation, so it could not price it even if it wanted to. The work happens off-platform, unlogged, and is therefore invisible to every system that might reward it.

And the rating system cannot distinguish prepared from unprepared, so the one mechanism that could reward preparation indirectly does not.

## What a Fix Looks Like

Several things help, in rising order of difficulty, and the first two are available now.

**Reduce the unpaid work rather than pricing it.** Automated progress reports from transcripts, prepared session plans from the student record, and a diagnostic handed to the tutor before the first session together remove a large fraction of the unpaid hours. This is the most realistic fix and it is a product investment rather than a pricing change — which is why it is the one likely to happen.

**Make the preparation visible.** Let a tutor log preparation against a student, shown to the parent as part of the session record: forty minutes of preparation, the materials prepared, the marking returned. Parents who can see it value it, and tutors who do it get credit for it in the one place it matters. This alone shifts the rating dynamic that currently treats prepared and improvised sessions identically.

**Price at least some of it explicitly.** Marking and written feedback are discrete, verifiable and separable, and platforms that offer them as a priced add-on find parents buy them. That does not cover planning, and it covers something.

**Include preparation in the quoted rate honestly.** A tutor setting their rate should be told the arithmetic: at your current preparation time, a $50 session is a $31 hour. Many tutors price from what others charge without ever doing this calculation, and doing it once tends to move rates upward across a market — which is the mechanism by which the unpaid work eventually gets paid for.

**Compensate short-notice cancellations properly**, since a cancelled session is preparation already spent plus an hour that is now worth nothing. Policies in this industry are generally written around parent conversion rather than around the tutor's committed time.

## Who Feels the Pain

Tutors who prepare, who are paid less per hour worked than tutors who do not, and who eventually either stop preparing or leave. Students, who get the outcome that incentive produces. Parents, who cannot tell the difference at booking and can tell it later. And the platform, whose quality problem is generated directly by its own pricing structure.

## Impact If Fixed

The work that makes tutoring effective stops being a personal cost borne by the tutors who care most. Automating a large share of it is the realistic path and is entirely available. And making the rest visible to parents changes what the market rewards — which is the only way the incentive stops running backwards.
