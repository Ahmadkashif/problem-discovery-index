# Traffic Fell and Nobody Knows Why

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-native-publishers/the-audience-editor/profile|The Audience Editor]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-native-publishers|Digital Native Publishers]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A third of the traffic disappeared on a Tuesday and the explanation offered in the meeting is a guess.
**Tags:** #change-point-detection #quick-win #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #automation #worker-facing #data-integration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to make someone accountable for traffic from platforms that change without notice and are actively reducing supply — and whoever gives that person evidence instead of folklore changes what the role can do.

## The Problem
The drop is visible by Wednesday. By Thursday the audience editor is asked to explain it, and the available evidence is a dashboard showing the decline, a search console that reports with a delay, and forum speculation. They assemble a plausible story, the meeting accepts it, and a response is decided on the basis of a guess. Sometimes the real cause was a broken template deployed on Monday night, and nobody checked.

## Why It's Still Broken
Diagnosis requires assembling several sources quickly and nobody prepared the assembly, so every incident starts from nothing — a situation that recurs monthly and is handled from scratch each time has no runbook. Technical causes sit with a different team and are not checked first. Platform data arrives late. And the pressure to have an answer arrives before the evidence does.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Prepare the diagnosis before the next one. Build a standing checklist of candidate causes in order of likelihood, which is the fix and turns an improvised explanation into a process. Check own-site technical health first, since it is the fastest to confirm or eliminate and is a common cause nobody looks at first. Segment the drop by source, device, page type and geography immediately, because the shape of the drop identifies the cause faster than any speculation. Compare against the publisher's own seasonal baseline rather than against last week. Check whether it affects all content or a subset, as a partial drop points somewhere very different from a uniform one. Look at ranking and impression data rather than only sessions, since a drop in clicks with stable impressions means something specific. Record every incident and its eventual explanation, so the pattern accumulates instead of resetting. Share observations with peer publishers where possible, which is the fastest way to confirm a platform-wide change. Resist responding until the cause is identified, because the wrong remedy is worse than a week of waiting. And report the diagnosis with its confidence, since a stated guess is more useful than a confident fiction.

## Who Feels the Pain
Audience editors explaining events they cannot diagnose; executives making decisions from guesses; newsrooms whose strategy shifts on a misdiagnosis; and publishers who respond to a self-inflicted problem by changing their journalism.

## Impact If Fixed
A situation that recurs monthly and is handled from scratch each time has no runbook. A candidate-cause checklist with own-site health checked first turns an improvised explanation into a diagnosis, and catches the self-inflicted cases.
