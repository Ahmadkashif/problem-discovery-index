# Whose Photograph Is That

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-native-publishers/archive-licensing-and-rights/profile|Archive Licensing & Rights]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-native-publishers|Digital Native Publishers]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A licensing conversation stalls because nobody can confirm who owns the images in six years of articles.
**Tags:** #compliance #quick-win #data-integration #automation #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #workflow-orchestration #large-language-models
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to know what its archive is worth, who is using it, and on what terms — and whoever can enumerate and price that asset negotiates from evidence rather than from a position of ignorance.

## The Problem
The buyer wants a warranty that the publisher has the rights to what it is licensing. The publisher's images came from agencies with per-use licences, from freelancers with varying terms, from readers, from press releases, and from sources nobody recorded. Determining the position for any individual article is a manual investigation. Across an archive it is impossible, so the publisher either warrants something it cannot verify or narrows the deal to text it is sure about.

## Why It's Still Broken
Image rights were checked at publication and the record was the checking rather than a stored fact, so nothing queryable survives — a control performed as a step in a workflow leaves no data behind. The content system has a credit field and no terms field. The problem only became commercial recently. And retrospective reconstruction is laborious with no immediate payoff.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Record terms as data from now, and reconstruct what is cheap to reconstruct. Add structured rights fields to the content system and require them at publication, which is the fix and stops the problem growing from today. Extract what can be extracted from existing credits, agency records and contracts, since a large share of the archive is traceable to a handful of sources with standard terms. Classify the archive into clearly owned, clearly limited and unknown, because a clean partial inventory is immediately usable and a perfect one is not. Separate text from images in any licensing conversation, as the text position is usually far clearer and the deal should not be held hostage. Record freelance grant terms in a queryable form, since they are the other major source of limitation. Flag the articles that cannot be licensed rather than trying to clear them all, which focuses effort where it pays. Keep the provenance of everything going forward, as the cost is trivial at the moment of publication. Standardise freelance and image terms in new contracts, because non-standard terms are what make the archive unclearable. Report the share of the archive that is clean, which is the number every negotiation needs. And do the reconstruction in order of commercial value rather than chronologically.

## Who Feels the Pain
Business development teams unable to warrant what they are selling; legal teams doing manual archaeology; publishers narrowing deals to what they can prove; and freelancers and photographers whose terms nobody recorded.

## Impact If Fixed
A control performed as a step in a workflow leaves no data behind, so rights checking left no record. Structured rights fields from today plus a value-ordered reconstruction produces a clean partial inventory that is immediately licensable.
