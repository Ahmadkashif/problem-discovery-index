# Knowing What You Own and What It Is Worth

**Niche:** [[niches/digital-native-publishers/archive-licensing-and-rights/profile|Archive Licensing & Rights]]
**Industry:** [[industries/digital-native-publishers|Digital Native Publishers]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** The archive became the publisher's most discussed asset and nobody can produce an inventory of what is in it or who owns each piece.
**Tags:** #data-integration #large-language-models #compliance #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #automation #graph-theory #confidence-intervals
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to know what its archive is worth, who is using it, and on what terms — and whoever can enumerate and price that asset negotiates from evidence rather than from a position of ignorance.

## The Problem
A publisher sitting down to license its archive discovers it cannot describe it. Which articles were written by staff and which by freelancers with limited grants, which images are licensed for a single use, which content was syndicated in and cannot be relicensed, which pieces contain third-party material — none of this is recorded in a form anyone can query. The asset everyone is now talking about is unenumerable, unpriced, and being used by parties the publisher cannot identify.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
Rights were handled per piece at the moment of publication, so the record lives in contracts and emails rather than in the content system — an asset that was never expected to be sold does not acquire an inventory. The archive had little standalone value for twenty years. Detection of external use was impractical. And the legal questions are unsettled, which makes the commercial work feel premature when it is the opposite.

## What to Build
Inventory it, price it, and watch it. Build a rights inventory across the archive by extracting terms from contracts and metadata, which is the core and is the precondition for every negotiation. Classify each piece by what can and cannot be licensed, since a partial archive cleanly described is far more valuable than a whole one that cannot be warranted. Record freelance, image and syndication terms in a queryable form going forward, as the accumulation problem otherwise continues. Value the archive by segment, because a specialist archive in a defined domain is worth far more per item than general coverage and publishers price it as one block. Detect external use where it is detectable, since evidence of use is the strongest negotiating position available. Structure the archive for licensing — clean text, metadata, provenance — as the buyer's cost of ingestion affects the price. Track what has been licensed to whom on what terms, which is basic and is frequently not done. Model the trade between licensing and the traffic it may cost, since the two interact and are decided separately. Support the collective bargaining route, because a single publisher's leverage is limited and the trade bodies need the inventory work done first. And keep the provenance record, as it is what makes a warranty possible.

## Target Customer
Business development and legal leadership, publisher trade bodies, licensing buyers, and rights management vendors with no publisher presence.

## Impact If Built
An asset that was never expected to be sold does not acquire an inventory, so rights live in contracts and emails. A queryable rights inventory is the precondition for pricing, warranting or negotiating anything.
