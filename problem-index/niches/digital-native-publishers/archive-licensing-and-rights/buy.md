## The Problem
Music and stock media faced the identical structure: a large catalogue, fragmented ownership, many potential licensees, and use that is hard to observe. They built rights registries with per-work ownership records, collecting societies that license collectively and distribute revenue, fingerprinting and detection systems, and standard licence structures. The apparatus took decades and it works. Publishing has none of it for text.

## What Already Exists
Rights registries and per-work ownership records; collective licensing organisations; content fingerprinting and usage detection; standard licence taxonomies; and revenue distribution mechanisms.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to text used as training data. It requires: (1) detection of use in a model rather than in a broadcast, which fingerprinting cannot do and which is the substantive technical gap — evidence of ingestion is fundamentally harder than evidence of a play; (2) an unsettled legal basis, where music's rights framework is codified and this one is being litigated; (3) fragmented ownership within a single article across writer, publisher, photographer and syndicator, which is more tangled than a song's splits and less recorded; (4) no collecting society, so the collective structure would have to be built; and (5) licensees who are a small number of large counterparties rather than a long tail, which changes the negotiation entirely.

## Target Customer
Business development and legal leadership, trade bodies and prospective collecting organisations, licensing buyers, and rights technology vendors.

## Impact If Solved
Music built registries, societies and detection over decades and text publishing has none of it. The registry and the ownership records are buildable now; detection of use in a model is the genuinely hard part.
