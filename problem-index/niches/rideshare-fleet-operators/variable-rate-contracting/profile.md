# Variable-Rate Contracting

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/rideshare-fleet-operators|Rideshare Fleet Operators]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Whether the rental can be written as a share of what the driver actually earned, which requires verified earnings and moves the demand variance onto the operator.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$720M — 40% of the rental pricing niche
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~12% of US rideshare fleet services
**Digital Adoption:** Very low — a handful of platform-affiliated programmes, almost nothing independent
**Target Buyer:** Fleet operators willing to change their capital structure; their lenders; platforms
**Automation Potential:** Moderate — the mechanics are simple; the obstacles are contractual, financial and regulatory

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

The other half infers earnings. This half acts on them: a rental priced as a percentage of the week's actual income, or with a floor and a share, or with automatic relief below a threshold.

The distinguishing property is that it cannot be done unilaterally. An estimate is good enough to price a rate card and nowhere near good enough to settle a bill — a driver billed on an inferred number will dispute it, correctly. Variable-rate contracting needs *verified* earnings, which means either the platform sharing data, or the driver authorising a connection to their earnings account, or payment routed through the operator.

And it moves risk. A fixed rate puts demand variance on the driver; a revenue share puts it on the operator, whose vehicle payments, insurance and financing are all fixed. That is not a pricing tweak, it is a change to what kind of business the fleet is, with consequences for its financing, its cash flow and possibly its regulatory characterisation.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Platform-affiliated rental programmes have experimented with earnings-linked structures, and some platforms deduct rental from driver earnings directly, which solves verification by construction and ties the operator to one platform. A small number of independent operators run informal relief arrangements — a reduced week after a bad one — negotiated case by case with no structure.

The gaps are the enabling infrastructure. There is no standard, driver-authorised way to verify gig earnings across platforms comparable to payroll verification in lending. There is no product for administering a variable rental with floors, caps and reconciliation. And there is no financing structure that accommodates a revenue-share receivable, so an operator who adopted one would find their lender unwilling.
