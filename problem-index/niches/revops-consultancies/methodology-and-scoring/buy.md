**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to establish which forecasting method actually predicts best, and where the bias sits, using a retained history — and whoever scores it takes the account.

## The Problem
Statistical forecasting established how to compare methods properly: rolling-origin evaluation, holdout design that respects time, scale-independent accuracy measures, tests for whether one method genuinely beats another, and the finding — repeatedly demonstrated in forecasting competitions — that sophistication frequently loses to simple baselines. The methodology is public and settled. Revenue forecasting selects methods by advocacy.

## What Already Exists
Rolling-origin and time-respecting evaluation; scale-independent accuracy measures; statistical tests for forecast comparison; benchmark baselines that must be beaten; and forecast combination methods.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to a forecast produced by people whose behaviour changes when scored. It requires: (1) the forecast being partly a management commitment rather than only a prediction, so scoring it alters the thing being measured — this is the substantive difference and has no analogue in statistical forecasting; (2) a quarterly close with a hard boundary rather than a continuous series; (3) very few observations per period, since a quarter is one data point at the company level; (4) structural changes — reorganisations, territory redesigns, product launches — that break comparability every year; and (5) a naive baseline that is a salesperson's judgement rather than a random walk.

## Target Customer
Revenue leadership and operations, RevOps consultancies, forecasting vendors, and analytics providers.

## Impact If Solved
Statistical forecasting settled fair method comparison and repeatedly showed sophistication losing to simple baselines. A forecast that is partly a commitment, whose measurement changes behaviour, is what makes the revenue version different.
