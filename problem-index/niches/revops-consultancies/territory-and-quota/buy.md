**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to set territories and quotas that are actually attainable across a sales team, when the method is splitting last year's number in a spreadsheet — and whoever models it takes the account.

## The Problem
Field force sizing and territory alignment is a mature quantitative discipline in pharmaceuticals and consumer goods. Territory potential is estimated from market data, alignment is optimised for balanced workload and opportunity, quota is set from potential rather than from history, and the equity of the resulting design is an explicit objective with vendors and consultancies serving it. Software and technology sales, with better data about its own accounts, divides last year's number.

## What Already Exists
Territory potential estimation from market data; alignment optimisation for balance and equity; quota setting from potential; disruption minimisation when realigning; and attainment distribution modelling.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to named-account selling with long cycles and few, large deals. It requires: (1) territories that are lists of named accounts rather than geographies, so potential must be estimated per account rather than from market density — this is the substantive difference and is where the software industry's own data is an advantage; (2) deal counts small enough that a single large opportunity dominates a territory's year, making variance the central design problem; (3) sales cycles spanning quota periods; (4) products and segments changing faster than a pharmaceutical field force's; and (5) a culture that treats quota as a stretch instrument rather than as a planning figure.

## Target Customer
Sales and revenue leadership, RevOps consultancies, sales performance management vendors, and field force planning consultancies.

## Impact If Solved
Field force planning has optimised territory potential and equity for decades with established vendors. Named-account territories where one deal dominates the year makes variance, rather than density, the central design problem.
