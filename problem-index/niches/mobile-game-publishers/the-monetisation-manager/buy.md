## The Problem
Regulated gambling was required to solve this and did. Licensed operators run behavioural markers of harm, automated intervention at defined thresholds, deposit and loss limits, self-exclusion, affordability assessment and audited reporting. The methods are documented, the vendors exist, and the effectiveness is studied. Mobile games with escalating bundles and randomised rewards have drawn scrutiny in several European jurisdictions for good reason, and almost none of this machinery has crossed over.

## What Already Exists
Behavioural markers of harm; automated intervention workflows; deposit, loss and session limits; self-exclusion infrastructure; and affordability and source-of-funds assessment.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to a product that is not gambling, for a player who is buying progress rather than wagering. It requires: (1) markers built on purchase and progression behaviour rather than on wagering, since there is no stake and no loss in the gambling sense — this is the substantive difference and most published marker sets do not transfer directly; (2) intervention that does not read as an accusation, because a game player is not a gambler and will react badly to being treated as one; (3) no licensing obligation, so adoption must be commercial or reputational rather than mandatory; (4) a much younger and broader player base, where age and payer identity frequently differ; and (5) mechanics that are deliberately not classified as gambling, which means the whole framing has to be built rather than borrowed.

## Target Customer
Mobile publishers, platform holders, consumer protection regulators, and responsible gambling technology vendors.

## Impact If Solved
Gambling built harm detection because a licence depended on it. Markers based on purchase and progression rather than wagering, and intervention that does not read as an accusation, are what the transfer requires.
