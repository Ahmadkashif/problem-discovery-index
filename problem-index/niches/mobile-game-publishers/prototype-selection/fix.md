# The Threshold Nobody Can Source

**Niche:** [[niches/mobile-game-publishers/prototype-selection/profile|Prototype Selection]]
**Industry:** [[industries/mobile-game-publishers|Mobile Game Publishers]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Every publisher has a kill threshold, everyone applies it, and nobody in the building can say where the number came from or when it was last checked.
**Tags:** #quick-win #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #descriptive-statistics #survival-analysis #revenue-impact #compliance
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to decide which of dozens of prototypes a month deserves to continue, on evidence that is three days long and drawn from a population that was itself filtered by the same rule — and whoever gets the selection right takes the account.

## The Problem
Ask a mobile publisher where its day-one retention threshold came from and the answer is that it has always been that. It was set during the hypercasual period for games with different depth, different acquisition economics and different monetisation, and it survived a structural change in all three. It is applied uniformly across genres. Nobody has checked it against the publisher's own outcomes, and nobody can say what it would cost to be wrong by two points.

## Why It's Still Broken
Nobody owns the number — a rule that everybody applies and nobody maintains drifts out of validity silently, because there is no moment at which anyone is required to defend it. Checking it requires outcome data the funnel destroyed. The business runs acceptably, so there is no forcing event. And revisiting it implies that past kills may have been wrong, which nobody wants to raise.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Audit the number before replacing it. Reconstruct the threshold's provenance and date, which is the fix and frequently ends the conversation on its own once the year is written down. Backtest it against the publisher's own realised outcomes, including the near-misses that were allowed through. Show the threshold's performance separately by genre, since that is where the uniform rule most obviously fails. Compute the sensitivity — how many concepts change fate if the number moves by a small amount — because that reveals whether the precision is meaningful at all. Attach the sample size and the resulting uncertainty to every kill decision, which most publishers have never displayed. Record the acquisition conditions of each test so comparisons are fair. Report the concepts killed narrowly, as that list is the cheapest source of learning available. Set a review cadence and an owner, which is the durable part. Flag when the portfolio mix has drifted away from the population the threshold was set on. And state the threshold as a decision rule with its assumptions rather than as a number, so it can be argued with.

## Who Feels the Pain
Teams whose work ended against an unexamined number; publishing leaders defending a portfolio they cannot explain; analysts asked to justify a rule they inherited; and the games that would have worked.

## Impact If Fixed
A rule that everybody applies and nobody maintains drifts out of validity silently, because there is no moment at which anyone is required to defend it. Reconstructing its provenance and backtesting it by genre is a week of work against a decision that governs the whole portfolio.
