# The Milestone Schedule That Never Survived Contact

**Niche:** [[niches/mobile-game-publishers/external-studio-partnerships/profile|External Studio Partnerships]]
**Industry:** [[industries/mobile-game-publishers|Mobile Game Publishers]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The contract has six milestones on fixed dates, the studio missed the second, and the relationship now runs on renegotiation.
**Tags:** #quick-win #workflow-orchestration #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #confidence-intervals #automation #revenue-impact #data-integration
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to decide which external studio and which deal terms will produce a hit, using judgement about people that nobody has ever converted into evidence — and whoever does takes the account.

## The Problem
Publishing agreements set milestones and payments against dates fixed at signing, months before anyone knows what the game requires. Studios miss them — routinely, and usually for legitimate reasons. What follows is an ad hoc renegotiation that consumes partner manager time, strains the relationship, delays payment to a studio that needs it, and leaves the publisher without a clear view of whether the project is actually in trouble or merely late in the ordinary way.

## Why It's Still Broken
Milestones are legal instruments rather than project management — a schedule written to be enforceable is not written to be informative, so a miss carries no information about whether the project is healthy. Nobody tracks slippage across the portfolio. Payment is tied to the date rather than to the state. And partner managers handle each case individually.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Make the miss informative and the response standard. Track milestone slippage across every partnership with a common definition, which is the fix and immediately distinguishes an ordinary delay from a failing project. Compare a studio's slippage against its own history and against the portfolio, since the relevant question is whether this is unusual for them. Define a standard response ladder rather than negotiating each case fresh, which removes most of the partner manager's time cost. Separate scope change from delay explicitly, as they require different responses and are usually conflated. Decouple payment from the date where the work is progressing, because starving a studio mid-project is the reliable way to guarantee the miss. Give the studio a view of what the publisher considers on track, which most agreements never communicate. Flag portfolio-level slippage so the publishing schedule can be replanned rather than repeatedly surprised. Record the eventual outcome against the slippage pattern, so the signal becomes calibrated. Warn early rather than at the milestone date, since the evidence exists weeks before. And write the next agreement's schedule against what this studio actually delivered.

## Who Feels the Pain
Partner managers renegotiating instead of managing; studios waiting on payment for work they are doing; publishing schedules replanned late; and projects that were failing and looked the same as the ones that were not.

## Impact If Fixed
A schedule written to be enforceable is not written to be informative, so a miss carries no information about whether the project is healthy. Common slippage tracking against a studio's own history separates ordinary delay from real trouble.
