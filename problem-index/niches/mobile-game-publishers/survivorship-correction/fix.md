# The Concept You Killed That Shipped Somewhere Else

**Niche:** [[niches/mobile-game-publishers/survivorship-correction/profile|Survivorship Correction]]
**Industry:** [[industries/mobile-game-publishers|Mobile Game Publishers]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** A concept killed on day-one retention was picked up by another publisher and earned for three years, and nobody joined that fact back to the decision.
**Tags:** #quick-win #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #causal-inference #confidence-intervals #data-integration #revenue-impact #hypothesis-testing
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to find out what the killed concepts would have done, because every threshold in the industry is validated against the population that passed it — and whoever assembles that evidence takes the account.

## The Problem
Some killed concepts do not disappear. A team takes it elsewhere, a studio self-publishes it, a mechanic reappears in a competitor's title that goes on to earn substantially. These are real observed counterfactuals — the closest thing the industry has to finding out what a kill cost — and no publisher systematically tracks them. The information is public, in store charts and revenue estimates, and it is never joined back to the decision record.

## Why It's Still Broken
Nobody owns the question — an organisation that never looks at what happened after its own rejections cannot discover that the rule is wrong, however many times the evidence walks past in public. The decision record and the market data live in different places. Finding out is mildly embarrassing. And the cases are individually anecdotal, so nobody treats them as a series.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Join the kill list to the market. Maintain a register of killed concepts with their test numbers and decision date, which most publishers do not keep in a queryable form and is the prerequisite for everything else. Monitor store charts and third-party revenue estimates for reappearances of killed concepts and teams, since this is public information that costs nothing to watch. Record the near-misses separately, as the marginal kills are where the rule is actually being tested. Compute an observed regret figure annually — what the tracked reappearances earned — which is the number that makes the case for a real holdout. Track the mechanics as well as the titles, because a killed loop often reappears in another form. Follow teams rather than only concepts, since a team that ships successfully elsewhere is evidence about the judgement applied to it. Present the register without blame, which determines whether anyone maintains it. Feed the confirmed cases back into threshold review as concrete examples. Note that the register is biased toward the kills that got a second life, and say so rather than overclaiming. And make it a standing report rather than an occasional exercise.

## Who Feels the Pain
Publishers who watched a killed concept earn for a competitor; teams who left and succeeded elsewhere; analysts with no evidence about the kills; and a threshold that has never been tested against its own errors.

## Impact If Fixed
An organisation that never looks at what happened after its own rejections cannot discover that the rule is wrong, however many times the evidence walks past in public. A register joined to store charts turns anecdotes into an annual regret figure.
