# Fix: Independent in Theory, Dependent in Practice

**Niche:** The Privacy Officer
**Industry:** [[industries/privacy-tech-vendors|Privacy Tech Vendors]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The role is required to be independent, is employed by the organisation it oversees, and is asked to approve the things that organisation has already decided to do.
**Tags:** #compliance #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #confidence-intervals #workflow-orchestration
**Contested on:** Whether the person who signs the processing record has any means of establishing that it is accurate.

## The Problem

The data protection officer is formally independent. They cannot be instructed on how to perform the role, cannot be dismissed for performing it, and must report to the highest level of management.

In practice they are an employee. Their salary, budget, headcount and career progression are determined by the organisation whose processing they oversee. They sit in legal or compliance, reporting to a general counsel for whom privacy is one responsibility among many. And the things they assess have usually already been decided — a product launch, a new vendor, a marketing initiative — with the privacy review arriving as a step before shipping rather than as an input to the decision.

The consequences are predictable. An officer who objects frequently becomes a bottleneck, which is a career-limiting reputation. One who objects rarely is doing the job the organisation prefers. The formal independence provides protection against dismissal for doing the job and no support at all for doing it, and the gap between the two is filled by the individual's own judgement about how much capital to spend.

The exposure is asymmetric. If something goes wrong, the regulator has their name.

## Why It's Still Broken

**Formal protection addresses the wrong risk.** Legislation protects against dismissal for performing the role. It does not address budget, staffing, seniority, timing of involvement or organisational standing, which are where the real constraints are.

**The reporting line is nominal.** Reporting to the highest level of management usually means an annual presentation. Day to day the officer reports to someone with other priorities and their own relationship with the business.

**Privacy review arrives after the decision.** By the time the assessment is requested, the product is built and the launch is scheduled. Objecting means delaying something with commercial momentum, which is a different act from shaping it earlier.

**Objection is individually costly and organisationally invisible.** The officer bears the reputational cost; the organisation records no benefit. This is exactly the structure that produces under-objection.

**The role is often part-time or junior.** Frequently combined with other duties or held by someone without the seniority to challenge a commercial decision, which makes the independence formal rather than practical.

**Nobody measures whether concerns were raised or heeded.** There is no record of advice given and decisions taken, so a pattern of overridden objections is invisible — including to the officer, who would benefit most from having it documented.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Record advice and decisions, formally.** Every significant assessment recording what was advised, what was decided, by whom and on what basis. This is protective for the officer, informative for the organisation, and is the artefact a regulator will ask for. Its absence is the largest single gap in the role's practice.

**Give the officer a real reporting line.** To the board or an audit committee, with a private session, on the model finance uses for internal audit. Formal independence without a reporting line that bypasses management is not independence.

**Involve them before the decision.** Privacy review at design rather than before launch, which is both more effective and far less confrontational — shaping a proposal is a different act from blocking one.

**Fix the budget and headcount independently.** Determined at board level rather than by the function the officer oversees, which removes the most direct lever over their behaviour.

**Track the objection rate and the override rate.** An officer who never objects and one whose objections are always overridden are both signals about the organisation, and neither is visible today.

**Make the role senior enough.** Either the officer has the standing to challenge commercial decisions, or someone senior is explicitly accountable alongside them. Asking a junior person to exercise independence against a leadership team is asking for a formality.

**Provide external support.** Access to independent counsel, and to a professional network where the officer can discuss a difficult position with peers outside their employer — which is standard in other professions carrying personal regulatory exposure.

## Who Feels the Pain

The officer, holding personal regulatory exposure for an organisation's decisions they can advise on and not determine, with formal independence that protects their job and not their position.

The organisation, which believes it has an independent privacy function and has an employee in a difficult position, and which will discover the difference during an enquiry.

Data subjects, whose protection depends on an individual's willingness to spend personal capital.

And the next officer, who inherits the same structure and the same asymmetry.

## Impact If Fixed

Recording advice and decisions is close to free, protects the individual, informs the organisation, and produces the artefact that regulators actually ask for when things go wrong.

A genuine board reporting line converts formal independence into structural independence, using a governance pattern finance established decades ago for exactly this problem.

And moving privacy review to the design stage removes most of the confrontation from the role, because shaping a decision early is a collaborative act where blocking one late is not.
