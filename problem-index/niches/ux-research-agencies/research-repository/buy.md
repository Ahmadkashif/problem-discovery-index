**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to make years of accumulated research answerable to the question somebody is asking today — and whoever does takes the account.

## The Problem
Large consulting and law firms learned that a knowledge base without a curation function is a filing cabinet. The response was to staff it: knowledge managers who edit, structure and retire material; contribution built into engagement close-out rather than left to goodwill; and a practice of pushing relevant precedents to teams rather than waiting for a search. The lesson — that the technology is the smaller half — is well established. Research teams bought the technology.

## What Already Exists
Staffed knowledge management functions; curation, editing and retirement practices; contribution built into project close-out; precedent push rather than search; and measured reuse as a managed metric.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to small teams with no headcount for a curation function. It requires: (1) agencies and in-house teams of five to fifty people who cannot staff a knowledge manager, so the curation must be largely automated or embedded in the workflow — this is the substantive difference and is why the consulting model has not transferred; (2) material that is largely recordings and transcripts rather than documents; (3) findings whose validity decays as the product changes, requiring age and context handling consulting precedents do not need; (4) client confidentiality limiting what can cross engagements; and (5) researchers who would rather run a study than search, which is a genuine preference to design around.

## Target Customer
UX research agencies and in-house teams, research operations, repository vendors, and knowledge management providers.

## Impact If Solved
Consulting learned that a knowledge base without a curation function is a filing cabinet, and staffed it. Teams too small to staff one is why the curation has to be automated or embedded in the workflow here.
