# The Job That Was Losing Money in Week Three

**One-liner:** Specialty contractors discover a losing job in the monthly cost report, the foreman knew in week three, and there is no channel by which what the foreman knows becomes something the company acts on.

## The Problem
A foreman watches his crew lose an hour a day to a stacked-trade condition, material delivered to the wrong side of the building, and a detail that does not work as drawn. He mentions it. It reaches a project manager as a complaint among several, gets a sympathetic answer, and does not become a change order, a schedule impact notice, or a resequencing request — because turning a foreman's observation into any of those requires documentation the foreman has neither the time nor the tools to produce. Six weeks later the company discovers the job is losing money and asks what happened, and the foreman tells them, and everyone agrees it is a shame.

## Why It's Still Broken
The foreman's day ends with a truck and a phone, not a desk, and every mechanism for escalating a productivity impact was designed for someone with a keyboard and twenty minutes. The impacts are also individually small — an hour here, a partial day there — which makes each one feel not worth the paperwork while their sum is the job's margin. And there is a cultural element the industry recognises: reporting an impact reads as complaining or as blaming another trade, and foremen who are good at their jobs absorb problems rather than escalate them.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Make the report cost fifteen seconds and make the aggregate visible. A structured impact capture on a phone — what happened, where, which crew, roughly how much time, a photo — with no free-text requirement, is a thing a foreman will actually do at the end of a shift if it is genuinely that short. The value is in the aggregation: fifteen small impacts in one area over two weeks is a documented, quantified, photographed pattern, and a pattern is a conversation with the general contractor rather than a complaint. Attach it automatically to the affected scope so the production-rate deviation and the reported cause sit together, which is what makes the case. Close the loop back to the foreman when something is done, because the reason foremen stop reporting is that nothing ever visibly happens.

## Who Feels the Pain
Foremen absorbing conditions nobody records; project managers explaining a loss they learned about too late; and owners of trade contractors whose margin is consumed by impacts that were documented nowhere and therefore never recovered.

## Impact If Fixed
Impacts documented contemporaneously are recoverable and impacts remembered later are not, which makes the timing the entire value — contractors who capture systematically recover a meaningful share of what they currently absorb. The by-product is a labelled record of why production rates deviate, which is exactly the explanatory data the build and buy notes need and which nobody in this industry currently collects.
