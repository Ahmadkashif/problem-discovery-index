# The Change Approved in a Driveway

**Niche:** [[niches/construction-tech-platforms/residential-remodel-platforms/profile|Residential Remodel & Custom Home]]
**Industry:** [[industries/construction-tech-platforms|Construction Tech Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Most residential change orders are agreed verbally on site and written up days later or never, so the contractor carries the work and the homeowner carries a memory, and the two do not match.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #compliance #automation #worker-facing #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in residential remodelling software is fighting to keep selections, allowances and change orders reconciled to the contract in real time so the homeowner conversation is about a number both sides can see — and whoever keeps that reconciliation current takes the account.

## The Problem
The homeowner comes out while the crew is working and asks whether the outlet can be moved and a second one added. The lead carpenter says yes, it will be a bit extra. Everyone understands the conversation the same way at the time. Two months later the invoice includes $480 for electrical changes, the homeowner remembers "a bit extra" as meaning a hundred dollars, and there is no record. This happens several times per project on nearly every project, and the aggregate is both a real margin loss for the contractor and a real trust loss for the homeowner — each side is honestly remembering a different conversation.

## Why It's Still Broken
The moment of agreement is the worst possible moment for paperwork: the contractor is in work clothes, the homeowner is mid-conversation, and pulling out a form changes the tone of a relationship that runs on informality. Contractors know that introducing friction into these conversations costs goodwill, and goodwill in residential work is the referral pipeline. So the industry has settled on an informality that works socially and fails financially, and every software answer so far has asked the contractor to be less informal.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Capture the conversation rather than replace it. Thirty seconds of voice — "moving the outlet by the window and adding one on the island, roughly four eighty" — recorded on the spot, transcribed and turned into a described, priced change with a photo attached, sent to the homeowner's phone before the contractor walks away. The homeowner taps to acknowledge. The whole interaction is shorter than the conversation that preceded it and preserves its tone, which is the design constraint everything else has failed. Where a price is not yet known, the record captures the change with the price pending and the homeowner sees it as pending, which is still far better than nothing. Pricing suggestions from the contractor's own history of similar changes make the on-the-spot number less of a guess.

## Who Feels the Pain
Contractors absorbing work they agreed to and never billed; homeowners surprised by invoices for conversations they remember differently; and lead carpenters caught between a customer asking a reasonable question and a company policy about change orders.

## Impact If Fixed
Contractors who capture verbal changes contemporaneously recover a meaningful share of what they currently absorb, and the relational effect is larger — the end-of-project conversation stops being a negotiation about memory. This is the highest-value sixty seconds in residential construction and currently nobody spends it.
