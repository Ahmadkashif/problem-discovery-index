# E-Signature and Payment Rails Wired to the Change, Not the Document

**Niche:** [[niches/construction-tech-platforms/residential-remodel-platforms/profile|Residential Remodel & Custom Home]]
**Industry:** [[industries/construction-tech-platforms|Construction Tech Platforms]]
**Type:** Buy & Customize (Vertical Adaptation)
**One-liner:** E-signature and digital payments are commodity infrastructure that residential platforms already embed, and they are wired to documents and invoices rather than to the individual decisions that actually change what the homeowner owes.
**Tags:** #workflow-orchestration #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #compliance #automation #data-integration #revenue-impact #quick-win
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in residential remodelling software is fighting to keep selections, allowances and change orders reconciled to the contract in real time so the homeowner conversation is about a number both sides can see — and whoever keeps that reconciliation current takes the account.

## The Problem
A change order is a document. Producing one takes the contractor twenty minutes at a desk: describe the change, price it, generate the form, send for signature. For a $600 lighting upgrade agreed in a hallway, nobody does that — the work proceeds on a verbal agreement and gets reconciled, or not, at the end. The e-signature capability sitting inside the platform is perfectly good and is attached to an artefact too heavy for the transaction it needs to govern. The result is that the small changes, which collectively are most of the overrun, are undocumented.

## What Already Exists
DocuSign, Dropbox Sign, and the embedded signature SDKs are cheap and legally robust; every residential construction platform already embeds one. Payment processing, ACH, card acceptance and financing integrations are equally commoditised and widely deployed in the segment. Mobile capture of signatures is a solved interface. Nothing here needs building.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to make the unit of approval the change rather than the document. It requires: (1) an approval object light enough for a $200 decision — a description, a price, a photo, and a tap — that is still legally sufficient, which is a drafting and product question more than a technical one; (2) approval requested at the moment and place of agreement, on the homeowner's phone while the contractor is standing there, since a request that arrives by email that evening is a request that sits unanswered; (3) batching into a formal change order for the record without requiring a separate approval, so the paperwork follows the agreement instead of gating it; (4) linking payment terms to the approval, so an approved change with a deposit requirement collects at approval rather than at the next draw; and (5) an unambiguous record of what was shown at the moment of approval — the running total at that instant — because that is the artefact that settles a later disagreement.

## Target Customer
Remodelling contractors whose change orders are currently verbal, and the residential platform vendors who already pay for signature and payment infrastructure and use it only for contracts and invoices.

## Impact If Solved
Documenting small changes at the moment they are agreed converts the single largest category of unbilled residential work into billed work, and it does so with components already licensed. The contractor's behaviour change is from a twenty-minute desk task to a ten-second one, which is the only version of this that will actually happen on site.
