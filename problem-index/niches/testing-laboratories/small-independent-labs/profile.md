# Small Independent Accredited Labs

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/testing-laboratories|Testing Laboratories]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to give a five- to twenty-five-person accredited lab a system it can run without an IT person or LIMS administrator, and whoever gets a small lab live and assessment-ready in weeks rather than a year wins a long tail the enterprise vendors ignore.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$4B US revenue of independent testing labs with fewer than about 25 staff (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~15% (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Low-Medium — instruments are digital; the LIMS is often a home-built database, spreadsheets or a cloud LIMS still being configured
**Target Buyer:** Owner and laboratory director (often the same person)
**Automation Potential:** Medium — reporting, validation paperwork and quality records automate well; the owner's time is the constraint

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
The 2022 Economic Census counted 7,970 testing lab establishments averaging about 21 employees, and the average hides a long tail of five- to twenty-person labs: a regional water lab, a food micro lab serving local processors, a materials lab next to an aerospace cluster, a specialist in one accredited scope. They carry the same ISO/IEC 17025 obligations as a national network — method validation, uncertainty, proficiency testing, document control, impartiality — with no IT department and no full-time quality manager. Enterprise LIMS cost more than many of them earn in profit; LabWare and STARLIMS are estimated at $100,000+ a year for five users.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Cloud LIMS built for smaller labs (QBench at roughly $275–425 per user per month, CloudLIMS from about $171–230), Microsoft Access and Excel systems built in-house, accounting software for invoicing. The gaps: configuration takes months the owner doesn't have; method validation and uncertainty work is done in personal spreadsheets; and migration off a legacy home-built system is feared more than its failure.
