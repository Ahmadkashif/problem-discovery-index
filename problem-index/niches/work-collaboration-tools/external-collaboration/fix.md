# The Smaller Party Maintains Eleven Logins

**Niche:** [[niches/work-collaboration-tools/external-collaboration/profile|External Collaboration]]
**Industry:** [[industries/work-collaboration-tools|Work Collaboration Tools]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** When two organisations collaborate, the larger one's tools win, so the agency, the supplier and the contractor each maintain a separate account, workflow and set of conventions for every client they serve.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #data-integration #workflow-orchestration #automation #worker-facing #quick-win #confidence-intervals
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in external collaboration is fighting to let someone outside the organisation participate properly without a licence, an account provisioning process or a loss of control — and whoever makes the outside party a first-class participant takes the work that currently happens over email.

## The Problem
An agency serves eleven clients. Four use one work management tool, three use another, two use a shared drive with a naming convention, one uses a portal, and one insists on email. The agency's project managers switch between eleven environments, learn eleven sets of conventions, and maintain the agency's own internal tracking in parallel because none of the client environments gives them a view across their portfolio. The cost is real, is borne entirely by the smaller party, and is invisible to every client — each of whom considers their own arrangement reasonable, which it is.

## Why It's Still Broken
The larger party's tools win because the larger party's security and compliance functions have a veto and the smaller party needs the work. No client sees the aggregate because each sees only their own relationship. And the smaller party has no standing to raise it: an agency telling a client that their tool is inconvenient is not a conversation that goes well.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Give the smaller party a portfolio view across the client environments it works in. Their own workspace aggregates the work they are doing for each client by reading from each client's system where an integration exists, and by lightweight capture where it does not — which is the same federation pattern as elsewhere in this industry, pointed at the party who currently has no view at all. Convention differences are absorbed by the aggregating layer rather than by the people: a status in one client's vocabulary and another's map into the agency's own. Their own internal tracking stops being a parallel effort and becomes the primary record with client-facing projections. And the measurement worth taking to a client is the cost: how many hours a month the agency spends on environment and convention overhead for this relationship, which is a number a good client would want to know and which nobody currently computes. Some of them would simplify their requirements if asked with evidence.

## Who Feels the Pain
Agencies, suppliers and contractors maintaining a separate working environment per client; their staff switching between eleven sets of conventions; and clients receiving less of the value they are paying for because a share of it goes into their own process overhead.

## Impact If Fixed
A portfolio view across client environments is the thing the smaller party most needs and no product provides, since every product is built for the larger side of the relationship. Quantifying the overhead is the part that could change client behaviour, because the cost is currently invisible to the party imposing it.
