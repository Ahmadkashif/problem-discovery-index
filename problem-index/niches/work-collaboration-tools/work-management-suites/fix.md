# Three Trackers, One Organisation

**Niche:** [[niches/work-collaboration-tools/work-management-suites/profile|Work Management Suites]]
**Industry:** [[industries/work-collaboration-tools|Work Collaboration Tools]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Most organisations run several work management products simultaneously, each adopted by a different function for good reasons, and nobody has counted what the proliferation costs or decided whether to do anything about it.

## The Problem
Engineering uses one tracker, marketing another, operations a third, and several teams maintain a parallel spreadsheet because none of the three fits. Each choice was reasonable. The aggregate is that no cross-functional programme can be seen whole, work is duplicated across systems, people maintain the same item in two places, and the organisation pays for three overlapping products plus the integration effort between them. Nobody owns the aggregate because each adoption was a local decision, and the standard response — mandating consolidation onto one — fails because the local reasons were genuine.

## Why It's Still Broken
Tool adoption in this category is bottom-up by design; the products are inexpensive enough to be bought by a department and good enough to be adopted enthusiastically. Consolidation attempts fail because the mandated tool is worse for at least one of the functions, which then works around it, producing a fourth system. And nobody measures the cost: the licences are visible, the duplication and the invisibility are not, so the conversation is about spend rather than about the thing that actually hurts.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Measure the overlap before deciding anything. Which work exists in more than one system, which teams maintain duplicates, how much of each tool's usage is genuinely distinct rather than parallel, and where cross-functional programmes lose visibility at a tool boundary — all computable from the tools' own data and none of it currently assembled. That measurement usually shows something more useful than the consolidation debate assumes: the tools are not redundant, and the cost is concentrated in a small number of specific seams where work crosses. Fix the seams rather than the estate, which means federation as the buy note describes rather than migration. Where genuine duplication exists — the same item maintained in two places by the same team — that is a specific process fix rather than a platform decision. And set an explicit federation standard for new tools rather than trying to prevent adoption, since prevention has failed everywhere and the realistic goal is that the tenth tool is readable rather than that it is not bought.

## Who Feels the Pain
People maintaining the same work item in two systems; programme leaders assembling a view by hand across three; and technology functions caught between a consolidation mandate and departments who will not give up tools that work for them.

## Impact If Fixed
Measuring overlap reframes a recurring and unproductive consolidation argument into a specific list of seams, most of which are fixable without migrating anyone. Setting a federation expectation for new adoptions is the durable change, because the proliferation is a permanent feature of how this category is bought and the realistic objective is legibility rather than uniformity.
