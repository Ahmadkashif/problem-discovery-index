## Why These Niches

The category's own promise is that work becomes visible, and it delivers that by asking people to type status updates about work the tools already recorded. A task's status says what somebody last set it to; the commits, documents, messages and review cycles that show what actually happened are captured and unused. Inferring status from activity rather than collecting it from people is the contested capability, and it is the one that would make the category's core claim true.

Work management suites are the larger block and **failed the filter as one niche**. Engineering work tracking contests on whether the tracker reflects the code — a connection to repositories, builds and reviews that makes status real. Cross-functional work management contests on making work visible across functions that use entirely different tools, which is a federation problem rather than an integration one. Different buyers, different incumbents, different definitions of success. Decomposed below.

Regulated collaboration and external collaboration are the two underdigitised areas, each for a structural reason: in regulated organisations a conversation may be a record, which makes adoption a compliance decision rather than a productivity one; and external collaboration remains on email because every tool is internal by design and inviting an outside party means a licence, an account and a control problem.

Attention and invisible contribution are the two underserved constituencies. Every tool defaults to notifying and none is accountable for the total, which is a tax nobody owns. And the work that does not produce a ticket — reviewing, unblocking, mentoring, responding to incidents — is invisible in every system that measures contribution, with consequences for the people who do most of it.

The automation niches are the category's two long-standing failures: template libraries abandoned within a month, and a dependency graph that exists in nobody's tool because the work spans nine of them.

## Filter Notes

**Niche 2 failed the filter as stated and was decomposed.** "Work management" is a product shelf rather than a contest. Engineering work tracking is bought by engineering organisations whose measure is whether the tracker reflects what the code actually shows, which is a connection to repositories, pipelines and reviews — and which is why the incumbent in that segment owns the surrounding developer stack. Cross-functional work management is bought to make work visible across marketing, legal, finance and operations, each of which works in a different tool, and its measure is whether a programme's real state can be assembled without asking anyone — a federation problem with different competitors. Both sub-niches are terminal.

**Niches 1, 3–8 are terminal as stated.** Each names something testable: status accuracy against observed activity, whether a conversation that is a record is retrievable and restricted, whether an external party can participate without a licence, the aggregate interruption count per person, whether non-ticketed work appears in any contribution measure, template survival past the first month, and whether the cross-tool blocking relationship can be stated at all.
