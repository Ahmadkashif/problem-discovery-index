# Ninety Days and a Chased Invoice

**Niche:** [[niches/influencer-marketing-platforms/the-creator/profile|The Creator]]
**Industry:** [[industries/influencer-marketing-platforms|Influencer Marketing Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The creator delivered on time, invoiced on time, and is chasing payment four months later through a manager who does not control the finance system.
**Tags:** #workflow-orchestration #compliance #automation #evaluation-metrics #quick-win #revenue-impact #worker-facing #descriptive-statistics
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to give a creator the market data and the settlement terms a supplier in any other industry would take for granted — and whoever does that changes the balance of every negotiation in the category.

## The Problem
The content was delivered, approved and published. The invoice went in the next day. Ninety-day terms mean payment in three months, except that the invoice was rejected for a missing purchase order number, resubmitted, and is now in a queue. The creator is chasing the influencer marketing manager, who has no access to the finance system and no influence over it, and who is apologising weekly. Four months after delivering, an individual with no cash reserve is funding a large company's working capital, and the only lever they have is a polite message to someone who cannot help.

## Why It's Still Broken
Creator payments run through supplier accounts payable, which applies the same terms it uses for large vendors, and nobody assessed whether those terms are appropriate for an individual — a process applied by default rather than by decision. The manager who owns the relationship has no authority over payment. Onboarding requirements designed for corporate suppliers create rejections. And late payment costs the brand nothing.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Treat creator payment as a distinct class. Set payment terms appropriate to an individual supplier rather than inheriting corporate terms, which is the fix, is a policy decision rather than a system change, and costs a large brand almost nothing in working capital at these amounts. Fund the payment at contracting rather than after delivery, which removes the queue entirely for the cases where it can be done. Automate approval-to-payment so that approved content triggers settlement without an invoice, since the invoice step exists to prove delivery that the platform already recorded. Simplify onboarding, because the corporate supplier requirements are the largest source of rejected invoices and delay. Give the creator real visibility of where the payment is, which removes most of the chasing traffic and the manager's weekly apology. Escalate automatically when a payment passes its due date rather than relying on the creator to chase. Let the manager see the payment status even without system access, since they are the one being asked. Offer early settlement as a standard option, which many creators would take and which costs little. Report payment performance publicly by brand, which is the mechanism that actually changes behaviour and which creators already share informally. And measure days from delivery to payment as a partnership metric, because a brand competing for good creators will find it matters more than its rate card.

## Who Feels the Pain
Creators funding large companies' working capital from no reserve; managers apologising for a system they cannot touch; and brands losing good creators to competitors who pay in two weeks.

## Impact If Fixed
Corporate supplier terms are applied to individuals by default rather than by decision, and at these amounts the working capital saving is negligible to the brand. Triggering settlement from approved content removes the invoice step that exists to prove a delivery already recorded.
