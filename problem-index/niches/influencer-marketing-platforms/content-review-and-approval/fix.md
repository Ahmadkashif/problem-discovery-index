# The Brief Nobody Read

**Industry:** [[industries/influencer-marketing-platforms|Influencer Marketing Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The brief is an eleven-page document, the creator skimmed page one, and the revision round that follows was entirely predictable.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #workflow-orchestration #evaluation-metrics #automation #quick-win #worker-facing #descriptive-statistics #compliance
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to make a hundred simultaneous creative conversations with people outside the company tractable — and whoever does that decides how many partnerships one team can actually run.

## The Problem
Brand briefs arrive as long documents containing positioning, tone guidance, brand history, legal requirements, mandatory inclusions, prohibited claims, hashtag rules and disclosure obligations. The creator, who has four deals running and makes content for a living, reads the first page and the deliverables. The draft comes back missing two mandatories and containing a claim legal will not allow. Everyone treats the resulting revision round as normal. It was entirely predictable from the moment an eleven-page document was sent to someone who was never going to read eleven pages.

## Why It's Still Broken
The brief is written to protect the brand rather than to instruct the creator, which means completeness is the drafting goal and comprehension is not — that inversion causes the whole problem. Legal adds requirements and nobody removes anything, so briefs grow monotonically. Nobody measures whether the brief was understood. And the revision round absorbs the failure, so the brief is never blamed.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Make the brief operational. Separate the few things that will cause a revision — mandatories, prohibited claims, disclosure requirements, deliverable specifics — from the context, and present those as a short checklist, which is the fix and typically reduces a document to a dozen lines the creator will actually read. Deliver it in the creator's own channel rather than as an attachment, since an attachment is a document and a message is read. Confirm comprehension explicitly with a short acknowledgement of the specific requirements, which is a small ask and converts a hopeful send into a recorded one. Check the draft automatically against the mandatories and prohibitions before a human reviews it, which catches the predictable failures in seconds and is where most revision rounds come from. Explain the reason for constraints, because creators comply far better with a rule they understand and argue with one they do not. Give examples of accepted and rejected content, which communicates more than any amount of prose. Tailor the brief to the creator's format and platform rather than sending one document to everyone. Track which brief elements are most often missed and rewrite those, which is a feedback loop nobody runs. Keep brand context available but optional, so it is there for creators who want it and not in the way of those who do not. And measure first-draft acceptance rate, because that single number tells a brand whether its briefing works and almost nobody tracks it.

## Who Feels the Pain
Creators guessing at requirements buried on page seven; managers running avoidable revision rounds; and brands whose campaigns are delayed by their own documents.

## Impact If Fixed
The brief is written for completeness rather than comprehension, which is why an eleven-page document produces a predictable revision round. A dozen-line checklist delivered in the creator's own channel, with automatic pre-review checking, removes most of them.
