# Compressing the Hours Before the Writing

**Niche:** [[niches/newsletter-media/the-daily-writer/profile|The Daily Writer]]
**Industry:** [[industries/newsletter-media|Newsletter Media]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Most of the day goes to finding out what happened, and only the last part of it produces the thing readers receive.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #bert #workflow-orchestration #worker-facing #automation #evaluation-metrics #word-embeddings
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to make a deadline that arrives every morning forever sustainable for the person who has to meet it — and whoever compresses the reading that precedes the writing gives that person their day back.

## The Problem
The writer reads across dozens of sources, filters for relevance to a specific audience, checks what is genuinely new, verifies the claims worth including, and only then writes. The reading is the majority of the work and is repeated every day from scratch. The sources are largely stable, the audience's interests are known, and the archive of what has already been covered exists — none of which reduces tomorrow's reading at all.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
Editorial tooling was built for newsrooms with staff, so a one-writer daily operation was never the design target — a category that assumes a research desk does not build for someone who is the entire desk. Generic summarisation tools do not know the audience or the archive and produce output the writer must verify anyway, which removes no work. Voice is the product, so anything that writes is suspect. And the writer has no time to adopt a tool.

## What to Build
Compress the sourcing without touching the voice. Monitor the stable source set continuously and surface what is new and relevant to this audience, which is the core and is where the hours are. Learn relevance from what this publication has actually covered, since generic news relevance is useless and the archive is the training signal. Check candidates against the archive automatically, because "did we cover this" is asked constantly and answered by memory. Assemble the supporting material for each candidate — the primary source, the numbers, the earlier coverage — so verification starts with the evidence rather than with a search. Draft the factual scaffolding rather than the prose, as the facts are compressible and the voice is not. Preserve everything the writer decided not to use, since it recurs and is currently discarded. Support the recurring formats — the daily roundup, the chart, the one-line takes — with structure rather than starting from a blank document. Track what was covered and what performed, so the editorial pattern becomes visible. Make continuity possible by capturing the writer's sourcing setup, which is currently entirely in their head. And measure hours spent reading versus writing, because nobody has and the ratio would be persuasive.

## Target Customer
Editorial leadership and daily writers, newsletter platform vendors, media companies operating newsletter portfolios, and editorial tooling vendors built for newsrooms.

## Impact If Built
A category that assumes a research desk does not build for someone who is the entire desk. Relevance learned from the publication's own archive is what makes sourcing compressible without producing output that must be fully verified anyway.
