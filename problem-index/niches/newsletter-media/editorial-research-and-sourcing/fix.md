# Fifty Tabs Every Morning

**Niche:** [[niches/newsletter-media/editorial-research-and-sourcing/profile|Editorial Research & Sourcing]]
**Industry:** [[industries/newsletter-media|Newsletter Media]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The same eleven versions of the same story are read separately, every day, because nothing groups them.
**Tags:** #quick-win #bert #automation #word-embeddings #worker-facing #evaluation-metrics #workflow-orchestration #k-nearest-neighbors
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to surface what is genuinely new and genuinely relevant to one specific audience from a stable source set — and whoever learns relevance from the publication's own archive replaces the hours that precede every issue.

## The Problem
The morning's reading is dominated by repetition. One event is reported by eleven outlets, each adding little, and the writer opens each one because the headlines differ enough that they cannot be sure. Most of the volume is duplication, some of the remainder is a restatement of something covered last week, and the genuinely new material is a small fraction that takes an hour to isolate.

## Why It's Still Broken
Feed readers present items chronologically, so the duplication is fully preserved — a chronological list is the simplest thing to build and the worst thing to read when most items are the same story. Deduplication is available in enterprise monitoring and not in the tools a small publisher uses. The writer's workaround functions. And the time cost is absorbed daily and never measured.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Group the duplicates and mark the familiar. Cluster items reporting the same event, which is the fix and removes the majority of the morning's reading volume. Show the primary source and the best account per cluster rather than all of them. Mark clusters the publication has already covered, since the repeat-coverage check is constant and manual. Surface what is new within a continuing story rather than re-presenting the whole thing, as ongoing stories are where duplication is worst. Rank clusters rather than listing them chronologically, because recency is a poor proxy for importance. Let the writer mark a story as done so it stops resurfacing. Keep the source list reviewable, since it accumulates and half of it is no longer read. Extract the key facts and numbers per cluster, as they are what the writer needs and are buried. Carry a cluster forward across days, which is how stories actually behave. And measure time spent reading, since it will make the case for all of it.

## Who Feels the Pain
Writers reading the same story eleven times daily; publications whose lead time is consumed before writing starts; and small teams whose only scaling lever is the writer's hours.

## Impact If Fixed
A chronological list is the simplest thing to build and the worst thing to read when most items are the same story. Clustering duplicates and marking already-covered stories removes the majority of the morning's volume before any modelling.
