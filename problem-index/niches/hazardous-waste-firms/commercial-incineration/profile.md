# Commercial Hazardous Waste Incineration

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/hazardous-waste-firms|Hazardous Waste Firms]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to guarantee generators a burn slot for the streams only a hazardous waste incinerator can take, and whoever honours the most committed tons through a capacity crunch takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$2.5B in US commercial hazardous waste incineration revenue (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~20% (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — units are automated and monitored; commitments and deferrals are managed by phone and spreadsheet
**Target Buyer:** VP of incineration or national accounts director at an incinerator operator
**Automation Potential:** Medium for capacity allocation and customer commitments; combustion control is already automated

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Some wastes have only one legal or practical outlet: halogenated organics, many listed wastes, unknown lab-pack contents, PFAS concentrates that generators want destroyed rather than landfilled, and residues from other treatment. A handful of operators own the commercial units. Clean Harbors has ten, including the new Kimball, Nebraska incinerator that should reach full capacity by end-2026, and Veolia six after the Clean Earth deal (search summary), with a 100,000-ton Arkansas unit at Gum Springs (search summary; as planned in 2024 for a 2025 opening, status unchecked). Heritage Thermal Services and Ross Incineration Services run others (estimate). When capacity is tight, the contest is not price but who keeps a generator's waste moving: whoever honours committed tons when others defer becomes the generator's primary outlet. Competitors are the operators themselves and brokers who hold capacity contracts with them.

## Current Tools & Gaps
TSDF ERPs and CRMs hold contracts and orders; plant schedulers plan feed in spreadsheets. Gaps: committed tons are not tracked against practical capacity net of outages; deferral risk is not forecast; and PFAS acceptance testing, which EPA recommends before taking large quantities, has no slot in the plan.
