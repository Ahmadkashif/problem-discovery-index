# Thermal Treatment: Incineration & Fuel Blending

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/hazardous-waste-firms|Hazardous Waste Firms]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** *Not terminal as stated.* The honest partial sentence is that every serious competitor is fighting to destroy generators' high-hazard and high-heat-value wastes inside a combustion permit, but incinerator operators win by guaranteeing scarce burn capacity to the streams only an incinerator can take, while fuel blenders win by turning liquids into fuel a cement kiln will accept. Decomposed into contested sub-niches below.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$3.5B in US commercial hazardous waste incineration and fuel-blending revenue (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~25% of the industry's receipts (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — combustion control and emissions monitoring are automated and regulated; capacity commitments, feed scheduling and outlet choice run on ERP queues, spreadsheets and phone calls
**Target Buyer:** VP of disposal operations or general manager of an incineration or fuel-blending facility
**Automation Potential:** Medium — feed planning and capacity allocation can be automated; combustion itself is already controlled to permit limits

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Thermal destruction is where the industry's scarce, permitted capacity sits. Clean Harbors operates ten commercial hazardous waste incinerators with 631,721 tons of practical capacity and ran all but its new Kimball unit at 89% utilization in 2025. Veolia, after buying Clean Earth, counts six US incinerators (search summary), and a broker put the 2023 backlog at 50,000–60,000 tons (search summary). Cement kilns that burn hazardous waste-derived fuel are the other thermal outlet, fed by fuel blenders who collect high-heat-value liquids and solids and blend them to the kiln's specification. Both run under the hazardous waste combustor emission standards (estimate), and both now sell PFAS destruction, which EPA's 2026 interim guidance lists among the lower-release options for permitted combustors. They compete for some of the same streams but win on different things, so the niche splits.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Distributed control systems and continuous emissions monitors run the units; TSDF ERPs (Terralink's TDX, WASTELINQ) hold profiles and orders; schedulers use spreadsheets. Gaps: no shared ledger of committed tons against practical capacity; no routing logic deciding, stream by stream, whether a profile goes to direct burn or to the blend tank; and capacity lost to outages and automatic waste feed cut-offs is not fed back into what sales promises.
