# Niche Analysis — Expert Networks

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/expert-networks|Expert Networks]]

## Niche Selection

An expert network sells a prediction — this person can answer your question — and observes its outcome within an hour, at a volume of hundreds of thousands of calls a year at the largest firms. Around that core sit a compliance apparatus that makes the calls purchasable by regulated investors, an expert database that decays between calls, a transcript library business that sells calls one-to-many, a survey business built on the same verified population, and two workers: the associate who sources the experts and the client analyst who has to make sense of what they said. The eight niches below split the category by what competitors actually fight over.

## Why These Niches

Custom call brokerage is most of the revenue and is where matching quality — the industry's real product — is decided, so it takes the largest share. Transcript libraries are second because they have changed the economics of a call from one buyer to many and are where the corpus accumulates. The two low-digitized niches are the ones the regulatory history created and the tooling never caught up with: compliance by attestation and sample, and an expert database whose most compliance-critical field goes stale between calls. The two underserved audiences sit at either end of the call — the associate learning matching by volume, and the client analyst left alone with the synthesis. The two automatable niches are the quantitative survey product built on the same verified population, and the synthesis layer above the calls, which every vendor is now racing to own.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. Custom Call Brokerage is not: the label names the product, and writing the contested statement produces two sentences with different winners. A public-equity analyst covering the same tickers every quarter buys non-redundancy against their own prior calls and the libraries, under the strictest MNPI posture, and the network that wins is the one whose expert adds something new and clears the fund's policy. A private equity deal team or diligence consultancy buys breadth inside a two-to-three-week clock, weighted to current customers of an often private target, with confidentiality of the deal rather than MNPI as the binding compliance concern, and the network that wins is the one that fills the call plan fastest with people who actually used the product. It therefore decomposes into **Public-Equity Coverage Calls** and **Deal-Sprint Diligence Calls**.

The Client Research Analyst and Cross-Call Synthesis are close and were tested for overlap. They are kept separate because the contests differ: the first is the client-side worker and is won by whoever owns the analyst's workflow, the second is the vendor-side product race among networks, libraries and research platforms and is won on faithfulness. Their build notes deliberately address different parties.

Three candidates were considered and rejected. **Life-sciences KOL engagement** — networks recruiting physicians for pharmaceutical companies — is a real revenue line but its contest is fair-market-value and transparency compliance for the pharmaceutical buyer, not research, and it belongs with pharmaceutical commercial operations rather than here. **Corporate (non-investor) clients** names a buyer rather than a contest; corporate strategy teams buy the same calls under the same matching contest. **Regional networks** (Asia-focused networks such as Capvision, whose offices were raided by Chinese authorities in 2023) is a jurisdiction rather than a contest; the cross-border regulatory risk is real but is a constraint on every niche, not a niche of its own.

## Pass 2 — Insight-Layer Discovery

Second, orthogonal sweep. Pass 1 split the category by contest; this pass sweeps seven positions in the value chain — aggregator/rollup, payer & intermediary, data & benchmark vendors, association research arms, specialist advisory & valuation, regulatory bodies, and suppliers selling into the industry — looking for a dedicated insight function of 10+ people whose analytical output is the billable deliverable. Full method in `_direction.md`. ## Why These Pockets

One qualifier, and it is the pocket where the industry's own product becomes a corpus. Transcript library platforms commission, host and publish expert calls as a subscription, which makes the transcript itself the invoice; production scales with analyst-hosted calls, a repeatable shape; and the corpus — tens to hundreds of thousands of dated, first-hand statements about products, pricing and competitors — is something no competitor can rebuild. The defect is the one this index keeps finding: the forecast is never graded. A large share of those statements later became publicly checkable, and no library has measured which kinds of expert claims prove right, so the product competes on size. Two adjacent defects sit with it: anonymisation done by editor judgement rather than measured re-identification risk, and call-host technique — the thing that decides a transcript's value — held tacitly by the best hosts.

Two pockets score above 50 and are blocked by kill switches, and the blocks are genuine rather than technical. Channel-check boutiques gather primary information about listed companies ahead of results, which is the exact territory of the expert-network insider-trading cases; and commercial due diligence advisors hold deep archives of sector work that are contractually the commissioning client's. Healthcare primary market research and expert-sourced data products fall just short — the first because study data is client-owned, the second because the labour mass behind a recurring index is small. The intermediary, association, rollup and buy-side compliance positions all fail the gate: the people who buy, police and consolidate expert networks do not run research functions of their own.
