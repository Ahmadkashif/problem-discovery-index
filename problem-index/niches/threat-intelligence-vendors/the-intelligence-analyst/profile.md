# The Intelligence Analyst

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/threat-intelligence-vendors|Threat Intelligence Vendors]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Whether an analyst can ever learn whether their judgement was right, in a discipline where the outcome is an attack that did not happen.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$400M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~8%
**Digital Adoption:** Low — research tooling, no feedback
**Target Buyer:** Vendor research leadership, analyst practice leads
**Automation Potential:** Moderate — collection and drafting automate, judgement does not

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

An intelligence analyst makes judgements for a living: that this activity is attributable to a particular group, that this campaign is likely to expand to a new sector, that this technique will be adopted more widely, that this infrastructure is under a particular actor's control.

None of those judgements is ever scored. Attribution assessments are made with stated confidence and almost never confirmed or refuted by anything the analyst sees. Predictions about campaign evolution are published and not revisited. And the reporting they produce disappears into a portal with no indication of whether anyone used it.

So a profession built on calibrated judgement operates with no calibration feedback whatsoever. An analyst with fifteen years of experience has made thousands of assessments and received confirmation on a handful — the ones where an arrest, an indictment or a leak happened to settle the question publicly.

This is an underserved audience because the entire apparatus of the job — collection tooling, analytical platforms, publishing workflow — serves producing the assessment and none of it serves finding out whether the assessment was right.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Analytical platforms for pivoting across infrastructure and malware data. Malware analysis environments. Collection tooling for underground sources. Graph-based link analysis. Writing and publishing workflow. Some use of structured analytic techniques, unevenly applied.

The gaps are about judgement and feedback. No assessment is recorded as a scoreable prediction, so nothing can be checked later when evidence arrives. Confidence language is applied inconsistently, which makes even self-assessment difficult. Attribution reasoning is narrative rather than structured, so a later contradiction cannot be traced to the specific inference that failed. Structured analytic techniques that reduce bias are known and rarely used under deadline. And the corpus of the firm's own past assessments is not queryable as a body of prior judgements, so an analyst cannot check what their team concluded about this actor two years ago without reading old reports.
