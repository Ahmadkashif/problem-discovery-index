# Fix: Sold on Volume Because Volume Is Comparable

**Niche:** Feed Quality Measurement
**Industry:** [[industries/threat-intelligence-vendors|Threat Intelligence Vendors]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Indicator counts and source lists are the only attributes a buyer can compare, so those are what vendors optimise and what the market rewards.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #revenue-impact #compliance #descriptive-statistics
**Contested on:** Whether a feed's value can be stated as a number, or remains a matter of collection breadth and analyst reputation.

## The Problem

A procurement process compares three threat intelligence vendors. The comparable attributes are indicator volume, source count, sector coverage claims, analyst headcount and credentials, and price.

None of those is quality. But they are numbers, and they appear on both sides of a comparison table, so they are what the decision is made on.

The predictable consequence is that vendors optimise them. More indicators is better in a bake-off, regardless of whether the additional indicators ever match anything. More sources is better, regardless of whether they overlap. The incentive points directly at volume, and volume is what customers receive — millions of indicators, most of which will never fire, delivered to a security team whose analysts must filter them.

The customer then experiences the consequence as alert fatigue and concludes the problem is their tooling or their staffing, when it is partly a procurement process that rewarded the wrong attribute.

Everybody involved understands this. Vendors know their feeds contain a great deal of inert content. Buyers suspect it. And the equilibrium holds because the alternative requires someone to publish a number that makes their product look worse than the volume figure does.

## Why It's Still Broken

**First-mover cost falls entirely on the mover.** A vendor publishing a match rate is compared against competitors who publish indicator counts, and the match rate will look small next to a number in the millions.

**Buyers do not know what to ask for.** Procurement teams ask for what vendors publish. Without an established metric there is no requirement to state, so the question is not asked.

**Customers could measure and do not.** Every organisation with telemetry could compute which feeds ever matched anything in their own environment. Very few do, because nobody owns feed evaluation and the analysis is not on anyone's objectives.

**Trials are too short and too shallow.** An evaluation period of a few weeks against a low base rate produces almost no matches either way, so trials cannot distinguish feeds and decisions fall back to the comparison table.

**Renewal runs on feeling.** The renewal question is whether the team found the intelligence useful, which is a judgement about reporting quality and relationship rather than about the feed.

**No independent evaluator exists.** Other categories have comparative testing bodies. Threat intelligence has academic studies with limited reach and no ongoing commercial evaluation anyone cites.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Customers should measure their own match rates.** Query the telemetry: which feeds produced matches, how many, and what happened to them. This costs an analyst a week, requires no vendor cooperation, and would inform every renewal decision far better than a comparison table. It is the single most useful thing a buyer can do and almost nobody does it.

**Ask for match rate in procurement.** A buyer requiring vendors to state the proportion of their indicators that matched anything at any customer in the last quarter changes the conversation immediately. Vendors that can answer will; those that cannot will explain why, which is also informative.

**Run longer, telemetry-based evaluations.** Replay historical telemetry against a candidate feed rather than running a short live trial. This produces a real match rate over a meaningful period in days, and it is technically straightforward.

**Measure overlap before buying the fourth feed.** How much of a candidate feed is already covered by existing subscriptions. Buyers routinely pay several times for substantially the same content and have never checked.

**Ask vendors for their own numbers and note who cannot produce them.** A vendor unable to say how often their indicators match is telling you something about whether they have looked.

**Support an independent evaluation body.** Comparative testing exists in other security categories and is what disciplined their vendor claims. Threat intelligence has no equivalent, and the buyers who would benefit are the ones who would have to fund it.

## Who Feels the Pain

The SOC analyst, investigating alerts generated by indicators that were shipped because volume wins procurement.

The security leader, renewing subscriptions on the basis of a feeling about usefulness, with no evidence either way.

Vendors with genuinely high-quality, lower-volume feeds, who lose bake-offs to competitors with larger indicator counts and no way to demonstrate the difference.

And the organisation, paying several times over for overlapping content it has never measured.

## Impact If Fixed

A customer computing their own match rates from their own telemetry is a week of work that would inform every subsequent procurement and renewal decision, and it requires nothing from any vendor.

Asking for match rate in procurement would, if a few large buyers did it, change what vendors optimise — which is the whole mechanism by which a market starts rewarding quality.

And measuring overlap before adding another subscription would stop the most straightforward waste in this category, which is paying four vendors for substantially the same indicators.
