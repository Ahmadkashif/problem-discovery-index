# Buy: Intelligence Requirements Practice From Government

**Niche:** Finished Intelligence Reporting
**Industry:** [[industries/threat-intelligence-vendors|Threat Intelligence Vendors]]
**Type:** Buy & Customize (Vertical Adaptation)
**One-liner:** Government intelligence built a discipline around stating what decision-makers need to know and measuring whether the product answered it, and commercial reporting publishes what analysts found.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #workflow-orchestration #worker-facing #compliance
**Contested on:** Whether a finished assessment reaches the person who can act on it in a form they can act on.

## The Problem

The intelligence discipline that commercial threat intelligence descends from has a structure the commercial version dropped.

Government practice begins with requirements: the consumer states what they need to know to make a decision, formalised into priority intelligence requirements that drive collection and production. Products are written against those requirements. There is a feedback mechanism through which consumers say whether the product answered the question. Analytic standards govern how confidence is expressed and how sourcing is described. And there are established structured analytic techniques for reducing bias in assessment.

Commercial threat intelligence has the analytical craft and none of the surrounding apparatus. Requirements are rarely stated — customers subscribe to a feed of reporting rather than commissioning assessments against questions. Production is driven by what analysts find interesting and what collection produces. Feedback is a portal page view. And confidence language varies between vendors and within them.

The tradition is well documented and public. The commercial world took the analysis and left the system that makes analysis useful.

## What Already Exists

Intelligence requirements practice: priority intelligence requirement frameworks, the intelligence cycle, requirements management and collection planning.

Analytic standards: published standards for analytic integrity, including consistent expression of confidence and likelihood, source description and the separation of judgement from evidence. The probability-language conventions are particularly transferable and particularly ignored commercially.

Structured analytic techniques: analysis of competing hypotheses, key assumptions checks, devil's advocacy and red teaming of assessments — a documented toolkit for reducing bias.

Feedback mechanisms: consumer feedback loops and production review, however imperfect, which at least exist.

Commercial: vendor portals, report libraries, tagging and advisory calls for large accounts.

## The Customization Gap

**Requirements are not gathered.** A customer subscribes and receives a stream. The most valuable thing a vendor could do is ask what decisions this organisation needs intelligence for and produce against that, which is a service model rather than a publishing model.

**Confidence language is inconsistent.** Analytic standards specify how likelihood should be expressed so that "likely" means the same thing every time. Commercial reporting uses the vocabulary loosely, which makes assessments harder to act on than they need to be and is fixable with a style guide.

**Feedback is not collected.** Even a light mechanism — did this answer a question you had, did you act on it — would transform what vendors know about their own product. Government practice has this and commercial practice does not.

**Structured techniques are rarely applied.** Analysis of competing hypotheses and key assumptions checks are cheap, documented and would improve assessment quality measurably, particularly on attribution, which is the most contested output in the field.

**The consumer is not identified.** Government products are written for a named consumer with a known decision. Commercial reports are written for a subscriber base, which means they are written for nobody in particular and have to serve everyone.

**Collection is not requirements-driven.** Commercial collection is driven by access and opportunity. Aligning it to stated customer requirements is the harder half and is what would make the model genuinely different.

## Target Customer

Vendors serving large accounts with dedicated intelligence functions, where a requirements-driven service model is viable and is what mature customers already ask for informally.

Customer-side intelligence functions, who should be stating requirements to their vendors and mostly do not — which is a practice change available to them immediately.

The whole category for the analytic standards, since consistent confidence language and structured techniques are cheap to adopt and would improve the product for everyone.

## Impact If Solved

Consistent confidence and likelihood language is a style-guide change that would make every assessment in the category easier to act on, and it is available now at no cost.

A feedback mechanism, however light, would give vendors the first information they have ever had about whether their reporting is used — which is the precondition for improving it.

And requirements-driven production would change the relationship from publishing to answering, which is what distinguishes intelligence that supports decisions from content that describes threats.
