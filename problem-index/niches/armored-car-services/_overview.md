# Niche Analysis — Armored Car Services

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/armored-car-services|Armored Car Services]]

## Niche Selection

An armored carrier sells custody of cash on a fixed network of vaults, trucks and armed crews, and the network is being reshaped from both ends: stops are thinning as retailers move to smart safes and branches and ATMs close, while the carriers sell more technology — provisional credit, ATM management, vault outsourcing — on top of the trucks. The eight niches below split the industry by what carriers and the vendors selling around them actually compete over: the two customer segments where the revenue sits, the two desks still run on paper and phone, two buyers the national tools ignore, and the two most mechanical workflows. Market sizes are estimates of carrier revenue, cost or tooling spend inside each niche; customer segments and functions overlap, so they do not sum to the industry's ~$3.5B of receipts.

## Why These Niches

Bank logistics and retail programmes take the market-share slots because they are where the receipts are, and because they now compete on different things: banks buy footprint coverage close enough to deliver the same day, retailers buy next-day credit without a daily truck. Loomis's US smart-safe line grew ~15% in reported kronor in 2025 (SEK 3.5B to 4.0B) while its US cash-in-transit line shrank ~7% (SEK 5.75B to 5.38B); currency hit both equally, so the twenty-point gap is the clearest sign of that split. The two low-digitized niches are where evidence still moves on paper and email: discrepancies between declared and counted value, and declared-value consignments of jewellery and metals, where the 2022 Lebec theft turned a manifest into a lawsuit. The two underserved audiences are rural institutions whose stops no longer pay for the drive, and the 86 small carriers that hold ~10% of receipts with courier-grade tools against national bundles. The automatable pair are the most data-rich and mechanical work in the industry: ATM fleets that report every dispense and fault, and counting rooms that sort every note to the Fed's fitness standard.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. ATM Managed Services is not: writing its contested sentence produced two contests with different winners. Keeping a machine supplied is an inventory contest between forecasting vendors, carriers' forecasting desks and banks' cash teams, won by whoever holds the least cash at the contracted cash-out rate. Keeping a machine working is an uptime and safety contest between carriers' service arms, ATM makers' service businesses and independent service organisations, won by whoever clears faults remotely and sends technicians only to genuine, risk-checked calls — a contest sharpened by the robbery schemes, tracked by the FBI since 2022, that stage jams to ambush technicians. Different data, clocks and competitors, so it decomposes into **ATM Cash Forecasting & Replenishment** and **ATM First-Line Service & Fault Dispatch**.

Two terminal calls were close. **Bank & Credit Union Branch Cash Logistics** and **Rural Banks & Credit Unions on Thin Routes** serve the same kind of customer; they are kept apart because the metro contest is footprint coverage — a vault near every branch — while the rural contest is density, packing enough stops onto a route to keep it running. **Retail Cash Pickup & Smart-Safe Programmes** could be split into traditional pickups and smart safes, but provisional credit now governs both: the retailer compares a daily pickup against a safe with next-day credit, and one decision decides the account.

Three candidates were rejected. **Cash-intensive merchants outside mainstream banking** — cannabis dispensaries, check cashers, pawnshops — were considered; their binding contest is whether a receiving bank will accept the deposits, a bank compliance decision rather than a carrier one, and the check-cashing side is already mapped under [[niches/money-service-businesses/check-cashing-centres/profile|Check Cashing & Neighbourhood Financial Service Centres]]. **Armed crew licensing and compliance** was rejected because its only contest ("track licences and renewals better") is generic and fails the filter. **Casino cage cash logistics** was considered and left to the queued casinos build, whose cage and count-room workflows are the larger part of that problem.

## Pass 2 — Insight-Layer Discovery

Second, orthogonal sweep. Pass 1 searched across the carriers themselves — 92 employer firms in the Census code, six of them taking ~90% of receipts — none of which sell insight as a product. This pass sweeps seven positions in the value chain — aggregator/rollup, payer & intermediary, data & benchmark vendors, association research arms, specialist advisory & valuation, regulatory bodies, and suppliers selling into the industry — looking for a dedicated insight function of 10+ people whose analytical output is the billable deliverable. Full method in `_direction.md`.

Several positions are already profiled under other industries and are not repeated. Site-level crime risk scoring, which carriers and insurers would use for route risk, is [[niches/security-guard-firms/crime-risk-data-providers/profile|Crime Risk Data & Forecasting Providers]] (48). ## Why These Pockets

Nothing qualifies, and the reason is the shape of the money. Cash is the customer's, so nearly every rich dataset in this value chain describes someone else's money held under a service agreement. The carriers' ATM forecasting desks (pocket 10) see dispense histories for thousands of machines across many owners and score 31 with a live "data isn't theirs" kill switch. The forecasting software vendors (15) and smart-safe makers (16) hold the same kind of client data but fail the gate first: counted per firm, analysts alone are fewer than ten, and the rest of their headcount builds, installs and supports products. The carriers themselves fail as buyers twice over: their analysis is a cost centre bundled into transport fees, and there are three of them. The operators' own concentration — six enterprises with ~90% of receipts — is what makes the industry hard to sell into, not what makes it rich in insight.

The strongest near-miss is the research houses that count ATMs and cash devices (34). Their reports are the invoice and their survey series are their own, but output is a few annual reports and projects, no third party sets their deadline, and only a handful of firms do the work. Their numbers disagree openly — 520,000–540,000 US ATMs by ATMIA's 2023 estimate against 427,500 by RBR's 2021 count — the same unmeasured-accuracy gap the vault's indexed data vendors share, at too small a scale to qualify.

The other gap worth recording is that nobody counts the attacks. The FBI's Bank Crime Statistics list 49 armored-carrier violations under the Hobbs Act for 2023 and just three robberies of armored carrier companies under the bank robbery statute, while FBI officials in Chicago alone described a tripling of armored car robberies that year. The best record sits with each carrier's corporate security department (pocket 14, which fails the gate because its analysts number fewer than ten per carrier), unpooled and unpublished; the industry's associations, the natural place for a shared incident record, have fewer than ten research staff between them.
