# Bank & Credit Union Branch Cash Logistics

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/armored-car-services|Armored Car Services]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to fill a bank's branch and vault cash orders on the next scheduled stop across its whole footprint, and whoever has a vault close enough to every branch to make same-day emergency deliveries takes the account.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$1.2B of US carrier revenue from branch deliveries, pickups and vault services for banks and credit unions (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~35% of receipts (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — orders flow through bank cash-management systems and carrier portals and are confirmed by scan, but schedule changes and emergency orders still go by phone
**Target Buyer:** Head of cash operations at a regional bank; VP of operations at a credit union
**Automation Potential:** Medium — ordering, inventory and planning automate; the delivery stays a truck and two armed people

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
This is the original armored business. A carrier delivers ordered currency and coin to branches before opening, picks up excess cash and commercial deposits, and runs vault services for the bank: holding its currency inventory, processing its ATM replenishment orders, and carrying its orders and deposits to and from the Federal Reserve cash office under the bank's Operating Circular 2 relationship. Contracts typically run one to three years. What the bank buys is coverage. A bank with branches across several metro areas needs a carrier whose vaults are close enough to every branch that a branch running short of $20s on a Friday can be resupplied the same day. That is why the national networks dominate this segment and why branch closures, which thin the stops on each route, hurt carrier margins here first.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Banks use their core systems' teller-cash modules, teller cash recyclers, the Fed's FedCash Services and forecasting software such as Morphis; carriers run order portals and vault inventory systems. The gaps: branch orders are set by branch managers by habit, ATM, branch and vault cash are planned separately, emergency orders arrive as phone calls with no recorded cause, and few banks see what their cash network costs per branch.
