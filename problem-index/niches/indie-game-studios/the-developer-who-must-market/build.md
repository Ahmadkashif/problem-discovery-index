# Marketing That Fits the Person Doing It

**Niche:** [[niches/indie-game-studios/the-developer-who-must-market/profile|The Developer Who Must Market]]
**Industry:** [[industries/indie-game-studios|Indie Game Studios]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** The commercial outcome depends on a sustained activity performed by someone temperamentally unsuited to it, alongside everything else.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #automation #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #descriptive-statistics
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to get eighteen months of consistent marketing out of someone who became a developer specifically because they did not want to do that — and whoever makes it survivable changes the commercial outcome of the whole sector.

## The Problem
Marketing an indie game requires showing work regularly for a year and a half to build the wishlist that determines the launch. The person doing it is a developer, often working alone, frequently uncomfortable with self-promotion, already at capacity, and aware that the thing they are worst at determines whether the thing they are best at reaches anyone. The advice available is written by people for whom this comes naturally and prescribes a volume of activity that is not achievable.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
Marketing tools are built for marketers, so they assume someone whose job this is — a product designed for a role does not fit a person doing that role as their fifth job. The advice ecosystem is written by the temperamentally suited. Nobody has measured what minimum viable marketing actually achieves. And the developer's discomfort is treated as something to overcome rather than to design around.

## What to Build
Design for the person rather than for the practice. Establish a minimum viable cadence and show what it achieves, which is the core — most developers do not know whether posting twice a week or twice a month is the difference, and the honest answer would relieve or focus enormous effort. Generate assets from the work itself, since a developer produces screenshots, clips and builds continuously and turning them into posts is the step that does not happen. Reduce the activity to something achievable rather than optimal, because an achievable practice sustained for eighteen months beats an optimal one abandoned in three. Support the formats that suit a reticent person — showing work rather than performing, writing rather than speaking, scheduled rather than reactive — as the standard advice assumes the opposite. Batch the work so it does not interrupt development daily, which is what makes it sustainable. Measure what actually converts to wishlists rather than what generates engagement, since those diverge and the developer is optimising the wrong one. Handle the events and festivals calendar, as those are disproportionately effective and are missed through inattention. Target by genre audience rather than by follower growth, which is where the effort is currently wasted. Tell the developer what to skip, since permission to not do things is the most useful advice available. And acknowledge the discomfort rather than prescribing through it.

## Target Customer
Solo and small-team developers, publishers whose portfolio depends on it, platform holders and festivals, and creator marketing tool vendors.

## Impact If Built
A product designed for a role does not fit a person doing that role as their fifth job, which is why marketing tools go unused here. Establishing a minimum viable cadence and generating assets from the work itself is what makes eighteen months survivable.
