# Rejected on Requirement 4.7.2

**Industry:** [[industries/indie-game-studios|Indie Game Studios]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The submission failed on a numbered requirement, the launch date is in three weeks, and the developer is reading a document to work out what it means.

**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to get a two-person team through four certification regimes written for studios with a compliance department — and whoever does it multiplies the reachable market without multiplying the team.

## The Problem
The rejection cites a requirement number and a brief description. The developer finds the requirement in a long document, interprets what it means for their engine and their game, guesses at a fix, rebuilds, resubmits and waits again. Each cycle costs days or weeks. The launch date was announced, the marketing was scheduled, and the delay compounds through everything downstream. The same requirements fail repeatedly across the sector and nobody has written down what they actually mean in practice.

## Why It's Still Broken
The rejection references a specification, so the interpretation is left to the reader — a notice that cites a rule assumes the reader can translate it, and these readers are two people who also do the art. Platform holders communicate through documentation. Studios do not share rejection experiences systematically. And each studio meets each requirement once.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Translate the requirement into what to do. Maintain a community translation of each common requirement into engine-specific terms, which is the fix and is exactly the knowledge that currently exists in scattered forum threads. Catalogue the requirements that fail most often, since a handful account for most rejections and knowing them in advance prevents most of the delay. Check the common failures before submission with a simple checklist or script, as most are mechanically detectable. Share rejection experiences across studios, because everyone is solving the same problems privately. Record what fixed each failure, which is the part that is always missing from the forum thread. Ask the platform holder for clarification in a structured way rather than individually, since the same question is asked repeatedly. Budget the submission cycles into the schedule, as planning for one pass is what turns a rejection into a crisis. Submit earlier relative to the launch date, which is the simplest protective change. Keep a per-platform checklist that carries to the next project. And publish the aggregate failure data, because it would tell platform holders which requirements are badly written.

## Who Feels the Pain
Developers decoding requirements under deadline; studios whose launch slips over a numbered clause; platform holders whose submission queues are full of the same failures; and every small team meeting the same problem independently.

## Impact If Fixed
A notice that cites a rule assumes the reader can translate it, and these readers are also doing the art. A community translation of the commonly-failed requirements into engine terms is knowledge that already exists in fragments and would prevent most of the delay.
