# Nobody Knows What a Good Wishlist Number Is

**Niche:** [[niches/indie-game-studios/commercial-forecasting/profile|Commercial Forecasting]]
**Industry:** [[industries/indie-game-studios|Indie Game Studios]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The studio has eleven thousand wishlists and no idea whether that is good.
**Tags:** #quick-win #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #time-series-forecasting #worker-facing #automation
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to tell a studio whether their game will sell while they can still change it — and the contest splits cleanly enough that it is not terminal.

## The Problem
The wishlist counter is the sector's most watched number and its meaning is folklore. A studio knows their figure, has heard conversion rules of thumb that vary by an order of magnitude, and cannot tell whether their curve's shape — steady accumulation, a spike from one event, a plateau — means anything. They plan a launch, a budget and sometimes a life around a number nobody has told them how to read.

## Why It's Still Broken
The platform reports the count and not its distribution, so a studio has a number with no reference — a metric shown without context will be interpreted by whatever folklore fills the gap. Nobody publishes the relationship between wishlist patterns and outcomes. Publishers know and do not share. And the advice circulating is anecdote from a handful of public postmortems.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Give the number a reference. Publish the distribution of wishlist counts and outcomes by genre and scope, which is the fix and could be assembled from public data plus voluntary contribution. Report the shape rather than the level, since a curve accumulating steadily and one that spiked once behave completely differently at launch. Show conversion rates by genre and price band, as the rules of thumb vary enormously and are applied universally. Track the studio's own curve against comparable trajectories, which is the interpretation they need. Flag the plateau early, because a stalled curve eighteen months out is the most actionable signal available. Relate wishlist patterns to launch outcomes with real data rather than with a ratio. Encourage studios to share outcomes anonymously, since the corpus benefits everyone contributing and nobody currently asks. Report the effect of events and festivals on the curve, as those are the main levers and their effect is guessed. Say plainly what the number cannot tell them, which is most of what it is currently used for. And publish the methodology, because this sector has been badly served by confident advice.

## Who Feels the Pain
Studios planning around a number they cannot read; developers making life decisions on folklore; publishers whose value rests partly on the asymmetry; and a sector where the most-watched metric is the least understood.

## Impact If Fixed
A metric shown without context will be interpreted by whatever folklore fills the gap, and this one is watched by everyone. Publishing the distribution of counts and outcomes by genre gives the sector's central number a meaning for the first time.
