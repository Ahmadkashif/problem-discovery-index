# The Capsule Everyone in the Discord Liked

**Niche:** [[niches/indie-game-studios/store-presentation-and-discovery/profile|Store Presentation & Discovery]]
**Industry:** [[industries/indie-game-studios|Indie Game Studios]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Three hundred people who have followed the game for a year voted, and none of them is the person the image has to work on.
**Tags:** #quick-win #hypothesis-testing #evaluation-metrics #descriptive-statistics #confidence-intervals #worker-facing #contrastive-learning #automation
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to make capsule art, a trailer and the first paragraph convert a browsing player into a wishlist — and whoever tests those choices rather than asking a Discord decides the launch a year in advance.

## The Problem
The studio posts three capsule options and the community votes. The community knows the game, knows the characters, recognises the references and is invested in the studio. The actual audience is a stranger scrolling past at speed who has never heard of it and will give the image a fraction of a second. The community reliably prefers the option with the most in-game detail, which is reliably the worst one at a small size for a stranger.

## Why It's Still Broken
The community is the only audience the studio has access to, so their preference becomes the decision — a selection made from the available sample will reflect that sample regardless of who the decision is for. Studios know this abstractly and have no alternative. Nobody measures the difference between community preference and stranger click-through. And the cost of getting it wrong appears a year later.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Test with people who do not know the game. Show the options to strangers in a browsing context rather than to the community in a poll, which is the fix and can be done cheaply with a small paid panel. Measure clicks rather than preference, since what people say they like and what they click differ systematically here. Show the image at the size it will actually appear, because a capsule judged at full resolution and seen as a thumbnail are different images. Place it among competing games rather than alone, as the real comparison is against everything else on the page. Ask whether a stranger can tell what the game is, which is the question the capsule actually has to answer and the community cannot judge. Test the genre readability specifically, since misreading the genre is the most common and most costly failure. Use the community for the things they are good at — spotting errors, reacting to details, testing the game itself — rather than for this. Report the divergence between community preference and stranger response, which will be instructive and will change how studios use their community. Test the trailer's first five seconds the same way. And do it before the page goes live, because the first impression is also the algorithm's.

## Who Feels the Pain
Studios whose page was designed for the wrong audience; developers who did everything right and got no clicks; communities whose generous participation misled the team; and a sector where the highest-leverage decision is made by the least representative sample.

## Impact If Fixed
A selection made from the available sample will reflect that sample regardless of who the decision is for. Testing capsules with strangers at thumbnail size among competitors costs very little and answers the question the community cannot.
