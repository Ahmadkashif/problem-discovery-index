# Eighteen Months, Every Time

**Niche:** [[niches/indie-game-studios/development-estimation/profile|Development Estimation]]
**Industry:** [[industries/indie-game-studios|Indie Game Studios]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Every project is planned at eighteen months, every project takes three years, and the plan is made the same way each time.
**Tags:** #quick-win #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #revenue-impact #worker-facing #time-series-forecasting #automation
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to tell a small team when their game will actually be finished — and whoever forecasts from recorded velocity rather than from optimism prevents the failure that kills more studios than bad games do.

## The Problem
The eighteen-month plan is close to a convention. It reflects what a team hopes, what a funding round covers, and what sounds achievable — not anything about how fast this team has ever worked. The same team, on their second project, plans eighteen months again, having taken three years on the first, because nobody wrote down what happened and the memory of an overrun fades faster than the optimism returns.

## Why It's Still Broken
The overrun is never recorded as a fact, so each project's plan starts from hope rather than from history — an experience that is not written down cannot inform the next decision, however vivid it was at the time. Studios that finished are relieved rather than analytical. Studios that did not are gone. And the convention is reinforced by everyone else using it.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Write down what happened and multiply. Record the original estimate and the actual duration at the end of every project, which is the fix and takes five minutes at the one moment the information is complete. Apply the studio's own historical ratio to the next estimate, since a team that took twice as long last time will probably take about twice as long again and nobody applies the correction. Publish the sector's distribution of estimate-to-actual ratios, which would make the convention visible as a convention. Plan the runway against the corrected estimate rather than the optimistic one, as that is the decision that determines survival. Record scope changes as they happen, since most of the overrun is scope and it is invisible in retrospect. Check in against the plan quarterly rather than continuously, which is achievable for a small team and is enough to see a divergence. Treat a doubling estimate as a scope decision rather than a schedule failure, because the realistic response is cutting. Tell funders the corrected number, since an unrealistic plan is a problem for them too. Ask other studios what their ratio was, as many will say and the answers would recalibrate everyone. And do the recording even if nothing else changes, because the next project is where it pays.

## Who Feels the Pain
Developers funding a year they did not plan for out of savings; studios that finished the game and not the year; funders holding projects that will not deliver on schedule; and a sector where the most common failure has an obvious and unapplied correction.

## Impact If Fixed
An experience that is not written down cannot inform the next decision, however vivid it was. Recording the estimate-to-actual ratio takes five minutes and is the only correction the next plan will ever have.
