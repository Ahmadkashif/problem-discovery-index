# The Contractor Layer of the Schedule

**Niche:** [[niches/indie-game-studios/the-contract-collaborator/profile|The Contract Collaborator]]
**Industry:** [[industries/indie-game-studios|Indie Game Studios]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Contract artists and composers staff most of an indie project and sit entirely outside the schedule that is supposed to predict it.
**Tags:** #workflow-orchestration #automation #worker-facing #evaluation-metrics #linear-regression #confidence-intervals #data-integration #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to make the freelance artist, composer and contractor who staffs most indie projects a predictable part of a three-year schedule rather than its largest variable — and whoever does it takes the account.

## The Problem
An indie project's schedule covers the core team's work. The contract portion — character art, environment art, animation, music, sound, writing, QA — is scoped in emails, tracked in someone's head, and delivered on a cadence that depends on the contractor's other clients and the studio's ability to pay. When the schedule slips, this is frequently where, and the studio finds out late because there is no shared view of it at all.

## Why Nobody Has Built This
Project management tools assume employees on a shared system, not contractors on their own. Freelance platforms are built for finding people rather than for running a multi-year engagement with them. Studios are too small to buy resource management software. And nobody has built for the contractor's side, where the cash flow risk actually lands.

## What to Build
Put the contract work inside the schedule rather than beside it. Model contractor deliverables as first-class schedule items with dependencies on the core team's work, which is the core — an artist waiting on a design decision and a designer waiting on art is the commonest silent delay and nothing surfaces it. Scope engagements in iteration rounds rather than in finished assets, since the revision count is where the estimate breaks. Give the contractor visibility of what they are blocking and what is blocking them, which they currently learn by asking. Track payment against milestone so the studio's runway problem becomes visible before it becomes a relationship problem. Handle asset handoff with versioning and the specification attached, rather than a file transfer and a message. Record rights, licence terms and credit at engagement time, because it is reconstructed painfully at ship. Maintain a working history per contractor — delivery reliability, revision rounds, responsiveness — as studios currently rely on reputation. Support the contractor's multi-client reality rather than assuming exclusivity, which is what makes generic tools unusable for them. Forecast the contract portion of the schedule separately, since it behaves differently from internal work. And make it free or near-free on the contractor's side, as they will not pay and their participation is the product.

## Target Customer
Indie studios, contract artists, composers and animators, publishers managing external development, and freelance platform vendors.

## Impact If Built
An artist waiting on a design decision and a designer waiting on art is the commonest silent delay and nothing surfaces it. Modelling contractor deliverables as schedule items with real dependencies makes the largest variable in the plan visible.
