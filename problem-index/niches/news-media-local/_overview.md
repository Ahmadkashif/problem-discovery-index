## Why These Niches

Community papers and local TV stations represent the dominant revenue pillars of local news, but face radically different tech stacks and cost structures — community papers run on manual pagination and print-first workflows, while TV stations have invested in broadcast automation but lag in digital monetization. Digital-only local news and print ad sales teams highlight the two ends of the digitization gap: digital-native outlets lack the back-office infrastructure that legacy papers built over decades, while print ad sales teams still operate on handshake deals and spreadsheets despite the industry's existential revenue crisis. Minority-language outlets and rural news deserts represent populations actively losing access to local journalism, each with distinct content, distribution, and economic constraints that mainstream news tools ignore. Obituary/classified operations and event calendar curation are the most automatable revenue and engagement workflows in local news — high-volume, rule-heavy, and currently consuming editor time that should go to reporting.

## Why These Pockets

This is an industry in structural decline — Pass 1 puts newsroom editorial staff down more than 60% from 2008 — and the insight layer above it has thinned accordingly. Most positions here score in the thirties, which is unusual and is itself the finding: association research, philanthropy research, and corporate analytics have all shrunk with the industry they serve.

One position has grown, and it is attached to the industry's most profitable and most cyclical revenue line. Political advertising tracking monitors every spot in every race across markets, and local broadcast is the largest destination for that spending. The trackers have near-complete occurrence and rate coverage, and sell it as a spend report: dollars by race, by market, by week, and share of voice.

The corpus supports something the entire industry assumes and nobody has measured. Media market boundaries cut across districts, so a voter in one county sees a saturation campaign while a demographically identical voter twenty miles away sees almost nothing — a natural experiment repeated in hundreds of races over a decade of tracking, joinable to public precinct results. Nobody has estimated advertising effects from it, partly because the customer asks for competitive intelligence and partly because a measurement firm that quantified the effect and found it modest would be selling its clients a conclusion they dislike.

The other structural note is about coverage. Political spending is shifting into connected television, digital video, and social, where targeting means no outside party can observe comprehensively — so completeness, the product's central claim, erodes each cycle unless the firm shifts from monitoring to honest estimation with stated uncertainty.

Elsewhere: audience measurement produces the currency the whole local market transacts on from panels that thin every year, and lands one point below threshold. And the public notice archives — foreclosures, probate, government contracting across states and decades — sit with press associations as a compliance archive.
