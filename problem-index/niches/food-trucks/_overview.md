## Why These Niches

Food trucks fragment along operating model (event circuit vs. fixed route vs. late-night), geography (urban vs. rural vs. mobile), and business function (location selection vs. permitting vs. operations). These 8 niches cover the two largest revenue segments (festival circuit and office lunch routes, together representing 60% of food truck revenue), the two most digitally neglected segments (late-night operators with no analytics and rural trucks with no tech infrastructure), the two most underserved populations (multi-jurisdiction operators drowning in permit paperwork and waste-plagued operators with no inventory tools), and the two highest-ROI automation targets (location intelligence and administrative automation, both rule-heavy and currently manual). Excluded: corporate catering trucks (covered under catering), commissary-based ghost kitchen trucks (different model), and ice cream/dessert trucks (distinct seasonal pattern).

This pass sweeps seven positions in the value chain — aggregator/rollup, payer & intermediary, data & benchmark vendors, association research arms, specialist advisory & valuation, regulatory bodies, and suppliers selling into the industry — looking for a dedicated insight function of 10+ people whose analytical output is the billable deliverable. ## Why These Pockets

**No new index entries**, and seven of thirteen pockets fail the gate outright — the highest proportion in the sweep. A $1.5B industry of 35,000 owner-operators does not support an insight layer, and this map is mostly a record of that.

Two findings are worth keeping. The first is that the industry's central problem already has an answer sold to somebody else. Pass 1 identifies location selection as the whole game — the same truck grossing $200 on a bad corner or $2,000 at the right event — and location analytics providers sell precisely that intelligence, at 52, to retailers and investors. They are the same pocket already indexed under `data-analytics-consultants`, so this is a cross-reference rather than a find, but it is a clean illustration of the sweep's premise: the operator's hardest question is somebody else's product, two layers up, priced for a buyer the operator is not.

The second is the shape of the gap that nobody fills. Permitting is described in Pass 1 as a jurisdictional nightmare where a single lapse shuts an operator down mid-shift, and the assembled knowledge of what each city, county, and health district requires is genuinely valuable, genuinely proprietary if anyone built it, and governed by hard deadlines. It scores well on every dimension except the one that matters here: the entire segment attempting it consists of firms of one to five people, because 35,000 owner-operators cannot fund more.
