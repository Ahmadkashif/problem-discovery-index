# Catalogue Acquisition & Underwriting

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/music-publishers|Music Publishers]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to forecast a catalogue's next ten years of earnings, song by song, more accurately than the rival bidders, and whoever forecasts best wins the catalogues worth winning and walks away from the ones that would be overpaid.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$150M a year of diligence, valuation and tooling spend by catalogue acquirers and their lenders (estimate). For context, one valuation firm alone priced 566 catalogues worth ~$13B in 2025 (Citrin Cooperman), across acquisitions, refinancings and asset-backed securities.
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~10% of US publishing earnings change hands or are refinanced in a typical year (estimate, from deal value at ~15x earnings)
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — statements arrive as data, but forecasts are spreadsheets rebuilt for each deal
**Target Buyer:** Head of acquisitions or chief investment officer at a catalogue fund or major publisher
**Automation Potential:** High for statement normalisation and forecasting, low for the bid itself

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Catalogues are now financial assets. Concord, Primary Wave, Chord, Reservoir, BMG and the majors compete for them, Sony Music Publishing agreed in 2025 to buy Recognition's ~45,000 songs for a reported ~$4B, and KBRA has rated about $8B of music royalty asset-backed securities since 2020. The contest is a single forecast: how each song's earnings will decline, stabilise or revive, by income type, after the buyer owns it. Older publishing catalogues trade around 15 times earnings (Citrin Cooperman), so a forecast that is 15% too generous on the songs that carry the catalogue is the difference between a good deal and a write-down. The Hipgnosis fund's sale of non-core songs at a 14.2% discount to its valuer's mark is the industry's reference case.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Data rooms with years of statements, Excel models built by the acquisitions team, third-party valuations from specialists and consumption data from Luminate or Chartmetric. The gaps: statements normalised by hand for each deal, decay assumed at catalogue level rather than modelled per song, revival and termination risk handled as judgement, and no record of how past forecasts compared with what the catalogue then earned.
