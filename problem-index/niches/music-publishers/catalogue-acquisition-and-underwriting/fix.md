# Forecasts Never Checked Against What the Catalogue Then Earned

**Niche:** [[niches/music-publishers/catalogue-acquisition-and-underwriting/profile|Catalogue Acquisition & Underwriting]]
**Industry:** [[industries/music-publishers|Music Publishers]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Every acquisition carries a forecast, and almost nobody goes back to grade it against the earnings that followed.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #hypothesis-testing #confidence-intervals #descriptive-statistics #quick-win #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to forecast a catalogue's next ten years of earnings, song by song, more accurately than the rival bidders, and whoever forecasts best wins the catalogues worth winning and walks away from the ones that would be overpaid.

## The Problem
The bid model is filed when the deal closes. Two years later the catalogue's statements arrive in the owner's royalty system, but nobody compares them with the forecast song by song. Whether the team's decay assumptions run high or low, and on which kinds of song, stays unknown until a valuation dispute forces the question, as it did at Hipgnosis.

## Why It's Still Broken
The bid model and the royalty system belong to different teams and use different song identifiers. The acquisitions team is rewarded for winning deals, not for grading them.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Store every bid forecast at song and source level in the same identifiers as the royalty system. Each quarter, report actual against forecast for every acquired catalogue, split by song age, genre and income type, so the next bid uses decay assumptions calibrated on the team's own history.

## Who Feels the Pain
Investment committees, fund investors and lenders relying on marks, and acquisitions teams whose next bid repeats the last one's bias.

## Impact If Fixed
A cheap, recurring report turns each acquisition into training data for the next. Over a few years it becomes the evidence base behind every mark the fund reports.
