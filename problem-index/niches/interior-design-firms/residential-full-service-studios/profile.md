# Residential Full-Service Studios

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/interior-design-firms|Interior Design Firms]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to become the single record from proposal to client invoice — selections, client deposits, purchase orders and markup — and whoever holds that record holds the studio's margin and its books.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$350M US spend on studio-management software, design-specialist bookkeeping and back-office services by residential studios (estimate)
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~55% of interior design revenue comes from residential full-service work (estimate)
**Digital Adoption:** Medium — most studios of three or more people run Studio Designer, Houzz Pro, DesignFiles or Programa, but tie them to spreadsheets, QuickBooks and email
**Target Buyer:** Principal designer or studio owner, often with the firm's bookkeeper or operations manager
**Automation Potential:** High for the financial record and procurement paperwork, low for the design itself

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
The full-service residential studio designs a home and then buys and resells nearly everything in it, from sofas and lighting to drapery and rugs, earning a trade-discount markup alongside its fee. That makes it a retailer, a project manager and a design practice at once, with client deposits and vendor deposits open for months. Its economics are decided less by design quality than by whether product margin survives freight, damage, returns, client concessions and the client who finds the item cheaper online. The software contest in this niche is about who owns that financial record: Studio Designer bought Mydoma and DesignDocs to cover it end to end; Houzz Pro bundles it with lead generation and 3D; Programa and DesignFiles lead with presentation and schedules.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Studio Designer and DesignManager are strongest on purchase orders and accounting; Houzz Pro, DesignFiles and Programa on clipping, boards and client-facing proposals; QuickBooks remains the ledger for most. The gaps: realised margin per item after everything that happens between proposal and install is not computed anywhere; price transparency is handled by awkward conversations rather than by fee structure; and the record stops at the approved proposal, not the client's reactions or the delivered room.
