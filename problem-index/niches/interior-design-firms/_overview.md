# Niche Analysis — Interior Design Firms

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/interior-design-firms|Interior Design Firms]]

## Niche Selection

An interior design firm sells a judgement about taste and then delivers a long chain of orders, deliveries and installations, earning as much from product markup as from fees in residential work, and working to franchisor and lease clocks in commercial work. The eight niches below split the industry by what vendors, service firms and the firms themselves actually compete over. Market sizes are estimates of software, services and outsourced spend within each niche, not of interior design revenue (the whole industry is ~$19.7B in the 2022 Economic Census).

## Why These Niches

Residential full-service studios take the first high-share slot because they are the largest segment by revenue and the one where studio-software vendors are visibly fighting — Studio Designer's purchase of Mydoma and DesignDocs, Houzz Pro's bundling — over who holds the financial record. Corporate workplace interiors takes the second because office fit-outs are the largest commercial segment and are bought around lease events with large budgets. The two low-digitized niches are where spreadsheets, PDFs and phone calls still run the work: hospitality spec books built against brand PIPs, and everything that happens to a to-the-trade order after the purchase order leaves the studio. The two underserved audiences are the one-person studio, the majority of firms by count, whose tools are shrunken versions of software for larger firms, and healthcare and senior living, where the strictest specification demands meet tools built for either homes or offices. The two automatable niches are the most mechanical steps in the workflow: turning a product into a complete specification line, and turning a selection into an image the client can approve.

## Filter Notes

Seven of the eight level-1 niches are terminal. Corporate Workplace Interiors is not: writing its contested sentence produced two contests with different winners. Before a lease is signed, firms compete for the broker's request — a test fit and a credible fit-out budget for each candidate building within a day or two — and the winner is whoever is fastest and closest on cost, because that firm usually gets the design commission. After occupancy, or ahead of a renewal, the contest is evidence: proving with measured utilisation how much space and what mix of settings the occupier needs. Different clocks (the broker's tour versus the renewal decision), different buyers (the tenant-rep broker versus the head of workplace) and different inputs (floor plates and cost data versus badge, booking and sensor data), so it decomposes into **Broker Test-Fits & TI Budgeting** and **Workplace Utilisation & Space Strategy**.

Two terminal calls were close. **Residential Full-Service Studios** and **To-the-Trade Procurement & Receiving** both touch procurement. They are kept apart because the contests differ: in the first, studio-software vendors compete to hold the financial record from proposal to invoice, and the buyer is the principal; in the second, the contest is physical status — where the item is and whether it is right — and receiving warehouses and procurement services compete alongside software. **Product Specification & Tearsheet Production** was also tested against Hospitality FF&E: the hospitality contest is turning a PIP into a priced, compliant scope by a franchisor deadline, while the tearsheet contest is product-data accuracy for any project, so they stay separate.

Three candidates were considered and rejected. **Kitchen and bath design** was rejected because much of it is done inside cabinet dealers and showrooms classified outside this NAICS code, and the contest there is cabinet sales. **Home staging** was rejected as a different service with a real-estate buyer and rented inventory; its contest belongs closer to residential real estate than to design. **Sustainability and material health certification consulting** was considered; its contested sentence came out as "help clients choose healthier materials", which is generic, and the specific parts (product attribute data) are already inside the tearsheet niche.

## Pass 2 — Insight-Layer Discovery

Second, orthogonal sweep. Pass 1 searched across the operator layer, where firms are mostly under 15 people and research functions do not exist. This pass sweeps seven positions in the value chain — aggregator/rollup, payer & intermediary, data & benchmark vendors, association research arms, specialist advisory & valuation, regulatory bodies, and suppliers selling into the industry — looking for a dedicated insight function of 10+ people whose analytical output is the billable deliverable. ## Why These Pockets

No pocket qualifies, and that is the finding. Interior design firms fail the gate themselves: even the largest practices run research groups of a few people whose output is pitch material, and the insight a firm sells — its read of a client's taste — is invoiced as design, not as research. The positions around the firms split into two kinds. Where the insight is the invoice (forecasting, A&D market research, workplace benchmarking, valuation, cost consulting), the teams are small, the clocks are self-set or the corpus leans on published sources. Where the corpus is genuinely unique (Material Bank's sample-request stream, e-design platforms' taste data, hotel brands' standards and inspection records, dealers' order histories), the data is monetised through logistics, commissions or furniture sales rather than insight, or sits in an incumbent's schema.

The near-misses cluster around the hospitality and office capital cycle rather than around design. FF&E valuation (48) has the cleanest shape — the report is the invoice and rendition deadlines and closings are hard clocks — but its cost basis leans on published cost services, and the larger real-property version of the same business already qualified under [[niches/commercial-real-estate/property-tax-appeal-consultancies/profile|🔍 Property Tax Appeal Consultancies]]. Fit-out cost consultants (46) hold thousands of completed-project costs but bundle estimates with project management and form a thin buyer market. Workplace experience benchmarking (46) owns the largest proprietary corpus in the sweep — the Leesman dataset of about 1.3 million respondents — and fails on clock and labour mass, not on data.

The strongest data asset nobody invoices is the taste record. E-design platforms and sample platforms both observe, at scale, what designers and clients choose and reject — the same signal the industry's high-impact problem needs — and neither sells it as insight. That makes this an industry where the commercial opening for research tooling sits with studio-software vendors and platforms building it into their products, not with an insight engine sold to a research team.
