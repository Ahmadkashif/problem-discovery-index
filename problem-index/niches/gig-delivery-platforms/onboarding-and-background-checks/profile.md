# Onboarding, Background Checks & Identity

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/gig-delivery-platforms|Gig Delivery Platforms]]
**Category:** Highly Automatable
**Contested on:** Whether an automated screening decision that excludes someone from an income source is accurate, contestable and proportionate to the work.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$3.6B — 4% of US gross order value
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~4%
**Digital Adoption:** High — fully automated and consequential
**Target Buyer:** Platform trust & safety and supply growth
**Automation Potential:** Very high, already realised; the open work is accuracy, dispute and proportionality

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

Onboarding a courier is automated end to end: identity documents, a selfie match, a driving record check where a vehicle is involved, and a criminal background screen, returning a decision in hours. Millions of these run a year.

The niche is contested on what happens when the screening is wrong or disproportionate. Background check data in the US is assembled from county and state records of varying quality, and misattribution to people with common names, records that should have been sealed or expunged, and stale dispositions are well-documented problems. A wrong result excludes someone from an income source with an appeal process that requires them to obtain court records themselves. The Fair Credit Reporting Act imposes real obligations here — notice, a copy of the report, an opportunity to dispute — and the quality of compliance across the category is uneven.

It is distinct from the deactivation niche because this is entry rather than removal, and because the decision rests on a third-party data product rather than on the platform's own observations.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Screening is bought from Checkr, Sterling, HireRight and similar, with identity verification from the established vendors. Continuous monitoring — re-screening on new records rather than annually — is increasingly standard. Adjudication matrices encode which findings disqualify, sometimes with jurisdictional variation reflecting fair-chance hiring laws.

The gaps are accuracy measurement, dispute experience and proportionality. Platforms do not measure their own false-exclusion rate, because a rejected applicant who does not dispute produces no signal. Dispute processes are slow, documentation-heavy and abandoned by most who start them. And adjudication matrices are frequently blunt — a decade-old unrelated conviction disqualifying someone from delivering groceries — set conservatively because nobody is accountable for the people excluded.
