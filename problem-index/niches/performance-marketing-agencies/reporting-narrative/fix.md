# Every Chart Gets a Sentence

**Niche:** [[niches/performance-marketing-agencies/reporting-narrative/profile|Reporting Narrative]]
**Industry:** [[industries/performance-marketing-agencies|Performance Marketing Agencies]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The report explains all fourteen metrics including the eleven that did nothing, which buries the two findings that mattered and takes three hours to write.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #change-point-detection #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #quick-win #automation #workflow-orchestration #worker-facing
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to write what happened rather than to move data into a chart — and whoever automates the part that consumes the week takes the value the pipeline vendors left on the table.

## The Problem
The weekly report has a slide per metric and a sentence per slide. Eleven of the fourteen metrics moved within their ordinary range and the sentences about them say things like impressions were slightly down week on week. Two metrics did something genuinely notable and their sentences look identical in weight to the other eleven. The client reads all fourteen, or more likely reads none, and the two findings that should have driven the conversation are indistinguishable from the noise. The writer spent three hours producing eleven sentences that convey nothing and two that are buried among them.

## Why It's Still Broken
The report template has a slide per metric, so every metric gets narrated whether or not it did anything — the template determines the work and nobody questions the template. Omitting a metric looks like hiding it. Nobody has defined what counts as notable. And a long report reads as thorough, which is a signal agencies are reluctant to give up.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Report the exceptions. Define notability statistically against each metric's own normal variation, which is the fix, is a straightforward calculation, and immediately reduces fourteen commentaries to two or three. Lead with the findings and relegate the rest to an appendix the client can consult, which preserves completeness without burying the message. Write a single summary paragraph as the top of every report, since that is what will actually be read and it currently does not exist. Say explicitly when nothing notable happened, which is honest, is frequently true, and is far more useful than eleven sentences of padding. Account for seasonality so predictable movement is not reported as a finding, which is the most common false positive. Rank findings by consequence rather than by slide order. Keep the full data available and unnarrated, since the client should be able to look without the agency writing about everything. Agree the notability threshold with the client, so the omissions are a shared method rather than a judgement call. Reuse the definition across accounts to make reports comparable and the practice teachable. And measure report length and production time, because both will fall substantially and the freed hours are the whole point.

## Who Feels the Pain
Account teams writing eleven sentences that say nothing; clients reading reports that bury their own findings; and agencies whose weekly deliverable demonstrates thoroughness rather than insight.

## Impact If Fixed
The template determines the work, so every metric is narrated whether or not it moved and the two real findings are buried. A statistical notability threshold cuts fourteen commentaries to two or three and makes the summary paragraph possible.
