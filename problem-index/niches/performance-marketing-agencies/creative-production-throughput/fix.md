# The Review Round That Takes Four Days

**Niche:** [[niches/performance-marketing-agencies/creative-production-throughput/profile|Creative Production Throughput]]
**Industry:** [[industries/performance-marketing-agencies|Performance Marketing Agencies]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The asset took two hours to make and four days to approve, and nobody has ever measured which of those numbers to attack.
**Tags:** #workflow-orchestration #automation #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #quick-win #worker-facing #convex-optimization #revenue-impact
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to produce assets at the rate the platforms consume them, at a cost that survives the fee — and whoever wins on cost per asset and cycle time takes the volume work the whole category depends on.

## The Problem
The designer made the asset in two hours. It then sat: waiting for an internal reviewer who was in meetings, then with the account team who batched it with others, then with the client who reviews on Thursdays, then back for a revision that took twenty minutes, then round again. Four days elapsed, of which perhaps three hours were work. Every improvement effort in the category targets the making — faster tools, generative production, better templates — because that is the visible part. The waiting is ninety percent of the cycle and nobody has ever put a number on it.

## Why It's Still Broken
Time is tracked on work performed rather than on elapsed cycle, so the waiting is invisible in every system the agency runs — you cannot attack what you do not measure, and the measurement is structurally absent. Client review cadence is treated as fixed. Batching feels efficient to each individual reviewer while being catastrophic for flow. And the making is where the craft is, so that is where attention goes.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Measure the elapsed cycle, then attack the waiting. Instrument brief-to-live time with the waiting broken out by stage, which is the fix, needs only timestamps at each handoff, and reliably shows that making is a small fraction of the total. Reduce batch size, since reviewing ten assets on Thursday is what creates the four-day wait and reviewing two daily is faster for everyone including the reviewer. Agree a review service level with the client explicitly, because the cadence is assumed rather than negotiated and most clients will agree to something faster when shown the cost. Approve at the concept level so derived assets do not each require a decision. Route reviews to whoever is available rather than to a named person, which removes the single largest source of variance. Give reviewers a fast, specific interface rather than a thread, since much of the delay is the effort of responding. Limit work in progress, because a pipeline full of half-finished assets is slower than one with fewer, and this is counterintuitive enough that it needs the measurement to be accepted. Auto-approve low-risk derivatives under a stated policy, which is most of the volume. Report cycle time to the client as a shared metric, since they are half the problem and cannot help while it is invisible to them. And target elapsed time rather than utilisation, because a fully utilised team with a four-day cycle is losing the account's media performance regardless of how busy it looks.

## Who Feels the Pain
Designers whose work waits four days to run; accounts whose creative arrives after the concept has decayed; and agencies optimising the wrong ten percent of their process.

## Impact If Fixed
Time is tracked on work performed rather than elapsed cycle, so the waiting that is ninety percent of the process is invisible in every system. Timestamps at each handoff make it visible, and batch size and concept-level approval are what actually move it.
