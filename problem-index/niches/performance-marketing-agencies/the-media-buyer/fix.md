# The Budget That Went Somewhere

**Niche:** [[niches/performance-marketing-agencies/the-media-buyer/profile|The Media Buyer After Automation]]
**Industry:** [[industries/performance-marketing-agencies|Performance Marketing Agencies]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** The client asks where their money went and the honest answer is that the platform decided and will not say, which is not an answer anyone can give in a meeting.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #worker-facing #compliance #quick-win #revenue-impact #causal-inference
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to give the buyer visibility and leverage inside a black box they are still accountable for — and whoever does that restores a profession the platforms hollowed out while keeping its responsibilities.

## The Problem
A client asks a reasonable question: of the money we spent last month, how much went to which audiences, placements and formats, and what did each produce. Under the automated campaign types the answer is largely unavailable — the reporting aggregates in ways that prevent it, some breakdowns are withheld entirely, and the platform's own interface will not disaggregate. The buyer knows this. The client does not, and hears either a vague answer or a confident one that was constructed. Neither is good, and the second is how trust is lost when it later comes apart.

## Why It's Still Broken
The reporting granularity is the platform's choice and nobody has the leverage to change it individually, which is a genuine constraint rather than an agency failing. Admitting the limit sounds like incompetence to a client who remembers when the answer existed. Agencies compete on appearing in control. And nobody has framed the disclosure as a shared industry position rather than one firm's weakness.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Say clearly what is known and reconstruct the rest honestly. State the reporting limits explicitly in every report, which is the fix, is uncomfortable once and protective thereafter — a client who understands the limit stops asking for the impossible and starts asking better questions. Reconstruct what can be reconstructed from the breakdowns that do exist, and label it as an estimate with its method. Report at the level the data supports rather than at the level the client requested, with the gap named. Use controlled tests to establish composition where reporting will not, which is slow, costly and sometimes the only route. Set expectations at the start of an engagement rather than in the meeting where the question is first asked. Give the client the industry context, since this is a structural change affecting every advertiser and framing it as universal is both true and far more credible than an agency-specific explanation. Focus the reporting on what the client can act on, since composition is interesting and the actionable levers are elsewhere now. Advocate collectively for better reporting, which is the only route to actually fixing it and which agencies could organise and have not. Document each platform's disclosure limits in a standing reference, so every agency is not rediscovering them. And never construct an answer that cannot be supported, because the alternative is a confident explanation that unravels and takes the relationship with it.

## Who Feels the Pain
Buyers who cannot answer a fair question; clients paying for spend they cannot account for; and agencies whose credibility depends on not being asked.

## Impact If Fixed
The granularity is the platform's choice and the buyer takes the meeting, so the honest answer sounds like incompetence. Stating the limits explicitly and framing them as a structural industry change is uncomfortable once and protects the relationship thereafter.
