# The Phase I Report That Does Not Set Up Phase II

**Niche:** [[niches/contract-research-labs/sbir-microfirms/profile|SBIR-Funded Microfirms]]
**Industry:** [[industries/contract-research-labs|Contract Research Labs]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Phase I final reports are written to close out a contract, when they are the main evidence a Phase II evaluator and the technical point of contact will use to decide whether to fund the next phase.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #evaluation-metrics #quick-win #revenue-impact #worker-facing
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor serving these firms is fighting to turn a capped number of proposal slots into Phase II awards — agencies set per-firm proposal limits from FY2027 — and whoever helps a firm pick the topics it can convert and set up Phase II from its Phase I results takes the account.

## The Problem
At DoD, 28% of Phase II proposals were selected in FY2022 (National Academies). The Phase I work is the strongest argument, but the final report is written in the last week of the contract as a lab notebook summary: what was tried, what was measured. It rarely states the feasibility conclusion against the topic's stated objectives, the remaining risks, and the Phase II plan the technical point of contact would need to champion.

## Why It's Still Broken
The founder is already writing the next proposal; the report template asks for work performed, not for an argument; and nobody compares it to the Phase II evaluation criteria.

## What a Fix Looks Like
A report structure built from the topic's Phase I objectives and the agency's Phase II criteria, with an LLM drafting each objective's feasibility finding from the firm's results for the PI to correct, and a one-page Phase II case the technical point of contact can forward.

## Who Feels the Pain
Founder-PIs who did good Phase I work and lost Phase II; technical points of contact who wanted to champion them and had nothing to forward.

## Impact If Fixed
A report that argues for Phase II costs a day more to write and raises the odds on the most valuable proposal the firm will submit that year.
