**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in this niche is fighting to decide which of its own games should send players to which others, when the cost of moving a player between titles has never been measured — and whoever measures it takes the account.

## The Problem
Corporate finance confronted this long ago: when one division supplies another for free, both make decisions that are wrong for the group. The answer was transfer pricing — internal prices that reflect opportunity cost, so a division consuming an internal resource faces its real value. The theory is standard, the practice is universal in multi-division businesses, and it exists precisely because zero-priced internal resources are misallocated. Cross-promotion inventory is priced at zero.

## What Already Exists
Transfer pricing methodologies; opportunity cost based internal pricing; divisional performance measurement net of internal transfers; internal market mechanisms; and group-optimal allocation frameworks.

## The Customization Gap
The adaptation is to an asset that is a person with their own future behaviour. It requires: (1) the transferred resource being a player whose value in either location is uncertain and depends on their fit, so the price is a distribution rather than a rate — this is the substantive difference from any intermediate good; (2) an opportunity cost that must be estimated counterfactually rather than read from a market; (3) the possibility that the transfer destroys value in both places, which has no manufacturing analogue; (4) allocation decisions made continuously per impression rather than periodically; and (5) game teams with strong incentives to dispute any price that reduces their installs.

## Target Customer
Publishers with portfolios, portfolio managers, finance functions, and portfolio analytics vendors.

## Impact If Solved
Transfer pricing exists because zero-priced internal resources are misallocated, and the practice is universal. A transferred resource that is a person whose value depends on fit makes the price a distribution rather than a rate.
