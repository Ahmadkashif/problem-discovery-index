# The Employee Who Clicked

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/security-awareness-training|Security Awareness Training]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Whether being deceived by a professionally designed message is treated as information about the message or as a failing of the person.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$240M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~8%
**Digital Adoption:** Low — a list and an assignment
**Target Buyer:** HR, works councils, employee representation, security leadership
**Automation Potential:** Low — this is a design and fairness question

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

Someone clicked a well-crafted fake email during a busy afternoon. They are now on a list, assigned training, and in some organisations discussed with their manager.

Their position has features that make it distinct. They did not consent to the test and could not decline. The message was designed by professionals specifically to be convincing. The result is attached to them individually, in a system they cannot see, on a basis they cannot contest. The difficulty of the item they received is not recorded, so someone who clicked a hard lure and someone who clicked an obvious one are treated identically. And in a substantial number of organisations the consequence extends beyond training to visibility, escalation or worse.

The effect is to teach the workforce that engagement with anything that might be a test carries risk, which is precisely the opposite of what the programme needs. The behaviour the organisation depends on is people coming forward when something looks wrong, and the person who has been listed for clicking has learned that coming forward is not free.

This is an underserved audience because the entire apparatus is designed around measuring them and none of it is designed around them.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Post-click landing pages explaining that this was a simulation. Automatic training assignment. Repeat-clicker tracking and escalation policies. Manager visibility in some configurations. Departmental leaderboards in others. Works council consultation where required.

The gaps are about fairness and what the experience teaches. The difficulty of the item is not recorded with the result, so an individual's record does not distinguish a hard lure from an easy one. There is no route to contest a result. Landing pages frequently read as admonishment rather than explanation. Repeat-clicker escalation treats a workflow or exposure problem as a discipline problem. Nothing is done to encourage the person to report next time, which is the behaviour that actually matters. And nobody measures what the experience does to their willingness to come forward.
