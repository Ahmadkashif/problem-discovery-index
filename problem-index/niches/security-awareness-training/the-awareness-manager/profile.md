# The Awareness Manager

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/security-awareness-training|Security Awareness Training]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Whether a role built on deceiving colleagues and then asking for their trust can be made coherent.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$180M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~6%
**Digital Adoption:** Low — a dashboard and a contradiction
**Target Buyer:** Security leadership, awareness programme owners
**Automation Potential:** Moderate — the operations automate, the position does not

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

The job is to send colleagues fake emails designed to deceive them, assign them training when it works, and then ask those same colleagues to trust the security team enough to come forward when something looks wrong.

That contradiction is the role. It is usually held by one person, frequently early in their career, reporting into security, with no authority over HR, communications or the business units whose people they are testing. They absorb the complaints when a campaign lands badly, defend the programme when its metric is questioned, and are the human face of an intervention many employees resent.

They are also measured on the wrong thing. Click rate is what their dashboard reports and what leadership asks about, and they frequently know it is partly a statement about their own campaign design choices. Making campaigns harder produces a worse number and more complaints. Making them easier produces a better number and a quieter life. The incentive is clear and they are the person holding it.

This is an underserved audience because every product in the category is built for running the programme and none is built for the position the person running it is in.

## Current Tools & Gaps

The simulation and training platform, with campaign scheduling, template selection, reporting and assignment. Vendor customer success support. Peer communities of varying quality. Internal relationships with HR and communications, built personally.

The gaps are about the position rather than the workflow. Nothing helps them make the case for a harder campaign against the metric it will worsen. Nothing supports the internal negotiation with HR, communications and works councils that a well-designed programme requires. There is no framework for the ethical judgements they make routinely and alone. Nothing measures the trust cost they are the first to notice. Their career path leads out of the role, so the accumulated judgement leaves with them. And they have no peer standard to point at when arguing against a practice they think is harmful.
