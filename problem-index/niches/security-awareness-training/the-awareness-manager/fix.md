# Fix: The Harder Campaign Makes Their Number Worse

**Niche:** The Awareness Manager
**Industry:** [[industries/security-awareness-training|Security Awareness Training]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Running a realistic campaign produces a spike in click rate, complaints from the business, and questions from leadership about why the programme is going backwards.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #worker-facing #hypothesis-testing #revenue-impact #descriptive-statistics
**Contested on:** Whether a role built on deceiving colleagues and then asking for their trust can be made coherent.

## The Problem

The awareness manager runs a genuinely realistic campaign. It resembles the phishing the organisation actually receives: a plausible sender, a real internal reference, good timing.

Thirty-one per cent click, up from four.

What follows is predictable. Complaints from two departments about the message being unfair. A question from the security director about why the number went backwards. A request from a business unit head to be excluded from future campaigns. And a conversation about whether the programme is working.

The manager knows the number is a better measurement than the four per cent was. They have no way to demonstrate it, because difficulty is not measured, so their explanation is an assertion against a chart.

The rational response is available and obvious. Return to the easier templates. The number recovers, complaints stop, leadership is satisfied, and the programme measures progressively less while reporting progressively better.

Every awareness manager understands this trade. Most have made it at least partly. And it is the mechanism by which an entire industry's headline metric drifted away from meaning anything.

## Why It's Still Broken

**Difficulty is not measured, so rigour cannot be defended.** Without a difficulty parameter the manager cannot show that the harder campaign was harder, only claim it.

**The metric is reported upward without context.** Leadership sees a trend line. Nothing on it indicates that campaign difficulty varies.

**Complaints have an immediate channel and quality does not.** A business unit head who objects reaches the security director directly. The argument for realistic testing has no equivalent route.

**The manager is junior.** Defending an unpopular number against a director and a business unit head requires standing the role usually does not have.

**Exclusion requests are hard to refuse.** A senior person asking to be excluded is asking the manager to make an exception, which is exactly the population that most needs testing.

**Nobody has set the expectation in advance.** The spike arrives as a surprise, which frames it as a failure rather than as a designed measurement.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Announce the difficulty change before the campaign.** Tell leadership in advance that this campaign is realistic, that the number will rise, and that the rise is the point. Setting the expectation beforehand converts a failure into a finding, and it costs one email.

**Report difficulty alongside the rate, however crudely.** Even a stated rubric — sender plausibility, pretext specificity, personalisation — applied consistently, gives the manager something to point at.

**Keep a fixed anchor set.** A small unchanged template set run periodically, whose rate is comparable over time regardless of what else the programme does. This is the manager's defence against the drift argument and it costs almost nothing.

**Make reporting rate the headline.** A realistic campaign that produces a high click rate and a high report rate is a good result, and framing it that way changes the conversation entirely.

**Get the harder-campaign policy agreed in advance.** Leadership agreeing annually that the programme will include realistic testing, with an expected effect on the metric, removes the argument from the moment it is least winnable.

**Refuse exclusions as policy, not as a judgement.** A stated rule that nobody is excluded, agreed at the top, so the manager is applying a policy rather than declining a senior person's request.

**Give the manager air cover.** The security director should present the realistic campaign result and own the explanation, because the person who cannot win that argument should not be the one having it.

## Who Feels the Pain

The awareness manager, penalised for running a better test, defending an unpopular result without standing, and learning that the easier campaign is the safer choice.

The organisation, whose programme drifts toward comfortable measurement because every incentive at the individual level points that way.

The security director, receiving a trend line they believe means something, from a programme whose design has been shaped by the pressure they apply to it.

And the workforce, tested against material that increasingly does not resemble what actually reaches them.

## Impact If Fixed

Announcing the difficulty change in advance is free and converts the spike from an apparent failure into a designed finding, which is most of the fix.

A fixed anchor set gives the manager an unarguable comparison over time and costs a handful of templates.

And agreeing the realistic-testing policy annually at leadership level moves the argument to a moment when it can be won calmly, rather than to the week the number jumps.
