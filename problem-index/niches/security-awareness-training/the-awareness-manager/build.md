# Build: Support for an Incoherent Position

**Niche:** The Awareness Manager
**Industry:** [[industries/security-awareness-training|Security Awareness Training]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Tooling and framework for the person in the middle — a metric that does not punish rigour, a design framework for the ethical calls, and support for the internal negotiation the role requires.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #bayesian-inference #compliance #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #hypothesis-testing
**Contested on:** Whether a role built on deceiving colleagues and then asking for their trust can be made coherent.

## The Problem

The awareness manager faces a set of decisions weekly that nobody has given them a framework for.

Should this campaign use a lure referencing the bonus cycle? It would work extremely well and would probably cause distress. Should the results go to managers? It would increase engagement and would damage trust. Should the campaign be harder this quarter? It would be a better test and would make their number worse, which is the number leadership asks about. Should the repeat clicker in accounts payable be escalated? Policy says yes and they suspect the person's workflow is the actual problem.

Each is a real judgement with competing considerations, and they make it alone, frequently without the seniority to push back on a policy they think is wrong, and without any professional standard to cite.

They also carry the internal relationships. A programme that is well designed requires agreement from HR about consequences, from communications about tone, from works councils about consent, and from business units about timing. All of that is relationship work performed by one person with no formal standing.

And their metric actively opposes good practice. Harder campaigns, honest reporting of harm, and non-punitive design all make the reported number worse.

## Why Nobody Has Built This

**The product serves the programme, not the person.** Platforms are built to run campaigns and report results. The manager's decisions are outside the product's frame.

**The role is small and dispersed.** One person per organisation, across many organisations, with no budget of their own and no collective voice.

**The ethical questions are uncomfortable for the vendor.** A design framework naming the harms would describe the vendor's own templates, which is not a product decision anyone has taken.

**The metric is the vendor's too.** A vendor whose product reports click rate has no reason to build tooling that argues against it.

**No professional standard exists to encode.** There is no body of practice for this role to point at, so a framework would have to be created rather than adopted.

**Career progression leads out.** People move on from the role, so the accumulated judgement about what works in their organisation leaves with them and the next person starts from the vendor's defaults.

## What to Build

**Report a metric that does not punish rigour.** Difficulty-adjusted susceptibility and reporting rate as the headline, so a harder campaign does not look like a worse programme. This is the single change that most improves the manager's position, and it depends on the calibration work in [[niches/security-awareness-training/difficulty-calibration/profile|🎯 Difficulty Calibration]].

**Provide a design framework for the ethical calls.** Which lure categories are excluded and why, what consequences are appropriate, when escalation is warranted, what notification is required. A written standard the manager can apply and cite, rather than a judgement they make alone each time.

**Support the internal negotiation.** Template agreements with HR on consequences, with communications on tone, with works councils on consultation. The manager currently constructs each of these personally from nothing.

**Surface the trust signal.** Reporting rates, complaint volumes and survey measures presented alongside the click rate, so the manager can show the cost of a punitive design rather than only assert it.

**Make the case for harder campaigns.** A projection of what the number will do when difficulty rises, prepared in advance, so the manager can set the expectation before the result arrives rather than explaining it afterwards.

**Capture the programme's institutional memory.** What has been tried, what caused complaints, which departments need what notice, what was agreed with HR. Currently in one person's head, in a role with regular turnover.

**Build the professional community.** A practice standard and a peer network would give this role something to point at, which is what people in it most consistently lack.

## Target Customer

Security leadership, who employ these people and lose them regularly, and for whom the argument is programme quality and retention.

Awareness managers themselves as the users, and the ones who would recognise every part of this immediately.

Professional bodies and industry groups, as the natural home for a practice standard, which is the element a vendor cannot credibly supply.

## Impact If Built

A metric that does not punish rigour removes the perverse incentive at the centre of the role, and it is the change that would most improve programme quality across the industry.

A written design framework would let the manager make the ethical calls against a standard rather than alone, which is both better decisions and a substantially better job.

And capturing the programme's institutional memory would stop each new manager starting from vendor defaults in an organisation whose specific history is entirely undocumented.
