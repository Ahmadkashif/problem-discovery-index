# Fix: The Warehouse Team Watches the Wire Fraud Module

**Niche:** Role-Based Risk Targeting
**Industry:** [[industries/security-awareness-training|Security Awareness Training]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Most of the content most people receive does not apply to their job, which is why the annual module is experienced as something to click through.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #worker-facing #descriptive-statistics #workflow-orchestration #compliance
**Contested on:** Whether the programme reflects what a person is actually exposed to and what they already know.

## The Problem

A warehouse worker sits through a module on business email compromise and wire transfer fraud. They do not have an email account that receives external mail, cannot authorise a payment, and have never been asked to change supplier bank details.

A developer with production database access sits through a module on tailgating and clean desk policy, and receives nothing about credential handling in their actual workflow.

An executive assistant, who manages the calendar and mailbox of someone named on the company website and is a well-documented target, receives the same generic phishing module as everyone else.

The mismatch is the main reason the programme is regarded as a ritual. An employee who spends most of an hour on content that visibly does not apply to their work concludes, correctly, that the exercise is about compliance rather than about them. That conclusion then applies to the parts that were relevant.

And the people who genuinely need depth do not get it, because depth for a few is not how a uniform programme works.

## Why It's Still Broken

**Uniform is easy to administer and easy to evidence.** One assignment for everyone, one completion report, one auditor question answered.

**Differentiation invites the fairness question.** Giving some people less training requires justifying why, which is a conversation nobody wants with HR or a works council.

**The vendor sells a library.** Content breadth is the product, and the natural way to use a broad library is to assign a lot of it.

**Department grouping looks like targeting.** Organisations that group by department believe they have addressed this, and a department contains wildly different exposure.

**Nobody measures relevance.** No programme reports how much of the assigned content applied to the recipient, so the waste is invisible in every report.

**Employees do not complain effectively.** The annual module is resented and tolerated, and the complaint is about length rather than about relevance, which is treated as an unavoidable cost.

## What a Fix Looks Like

**Ask people what applies to them.** A three-question survey — do you receive external email, can you authorise payments, do you handle customer data — assigns far better than a department code and takes a minute.

**Cut modules that do not apply.** A person without payment authority should not receive the payment fraud module. This is the fix, it shortens the programme substantially for most people, and it requires only the willingness to assign differently.

**Deepen for the few.** The small group with real exposure — payment authority, privileged access, public visibility — should receive something substantially better than the standard module, and there are few enough of them to make it practical.

**Record the diagnostic as completion.** Where compliance requires universal training, a short diagnostic pass can satisfy it for people who demonstrably know the material, which resolves the conflict without breaching the requirement.

**Use the identity data.** Privileged access is computed continuously by the identity system and is the single clearest exposure signal available. Asking for it is an internal request, not a project.

**Report relevance.** Track and report the proportion of assigned content that applied to the recipient. Making the waste visible is what would justify changing the assignment.

**Let people say it was irrelevant.** One question at the end of a module. It costs nothing and would tell the programme owner what the workforce already knows.

## Who Feels the Pain

The employee spending most of an hour on content about a job that is not theirs, learning that the programme is a formality.

The high-exposure individual — the payment authoriser, the executive assistant, the privileged administrator — who is a genuine target and receives generic content.

The awareness manager, who knows the mismatch is the main source of the programme's poor reputation and cannot address it without data they do not have.

And the organisation, whose actual risk concentrates in a small number of roles that the programme treats identically to everyone else.

## Impact If Fixed

A three-question survey assigns better than a department code, takes a minute per person, and is available to any programme immediately.

Cutting content that does not apply would shorten the annual burden substantially for most of the workforce, which is the change most likely to improve how the programme is regarded — and its reputation is what determines whether people report things.

And concentrating real depth on the small number of genuinely exposed roles addresses the organisation's actual risk, which is skewed in a way a uniform programme is structurally unable to reflect.
