# Training Content & Delivery

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/security-awareness-training|Security Awareness Training]]
**Category:** High Market Share
**Contested on:** Whether the training changes what people do, or produces a completion record.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$750M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~25%
**Digital Adoption:** High — modules, assignment and tracking are solved
**Target Buyer:** Awareness managers, compliance, HR
**Automation Potential:** High for delivery, low for whether it works

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

The content half of the category: modules on phishing, passwords, data handling, physical security and social engineering, assigned annually and after a simulation failure, completed, and recorded.

The delivery infrastructure is good. Assignment, reminders, completion tracking, multi-language support and compliance reporting all work.

The contest is whether any of it changes behaviour. The evidence base for security awareness content is thin, the measurement is completion, and completion is a measure of compliance rather than of learning. Modules are generally the same for everyone regardless of role, exposure or existing knowledge — which the source of this industry names directly — and they are delivered annually, which is a schedule set by compliance requirements rather than by anything known about retention.

The vendors compete on production quality, library breadth, engagement features and localisation. None competes on demonstrated behaviour change, because none can demonstrate it and no customer asks.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Module libraries with substantial production values, micro-learning formats, gamification, assignment engines, reminder workflow, completion tracking, quiz scoring, multi-language delivery and compliance reporting. Some adaptive assignment based on simulation results.

The gaps are about effect and fit. Completion is the metric, and it measures attendance. Quiz scores measure recall immediately after viewing, which is the weakest possible evidence of durable behaviour change. Content is uniform regardless of role, so a finance team handling payment instructions receives the same module as a warehouse team. Annual cadence is set by compliance and bears no relationship to what is known about spacing and retention. Nothing measures whether a module changed anything in the workplace. And the content's own evidence base — whether these messages produce behaviour change at all — is largely unexamined.
