# Build: Measured Harm and a Governed Design

**Niche:** Simulation Harm & Consent
**Industry:** [[industries/security-awareness-training|Security Awareness Training]]
**Type:** Build (Greenfield Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Measure what simulations cost the workforce — distress, complaints, trust in the security function — and characterise which designs deliver resistance without it.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #bayesian-inference #compliance #worker-facing #descriptive-statistics
**Contested on:** What deliberately deceiving employees costs them, and whether the resistance the programme produces can be obtained without it.

## The Problem

The category has an effect it does not measure. Simulations cause distress, sometimes considerable, and they affect how a workforce regards the security function. Neither is tracked by any vendor or most customers.

The pattern of the documented controversies is consistent. A simulation imitates a bonus announcement, a redundancy notice, a benefits change or a similar subject that touches money, employment or health. Employees respond with real emotion. The story becomes public. The organisation apologises. The lesson is drawn narrowly — do not use that particular lure — and the underlying design question is not addressed.

The underlying question is a trade. Lures that exploit genuine anxiety produce higher click rates, which makes them better at discriminating between people who would and would not fall for a real attack. Excluding them costs some measurement power. How much is unknown, because nobody has measured either side.

Meanwhile the trust effect is the more consequential one and is completely unmeasured. A security function that deceives colleagues and then punishes those who are deceived has spent something, and what it has spent matters because the behaviour it most needs — people reporting things that look wrong — depends entirely on employees being willing to come forward.

## Why Nobody Has Built This

**Measuring harm produces a number nobody wants.** A vendor reporting distress rates from its own campaigns has generated a metric no competitor reports and that customers may react to.

**The harmful lures are the effective ones.** Addressing harm may reduce the headline click rate's discriminating power, which is the one number the category reports.

**Nobody owns the trust question.** Security owns the programme, HR owns the workforce relationship, and the effect of the first on the second belongs to neither.

**Controversies are treated as content failures.** Each incident is resolved by retiring a template rather than by examining the design principle, so the pattern recurs.

**Employees cannot meaningfully object.** The affected party is in an employment relationship and cannot decline, which removes the feedback that would otherwise constrain the practice.

**No ethical review exists.** The same intervention in a research setting would require independent review, and in a workplace it requires nobody's approval but the programme owner's.

## What to Build

**Measure the harm directly.** Complaint rates, distress reports, help desk contacts and support escalations following each campaign, tracked per template and per lure category. This costs almost nothing and would produce the category's first harm data.

**Survey trust in the security function, longitudinally.** Willingness to report, comfort in approaching security, perceived fairness of the programme. Occupational psychology has validated instruments for adjacent constructs and the measurement is ordinary.

**Characterise the trade.** For each lure category, the discrimination it provides against the harm it causes. This is the analysis that would let the category make an informed design choice rather than reacting to the last controversy.

**Publish default exclusions.** Compensation, employment status, health, bereavement, family and similar categories excluded by default, with deviation requiring documented approval. The controversies have a recognisable shape and this prevents almost all of them.

**Establish independent review.** A panel including HR and employee representation that approves campaign designs, separate from the security function running the programme. This is the governance element every comparable deceptive intervention has and this one does not.

**Design debriefing properly.** The post-click experience should explain rather than admonish, should make clear that the design was intended to be difficult, and should not feel like a punishment. Research ethics has a well-developed practice here and the current landing page is not it.

**Report harm alongside the click rate.** A programme that reports both is making a claim about being run responsibly, and it is the only way the trade becomes visible to the people authorising it.

## Target Customer

HR, works councils and employee representation bodies, who are increasingly the parties asking about these programmes and who currently have no data to ask with.

Security leadership at organisations that have had a controversy or want to avoid one, which is a growing population as the incidents accumulate publicly.

Vendors positioning on responsible design, for whom published harm measurement and default exclusions would be a genuine differentiator in a category where every competitor reports only the click rate.

## Impact If Built

The category acquires harm data, which it has never had, on an intervention whose harms are documented and recurring.

Publishing default exclusions would prevent almost all of the controversies that have occurred, because they follow a recognisable pattern and the exclusions are known.

And measuring the trust effect would address the more consequential harm — a security function that has spent its workforce's willingness to come forward has damaged the thing the programme was supposed to build.
