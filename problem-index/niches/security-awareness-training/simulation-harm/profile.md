# Simulation Harm & Consent

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/security-awareness-training|Security Awareness Training]]
**Category:** Contested Sub-Niche
**Contested on:** What deliberately deceiving employees costs them, and whether the resistance the programme produces can be obtained without it.

## Profile

**Market Size:** ~$360M
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~12%
**Digital Adoption:** Very low — harm is not measured by anyone
**Target Buyer:** HR, works councils, employee representation, security leadership
**Automation Potential:** Low — this is a design and governance problem

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche

Phishing simulation is deliberate deception of employees, at scale, without meaningful ability to decline. That is a defensible intervention and it is not a costless one.

The documented harms follow a recognisable pattern. Simulations imitating bonus announcements, redundancy notices, benefit changes and similar subjects have repeatedly caused real distress and public controversy, because the lures that produce the strongest responses are the ones that exploit genuine anxiety. And punitive use of results — lists, manager notification, disciplinary consequences — is common enough to shape how a workforce regards the security function, which matters because the behaviour that actually helps an organisation is reporting a suspicious message, and a programme that punishes clicking teaches people to stay quiet.

This is a different problem from its sibling in every respect. [[niches/security-awareness-training/difficulty-calibration/profile|🎯 Difficulty Calibration]] is a psychometrics problem answerable from data the vendor holds, which makes the product more defensible. Harm is a research-ethics and occupational-psychology problem with a different owner inside the customer, a different evidence base, and no measurement that would make anything look better — the most effective lures are exactly the harmful ones, so addressing it may cost discrimination.

## Current Tools & Gaps

Template libraries that in some cases exclude particular subjects by default. Vendor guidance discouraging certain lure types. Customer-side approval of campaign content, of varying rigour. Post-click landing pages that explain the simulation, which is the nearest thing to debriefing. Works council consultation in jurisdictions where it is required.

The gaps are that nothing is measured and nothing is governed. No vendor reports distress, complaint rates or trust effects from its own campaigns. There is no ethical review independent of the party running the programme, which is standard for any comparable deceptive intervention. Consent and notification practice varies arbitrarily. Debriefing is a landing page and a training assignment, which can read as punitive. And the trade between lure effectiveness and harm has never been characterised, so nobody knows how much discrimination is actually lost by excluding the damaging categories.
