# Small Independent Agencies

**Parent Industry:** [[industries/insurtech-platforms|Insurtech Platforms]]
**Category:** Underserved Audience
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor selling to small agencies is fighting to let a service representative shop a renewal to six markets without rebuilding the submission six times — and whoever makes application data reusable across carriers takes the agency.

## Profile
**Market Size:** ~$890M US technology spend across independent agencies with fewer than ten staff
**Share of Parent Industry:** ~6% of insurtech revenue, against the majority of independent agencies by count
**Digital Adoption:** Low-Medium — agency management systems are near-universal and used shallowly
**Target Buyer:** Agency owners, who are usually also the principal producer
**Automation Potential:** Very High — remarketing, data re-entry and service work are all transcription-heavy

## What Makes This a Distinct Niche
Most independent insurance agencies are small businesses of a handful of people, and the constraint on all of them is the same: service work consumes the capacity that would otherwise go into selling. The clearest expression is remarketing. A commercial renewal that needs shopping means taking the account's information — which is in the agency management system, and in last year's application, and in the expiring policy — and entering it into six carriers' submission processes, each with its own portal, its own required fields and its own supplementals. A service representative does this by hand, six times, for one account. The data was structured, the carriers all want substantially the same facts, and the interchange standard that was designed for exactly this exists and is honoured inconsistently. The result is that small agencies remarket less than they should, which costs their clients money and costs the agency the commission a better placement would have earned.

## Current Tools & Gaps
Applied Epic and Vertafore AMS360 are the systems of record and hold the account data. Comparative raters serve personal lines well, where the data model is simple and the carriers participate. Commercial lines raters exist and cover a fraction of the market. ACORD forms are the interchange standard and carriers accept them with varying completeness, frequently requiring their own portal in addition. The gaps: no reliable path from the agency system's account data into multiple carriers' submission processes, so the data is re-entered; supplemental applications, which differ per carrier and per class, are completed from scratch each time; and the agency's own book — which carriers write which classes well, where its placements actually bind, what its retention looks like by carrier and by producer — is not analysed at all.
