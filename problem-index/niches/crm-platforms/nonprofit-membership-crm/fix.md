# Cost Per Dollar Raised, By Channel, Actually Computed

**Niche:** [[niches/crm-platforms/nonprofit-membership-crm/profile|Nonprofit & Membership CRM]]
**Industry:** [[industries/crm-platforms|CRM Platforms]]
**Type:** Fix (Pain Point)
**One-liner:** Every board asks what it costs to raise a dollar, every development office answers with an annual aggregate, and nobody computes it by channel — which is the only version of the number that could change a decision.
**Tags:** #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #revenue-impact #compliance #quick-win #automation
**Contested on:** Every serious competitor in donor and member software is fighting to attribute a gift or a renewal to what actually caused it — and whoever answers that credibly takes the development office.

## The Problem
The board asks about fundraising efficiency. The answer is a ratio computed from the audited financials: total fundraising expense over total contributions. It is a real number and it is useless for any decision, because it averages a direct mail programme that may break even, an events programme whose net is frequently far smaller than it appears, a major gifts function whose cost is mostly salary and whose return arrives years later, and a grants function that is a different activity entirely. The office knows the aggregate and cannot say which of its four programmes is subsidising the others.

## Why It's Still Broken
Fundraising expense is tracked for financial reporting, which requires an aggregate, and nobody allocates staff time across programmes because time tracking in a small nonprofit is both burdensome and culturally unwelcome. Events are the worst case: the gross is celebrated, the direct costs are known, and the staff and volunteer time consumed is never counted, which is why events persist in portfolios where the arithmetic would not support them. And computing the number honestly risks showing that a beloved programme loses money, which is a conversation nobody initiates.

## What a Fix Looks Like
Allocate cost by programme with light-touch measurement and report the ratio per channel with its lag. Staff time by programme from a monthly estimate rather than a timesheet — a five-minute allocation exercise is accurate enough for this purpose and is acceptable where a timesheet is not. Direct costs already exist in the accounts and need coding by programme rather than by expense type. Events include staff and volunteer hours, which is the correction that changes the picture most. Major gifts are reported with the lag acknowledged — cost this year against gifts that will arrive over several — rather than forced into an annual ratio that makes cultivation look expensive. Report each programme's cost per dollar raised with its uncertainty and its trend, alongside the aggregate the board is used to. And include the retention effect, since a channel that acquires donors who lapse after one gift is worse than its first-year ratio suggests and a channel that acquires durable donors is better.

## Who Feels the Pain
Development directors defending a portfolio they cannot decompose; boards making budget decisions from an average; and the organisations that continue running events at a net loss because nobody counted the hours.

## Impact If Fixed
Per-channel cost with the lifetime effect included is the number that actually governs fundraising portfolio decisions and is absent from almost every organisation in the sector. The event finding is the one most likely to be uncomfortable and most likely to matter, and the arithmetic needed is an allocation exercise rather than a system.
