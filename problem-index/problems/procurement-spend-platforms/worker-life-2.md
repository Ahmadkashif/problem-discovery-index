# Category Manager Sourcing Event Assembly

**Industry:** [[procurement-spend-platforms|Procurement & Spend Platforms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Category managers stop spending most of a sourcing event assembling specifications and normalising bid responses in spreadsheets, and get the time back for the negotiation itself.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #bert #transformers #word-embeddings #k-nearest-neighbors #evaluation-metrics #automation #worker-facing

## The Problem
Running a competitive sourcing event is the core of category management. It looks like a structured process and is mostly assembly.

Define the scope, which means reconstructing what the company actually buys in this category from a spend cube that classifies at supplier level. Write the specification, usually by adapting a document from a previous event and asking stakeholders what has changed. Build the bid sheet. Identify suppliers, largely from memory and the incumbent's competitors. Issue the event. Answer supplier clarification questions, most of which reveal an ambiguity in the specification. Receive responses in whatever structure each supplier chose to use, despite the template. Normalise them into a comparable form, which is the largest single task and is done in a spreadsheet. Build the award scenario analysis. Present.

The negotiation — the part requiring commercial skill and the part that determines the outcome — happens at the end, with whatever time and energy remains.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Category managers are the most commercially skilled people in the procurement function and are typically running several events concurrently against annual savings targets. The assembly work consumes the capacity that would otherwise go into market knowledge, supplier development and the negotiation itself.

Bid normalisation is the particular grievance. Suppliers respond in their own formats with their own assumptions about scope, units and inclusions, and making the responses genuinely comparable requires reading each one carefully and rebuilding it. It is done under deadline, in a spreadsheet, and an error in it selects the wrong supplier for a multi-year agreement.

The events also repeat. The same category is sourced every few years, by a different person, from scratch, because nothing retained the specification, the supplier list, the questions that were asked or what the market said last time.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Scope reconstructed from properly classified line-item spend rather than from a supplier-level cube, so the event covers what the company actually buys.

Specifications drafted from the prior event and from comparable events, with the changes stakeholders describe applied — and with the clarification questions suppliers asked last time surfaced as ambiguities to fix before issuing.

Supplier identification from the platform's cross-customer view: who else serves this category at this scale in this region, rather than who the incumbent's salesperson mentioned.

Bid normalisation automated. Responses arrive as documents and spreadsheets in inconsistent structures, and extracting them into a comparable frame — with assumptions and exclusions flagged rather than silently absorbed — is the single largest time saving available and is a well-shaped extraction problem.

Award scenario modelling with the constraints stated once: volume commitments, supplier count, transition cost, risk concentration.

## Impact If Solved
Sourcing events are where procurement creates value and most of the effort inside them is assembly and normalisation performed under deadline. Returning that time to negotiation and market work is the difference between a function that administers a process and one that improves a company's cost position.
