# Hotel Tax and Assessment Compliance Reporting

**Industry:** [[visitors-bureaus|Convention & Visitors Bureaus]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Every bureau has to show its city, its hotel board and sometimes the state that each hotel-tax and district-assessment dollar went to a lawful tourism purpose, and it builds that record by hand from an accounting system that knows nothing about the rules.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #descriptive-statistics #compliance #automation #workflow-orchestration #data-integration #quick-win

## The Problem
Most of a bureau's money arrives under conditions. The city's services contract passes on a share of hotel occupancy tax and says what it may be spent on. A tourism improvement district, where one exists (211 across 23 states in a 2024 count), passes on an assessment that hoteliers voted for, under a management district plan that fixes spending categories and percentages. Some states write the test into law. In Texas, every municipal hotel-tax expenditure must both directly enhance and promote tourism and the hotel and convention industry and fit one of the categories listed in the Tax Code. Bureaus that re-grant hotel tax to festivals, sports events and arts groups also have to collect evidence that those events drew overnight visitors.

So once a quarter or once a year the finance lead assembles a report: spending by category against the contract or plan, the share that went to administration, the grants made and the room nights each grantee claimed. The work is pulling general-ledger exports into a spreadsheet, re-coding transactions into the funder's categories, chasing grantees for their post-event reports and writing the narrative. In a city audit or a district renewal, the same records are pulled again under more pressure.

## What Already Exists
Fund accounting and nonprofit finance systems (Sage Intacct, Blackbaud Financial Edge NXT, QuickBooks with classes) track restricted funds and produce grant reports. Grant management platforms such as Submittable and SmartSimple handle applications and post-award reporting. Granicus, which now owns Simpleview, also sells municipal compliance and short-term-rental tax tools to the cities on the other side of the contract. Audit firms review the books annually.

## The Customisation Gap
None of these tools knows the rules a bureau is judged by. The adaptation needs: the funder's eligibility categories encoded as a rule set, per city contract, district plan and state statute, so each transaction is tagged at entry rather than re-coded at year end; a check against administrative-spend caps and plan percentages as the year runs; grant applications screened against the statutory test, with the room-night evidence each grantee must return defined up front; a link from each grant to the room nights reported and, where possible, to hotel data; and an audit-ready report generated in the funder's own format. Language models help with the messy part, reading invoices and grantee reports and proposing the category and the reason.

## Impact If Solved
A mid-sized bureau's annual compliance report drops from ~2-3 weeks of finance and executive time to ~2-3 days (estimate). More importantly, the bureau walks into a budget hearing or district renewal with an answer for every dollar instead of a reconstruction, at a time when 42% of destination organisations say their funding is at risk.
