# The Building Only the Chief Engineer Understands

**Industry:** [[facilities-management-firms|Facilities Management Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced building engineer reads a plant's sounds, trends and quirks and knows which equipment is about to fail and which calls are not what they seem, and none of that reaches the CMMS, so it leaves when the engineer retires, changes shift or is rebadged to the next provider.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #lstms-and-grus #autoencoders #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #feature-engineering #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A corporate campus, a hospital or a federal building under an IFM contract has a chief engineer and a handful of building engineers who have run its plant for years. They walk the mechanical rooms every morning. They hear a chiller start to surge before the BAS raises an alarm. They notice that an air handler's discharge temperature hunts on cold mornings, which means a valve actuator is going. A burning-belt smell in a penthouse tells them which fan to check first. They know that "too cold" calls from the fourth floor in March are a reheat valve, not the thermostat, and that a cooling tower basin heater has failed twice in the same week of January. They triage the service desk's work orders by reading them, because they know which tenants call about everything and which call only when something is wrong.

This is tacit knowledge in the craft sense. Experienced engineers act on patterns they cannot write a specification for, built from years of exposure to one building's equipment. The provider pays for it without seeing it. The CMMS holds a work-order history in which most reactive tickets close as "completed," "adjusted" or "reset," with no failure mode, no cause and no record of the early signs that prompted the engineer to act. The BAS holds years of trend data that nobody labels. The engineer's judgement connects the two and is written nowhere.

The loss is regular and expensive. Engineers retire, and replacing a chief engineer's years in one building takes years. At a rebid the new provider often rebadges the site team, keeping the people and losing everything else, or loses the people and inherits a building it does not understand. Federal base-operations contracts change hands at recompete on a five-year rhythm. Each time, the incoming team relearns the plant through failures, and the reactive work-order rate rises in exactly the months when the contract's transition KPIs are being watched.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's read has to be captured while the expert works, and the expert's work is walking, listening and fixing, not typing. A morning round in a mechanical room produces no record unless someone makes recording effortless. The signals the engineer uses are partly in the BAS (trend shapes, cycling, approach temperatures), partly in sound and vibration no BAS measures, and partly in the work-order text the engineer reads. Capturing them means pairing BAS trend history with the engineer's own short annotations ("starting to surge," "actuator hunting") at the moment of noticing, and adding cheap acoustic or vibration sensors on the few machines that matter most.

The labelling challenge is that the outcome label is noisy and the expert is inconsistent. A chiller the engineer flagged may be repaired before it fails, which removes the failure that would have proved the call right, so successful interventions erase their own labels. Failure codes in the CMMS are rarely filled in and, when they are, mean different things to different technicians. The same engineer calls a trend shape "normal for this unit" in October and "worrying" in July. Labels need repeated review on a sample to estimate how far the engineer agrees with themselves, and the outcome has to be defined as "intervention or failure within N days" rather than failure alone.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. An alert that arrives after the engineer has already noticed is noise, and one that is wrong twice is ignored for good. The system has to be faster than the expert, which means surfacing the pattern to the junior technician on the night shift or to the incoming team after a transition, the people who do not yet have the expert's read, rather than to the chief engineer who does. Most buildings' BAS data is also locked in the controls vendor's format and often owned by the client, which adds a data-access step before any model runs.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Building memory first and a model second. A technician-facing app lets engineers attach a few spoken words to a piece of equipment during their round, and it links each note to the BAS trends, alarms and work orders for that asset at that moment. Closed work orders get a one-tap failure mode and cause, suggested from the technician's words and the trend data, rather than a free-text "completed." Over months this builds a labelled history of what the engineers noticed, what they did and what happened next.

On top of that history, learn the engineers' reads. Sequence models over BAS trends find the shapes the engineers annotate as early warnings. Anomaly models trained on each unit's own normal operation catch departures no fixed alarm limit would. Survival models estimate time to intervention per asset given its recent behaviour. Classifiers over work-order text flag which incoming calls are likely to be something other than what they say. The output goes to whoever lacks the expert's knowledge: the night technician sees "this AHU's discharge hunting matches the pattern the day engineer flagged before the last two actuator failures," and the transition team inherits an annotated plant rather than an asset list.

## Impact If Solved
Reactive work orders and emergency call-outs on the equipment that matters most would fall (estimate: by a fifth to a third on instrumented central plant), and transitions would stop producing their predictable spike of failures in the first six months. The provider would keep its most valuable asset, the building knowledge that today it rents from a few individuals, through retirements, shift changes and rebids. That knowledge then becomes a reason to renew the account instead of something the client could hire away.
