# The Platform Certifies Compliance in Sixty Countries and Nobody Can Audit the Determination

**Industry:** [[remote-work-infrastructure|Remote Work Infrastructure]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A green tick says this engagement is compliant in this country, the determination behind it was a specialist reading an internal note of unknown vintage, and the customer carries the residual risk.

## The Problem
An employer-of-record platform lets a company employ someone in a country where it has no entity. The platform's local entity employs the worker, handles payroll, benefits, tax and statutory obligations, and invoices the client. The product presents this as solved.

Underneath sit determinations that are legal judgements. Is this engagement employment or contracting under this jurisdiction's test, applied to the facts of what the person actually does. Does the arrangement create a permanent establishment, exposing the client to corporate tax in that country — a question that turns on the worker's role, authority and activities, and where the platform's structure helps and does not eliminate the risk. Are the benefits, notice periods and termination terms compliant with local law, which changes.

Those determinations are made by compliance specialists reading internally-maintained country guidance. The guidance is updated by people watching legislative and case law developments across dozens of jurisdictions, at a cadence nobody publishes. A rule that changed eight months ago may or may not be reflected. The customer sees a status, not the rule, not its date, and not the reasoning.

The liability is carefully allocated. Platform terms generally position the service as facilitation with the client retaining residual exposure, and the guidance is framed as information rather than advice. So the party who can evaluate the determination does not carry the risk, and the party carrying the risk cannot evaluate it.

When it is wrong, the consequence is delayed and expensive. Misclassification and permanent establishment findings arrive years later from an authority, with back taxes, social contributions, penalties and interest, and by then the engagement may have ended and the facts may be hard to reconstruct.

## Why It's Unsolved
Compliance is jurisdiction-specific, fact-dependent and genuinely hard. Each country has its own classification test, its own permanent establishment thresholds, its own statutory entitlements, and its own case law developing continuously. Maintaining accurate coverage across sixty countries is a substantial specialist operation and no amount of engineering removes the need for legal judgement.

The liability structure discourages transparency. A platform that published its reasoning and its rule vintages would be making auditable claims about legal positions, which creates exposure it has been careful to avoid — the current framing as facilitation rather than advice depends on not making a professional determination.

Facts are also poorly captured. The classification test applies to what the worker actually does — the control exercised, the exclusivity, the integration into the client's organisation — and the platform generally knows only what the contract says. A contractor arrangement papered correctly and operated as employment fails the test in most jurisdictions, and the platform cannot see the operation.

And the customer's incentive is mixed. A client engaging a contractor because employment is expensive does not necessarily want a rigorous classification test applied to the facts, and platforms compete partly on how readily they accommodate that.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Make the determination auditable. Each status should carry the rule applied, its source, the date it was last verified, and the facts it was applied to. That converts a tick into evidence, and evidence is what a client needs if an authority asks three years later.

Monitor the rule base continuously rather than manually. Legislative, regulatory and case law changes across jurisdictions are published and trackable, and a system that flags which existing determinations a change affects — and which clients are exposed — is the difference between discovering a change through an audit and discovering it through a monitor.

Assess classification on the facts, not the contract. Structured capture of how the engagement actually operates — control, exclusivity, integration, tools, working hours — applied against each jurisdiction's test, produces a risk assessment rather than a paperwork check. It will sometimes return uncomfortable answers, which is the point.

Estimate permanent establishment risk as a continuous exposure rather than a binary. The factors are known and the thresholds are jurisdictional; a monitored risk score per client per country, updated as roles and headcount change, is more useful than a determination made once at onboarding and never revisited.

And learn from disputes. Platforms handle classification and tax challenges and the outcomes are the only empirical evidence about which determinations actually hold. Feeding them back into the rule base is the mechanism by which this stops being purely doctrinal.

## Impact If Solved
Companies are building distributed workforces on compliance assertions they cannot evaluate, carrying residual liability that surfaces years later. Auditable determinations with rule provenance and vintage give the client the evidence they will eventually need, continuous rule monitoring converts a manual update cadence into a tracked one, and fact-based classification assessment addresses the gap between what the contract says and what the engagement is — which is where the exposure actually lives.
