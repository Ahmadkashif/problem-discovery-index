# The Resident Who Is "Not Right" Before the Vitals Say So

**Industry:** [[skilled-nursing-facilities|Skilled Nursing Facilities]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Experienced CNAs and charge nurses notice a resident sliding toward a hospital transfer a day or two before anything measurable changes, and the facility neither records that read nor learns from it, so avoidable readmissions keep costing residents their health and facilities their SNF VBP money.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #large-language-models #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A CNA on day shift cares for perhaps eight to twelve residents (estimate). She bathes, dresses, toilets and feeds them, and after a few weeks she knows each one's baseline. She knows how Mrs. D. holds her fork, how far Mr. R. walks before he reaches for the rail, and what time the resident in room 14 starts asking for her daughter. When that changes, she often knows before anyone else. The resident eats half the tray, needs two prompts instead of one, and seems "off", with no fever, no low oxygen and no abnormal pressure yet. A good charge nurse hears that, looks, and calls the practitioner. A day later the same resident has a urinary tract infection with delirium, an aspiration pneumonia, or a heart failure exacerbation. If someone acted on the soft signal, it might be treated in the building. If not, it ends in a 2 a.m. ambulance ride. The hospital version of this judgement is the ward nurse's read in [[problems/general-hospitals/high-impact|The Nurse's Read of a Patient Going Downhill]] under [[general-hospitals|General Hospitals]]. The nursing-home read differs in who makes it and what it protects against: it is mostly made by aides rather than licensed nurses, against a baseline built over months rather than hours, and the outcome to prevent is a transfer out of the building rather than an escalation on the same ward.

That pattern recognition is tacit in exactly the craftsman's sense. Experienced aides and nurses say they "just know" when a resident is going downhill, and they cannot write the rule. The industry has tried to capture it on paper. INTERACT's "Stop and Watch" slip asks a CNA to tick boxes such as "seems different than usual" and hand it to a nurse. But it is used unevenly, it leaves no structured trace in the EHR, and nothing tells the aide afterwards whether her concern was right.

The cost is concrete and now priced. Hospital transfers are bad for frail residents, and they are measured. SNF VBP scores readmissions through SNFRM in FY 2026–27 and the new within-stay potentially preventable readmission measure from FY 2028, and adds a Long Stay Hospitalization measure from FY 2027 (CMS). The program withholds 2% of Medicare Part A payments and pays back only part of it, with net reductions estimated at $203.60M in FY 2027. MA plans and hospital preferred-provider networks choose SNF partners on readmission rates, and Five-Star counts hospitalisations and ED visits in its quality domain. A facility that catches decline earlier keeps residents out of hospital, keeps its stars and keeps its referrals.

## Why It's Unsolved
**Data collection** is the first wall. The expert's read has to be captured as it happens, by the person having it, in the busiest minutes of a short-staffed shift. CNA documentation today is mostly ADL flowsheets entered at a kiosk at the end of the shift: transfers, toileting, meals eaten, behaviours. It is recorded as checkboxes, often in bulk and from memory. The richest signal, such as "she's not herself today", is said aloud at the nurses' station and never written down. Nurse progress notes are free text, written late and inconsistently. Vitals are taken on a schedule, not when someone is worried. Any system that asks the CNA for more typing will be ignored.

**Labelling** is the second. The outcome, a hospital transfer within a few days, is observable, but it is a noisy label for whether the early read was right. Some transfers are unavoidable, some are driven by a family's or practitioner's preference, and some deteriorations are treated in place and leave no transfer at all. Experts also disagree with themselves. The same aide, tired and covering twenty residents on a night shift, will flag differently than when she has eight on days. Inter-rater agreement on a soft judgement like "seems different" is low and has to be measured, not assumed. Turnover adds a further problem. With nursing staff turnover near half a year (estimate), the experienced observer whose labels are best may have left before the model is trained.

**Deployment** is the third. An alert has to arrive faster than the experienced nurse's own sense, and it must not add to alarm fatigue in a building with no spare hands. It has to make sense to a CNA paid around $20 an hour (HCS, 2025) and to an LPN charge nurse covering two units. And it must lead to a defined action: a focused assessment, a practitioner call, or a lab draw in the building. A risk score that only fills a dashboard for the director of nursing will change nothing on the floor.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Capture first, then model. Give CNAs a one-tap or voice "something's different" signal on the point-of-care device they already use, tied to the resident and timestamped, with optional words ("ate less", "more confused", "won't stand"). Pull in the streams the facility already has: ADL flowsheet changes against the resident's own baseline, meal percentages, vitals, weights, new medications and antibiotics, lab results, nurse note text, and the MDS functional and cognitive scores. Learn each resident's normal and score departures from it. Then join every flag and every score to what happened over the next 72 hours to 7 days: in-house treatment, transfer, admission, death.

The model's job is not to replace the aide's read but to make it count. It raises the priority of a flag from a CNA whose past flags were usually right. It surfaces the resident whose soft signals are accumulating across shifts that no single person sees. It prompts the nurse with the facility's own change-of-condition protocol. Over time it tells each aide, privately, how often her concerns were borne out, which is the feedback the work has never had.

## Impact If Solved
An average freestanding SNF handles ~104 fee-for-service Medicare stays a year (1.5M stays across ~14,400 SNFs, MedPAC), or ~150–180 short-stay admissions once managed care is added, since managed care was 42% of admissions (CLA; estimate). CMS's FY 2026 SNFRM performance standards (inverted rates of 0.788 and 0.830) imply readmission rates of ~17–21%, so that facility sends ~25–40 short-stay residents back to hospital a year (estimate), before counting long-stay transfers. If a quarter of those are avoidable and half of those are caught early, ~3–5 hospitalisations a year are avoided per building (estimate). The money at stake is concrete. The 2% SNF VBP withhold on the ~$1.8M of fee-for-service Part A revenue an average freestanding SNF receives ($26B across ~14,400 SNFs, MedPAC; derived) is ~$36,000 a year, and the facility earns part of it back only by scoring well. Treat-in-place care improves residents' outcomes, protects readmission and hospitalisation scores in SNF VBP and Five-Star, and protects the MA and hospital-network referrals that follow those scores. It also gives the CNA's judgement, the most undervalued expertise in the building, a record, a track record and a reason to be heard.
