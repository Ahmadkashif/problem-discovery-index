# The Set-Up and Load-Out Crew

**Industry:** [[auctioneers|Auctioneers]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The crew who empty basements, carry furniture down stairs and load buyers' trucks do the heaviest, dirtiest work in the trade with no warning of what a house holds. What they get back is predictability and their backs.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #cnns #object-detection #gradient-boosting #optimization-fundamentals #workflow-orchestration #automation

## The Problem
Before anything can be photographed, someone has to get it out. Set-up crews carry the contents of a house into display rows on tables in the garage or the yard: dressers down narrow stairs, chest freezers out of basements, a workshop's worth of tools from an outbuilding. Some houses are hoarded floor to ceiling. Some have been empty for months, with mould, mice or broken plumbing. Often the utilities have already been cut off, so the crew works without lights, heat or water. After the sale the same crew runs pickup day, helping buyers load what they won, and then clears out whatever did not sell for donation or the dumpster.

The work arrives without warning. The crew is told "a three-bedroom ranch, Thursday", not that the basement is full or that the piano has to come up from it. Crews are small, often two to four people (estimate). They are paid hourly, ~$15–20 an hour (estimate), and are frequently part-time or seasonal, so when two houses overlap in the spring rush the same people work both.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is manual handling work with real injury risk: backs, knees, falls on stairs, cuts from hidden glass and tools. Hoarded and neglected houses add exposure to dust, mould and pests. Crews rarely have the moving company's equipment or training. They improvise with a hand truck and whoever is strongest. The unpredictability is its own stress: a day planned for a tidy house becomes twelve hours in a full one, and the crew stays late because the sale date was promised to the executor.

Pickup day ends the week with the public. Buyers arrive with vehicles too small for what they bought and expect the crew to load it, and the crew absorbs the frustration of anyone who is late, short-changed or unhappy.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A workload estimate before the crew is sent. A walkthrough video, analysed against the firm's past houses, flags heavy and awkward items (pianos, safes, freezers, gun cabinets), the volume in basements and outbuildings, and hazards such as hoarding or missing utilities. It estimates crew-hours and the equipment needed. Scheduling uses the estimate to staff each house properly, to book a mover or a hauling contractor for the heavy pieces, and to keep two big houses off the same days. On pickup day, timed slots and published size and loading rules ("bring help and a trailer for lots marked heavy") take the crew out of disputes.

## Impact If Solved
Crews sent with the right numbers and tools to houses they already know about would work fewer surprise overtime days in peak season. They would carry less without help and see fewer injuries, which matters in a trade that relies on the same few people every week. Handing heavy items to movers when the estimate calls for it could take the worst lifting off the crew in perhaps one house in five (estimate).
