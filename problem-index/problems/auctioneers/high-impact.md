# The Pre-Sale Estimate Nobody Scores Against the Hammer

**Industry:** [[auctioneers|Auctioneers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An auctioneer wins or loses every consignment on a spoken estimate of what the goods will bring, the hammer grades that estimate within weeks, and almost no firm records the two side by side, so the craft's core judgement is neither measured nor passed on.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #transformers #cnns #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A widow's son calls three auction firms about his mother's house. A retiring farmer invites two auctioneers to walk his machine shed. A bankruptcy trustee asks for a liquidation proposal on a closed restaurant. In each case the auctioneer walks the property, picks up pieces, looks at serial plates and maker's marks, and gives a number: the house contents should bring $40,000 to $60,000; the line of equipment around $1.2 million; the restaurant $85,000 after costs (figures illustrative). The number is half forecast and half sales pitch. A competitor who says $70,000 may win the consignment, and the son will judge whoever wins by the cheque that arrives after the sale.

That estimate drives every downstream decision. It decides whether the auctioneer offers a guarantee or an advance, whether the sale runs absolute or with reserves on the better pieces, how much is spent on advertising and on which platform, whether the Tiffany lamp goes into the local sale or is sent to a specialist house, and what reserve the farmer is told to accept on the combine. At lot level, experienced auctioneers know things they cannot write down: that brown furniture is soft in this county but mid-century pieces draw Chicago dealers; that a particular tractor model with a cab brings a premium in March and not in July; that a box lot of costume jewellery hides one signed piece worth more than the rest of the box; that this firearm collection will bring more if the military rifles are sold first. Order buyers and dealers have the same feel from the other side of the ring.

The hammer grades all of it, quickly and objectively. Within ~2–6 weeks (estimate) every lot has sold for a price or failed to sell, and the clerking system or the bidding platform holds the result. The estimate, meanwhile, was spoken at the kitchen table, written as a single total on the contract, or never written at all. Firms therefore cannot say whether their estimates are biased, in which categories they are reliable, which of their people estimate well, or whether a lost consignment was lost to a competitor who later delivered less. When the founding auctioneer retires, the pattern recognition that made the firm's estimates trusted leaves with them.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's judgement has to be captured at the moment it is made, by the expert, during a walkthrough that is also a sales meeting. Auctioneers estimate in aggregate ("the house will bring fifty"), not lot by lot, and the lots are not yet defined when the estimate is given: lotting happens days later, when the cataloguer decides that twelve Depression glass pieces become one lot or three. A usable record needs the estimate attached to the eventual lot, with photographs, before the sale. Nobody will type that on a phone in a farmer's yard.

The labelling problem is that the hammer is a noisy label for the quality of the estimate. A lot that sells low may have been estimated well and then hit a weather-cancelled preview, a bad closing night or a missing photo; a lot that passes unsold produces no price at all, and unsold lots are exactly the ones where the estimate was most wrong. The same auctioneer gives different numbers for the same item depending on the season and on how badly they want the consignment, so experts disagree with themselves. And estimates are selected: the auctioneer only learns the hammer for consignments they won, never for the ones lost to a competitor's higher number.

The deployment problem is speed and authority. An estimate aid has to work in the walkthrough, from a photo or a short video, faster than the auctioneer's own recall, and it has to show its comparables, because the auctioneer will repeat the number to a seller who will hold them to it. It must also never present a number the auctioneer cannot defend in front of a licensing board if the seller complains.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An estimate ledger first, a model second. During the walkthrough the auctioneer narrates and films; the system drafts a provisional item list with low and high estimates the auctioneer adjusts by voice. When the cataloguer later lots the sale, each lot inherits the estimates of the items it contains, and the reserve and any guarantee are recorded against it. After the close, every lot is joined to its hammer, its unsold status, its photo count, its platform and its closing time.

On top of the ledger, learn the auctioneer's read. Train a model on the firm's own estimates, condition notes and hammers, plus public price-realised archives, to produce a calibrated price range and a probability of selling for each lot, with the comparables that justify it. Report each estimator's bias by category and season, flag lots whose estimate sits far outside the model's range before the reserve is set, and route pieces the local market will undersell to the right venue.

## Impact If Solved
A firm that knows its estimates run high on glassware and low on shop tools, and by how much, can quote with confidence, set reserves that clear, and win consignments without overpromising. Across a sale of ~600 lots (illustrative), avoiding a handful of misplaced reserves and a few undersold specialist pieces is worth thousands of dollars (estimate) to the consignor and a referral to the firm. The founder's judgement, recorded and graded, becomes something the next generation inherits.
