# Alone on the Memory Care Night Shift

**Industry:** [[assisted-living-communities|Assisted Living Communities]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The overnight caregiver in memory care gets back the ability to be where she is needed instead of walking blind rounds, and the evidence to show what really happened when a resident falls.
**Tags:** #cnns #object-detection #change-point-detection #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation

## The Problem
From 10 p.m. to 6 a.m., a memory care neighbourhood of 20–30 residents may have one or two caregivers on the floor (estimate). Daytime staffing runs at about one caregiver per 5–8 residents (Ziegler, 2026). Many states do not require a nurse in the building overnight in assisted living, so the nurse is a phone call away (recalled). The caregiver does scheduled checks, every two hours or more often for residents flagged as high risk. She changes briefs, settles residents who wake confused, and answers call lights that residents with dementia often cannot use. Meanwhile a resident at the other end of the hall gets up without her walker or tries the exit door. Most falls in memory care happen unwitnessed. She finds the resident on the floor, does not know how long she has been there or whether she hit her head, and must call the nurse, the family and often 911. Then she writes the incident report at 4 a.m.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
She is paid about $17 an hour, the mean wage for the industry's aides (BLS OEWS, May 2023). She carries the fear that the fall or the open door on her shift will cost her the job, or worse, a resident's life. The Washington Post counted more than 2,000 senior living residents who wandered away since 2018, 98 of whom died. Blind rounds wake residents who were sleeping and miss the ones who are not. The work is lonely, physical and never finished, which is why caregivers leave: turnover ran at 39–44% for nursing assistants in Ziegler's last two surveys.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Room sensors or privacy-preserving video detect a resident sitting up, leaving the bed, entering the bathroom alone, lying on the floor or approaching an exit, and route a short alert to the caregiver's phone, ranked by urgency. Checks become targeted: she goes to the resident who is up, not to the one who is asleep. A fall comes with a short clip or timeline showing how it happened and how long the resident was down. That speeds the nurse's call and protects the caregiver from assumptions. Rounds and responses are documented automatically, so the 4 a.m. paperwork shrinks to a confirmation. Vendors already sell parts of this (SafelyYou, Inspiren, Teton.ai); the gap is alerts tuned to one person covering two halls, not to a nurse's station.

## Impact If Solved
If targeted alerts replace a third of blind checks and cut time-on-floor after falls (estimate), the caregiver reclaims ~1–2 hours a shift for direct care and documentation (estimate). Unwitnessed falls become witnessed events with a record. The vendor study in 11 memory care communities reported 75% fewer EMT visits and 80% fewer ED visits after falls (SafelyYou, 2018 data). The result is a night shift that is survivable, and a caregiver who stays.
