# The Wellness Nurse's Read of How Much Care a New Resident Will Really Need

**Industry:** [[assisted-living-communities|Assisted Living Communities]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced wellness nurse can tell at the move-in assessment how much care a new resident will really need and whether the community can keep them safe, and because that read is never captured, communities underbill care, admit residents they cannot serve, and lose them months later to a fall, a move to memory care or a move-out.

## The Problem
Before anyone moves in, the community's wellness director, usually an RN or LPN, does a pre-admission assessment. She visits the prospective resident at home, in the hospital or in rehab, reads the physician's report the state requires, and listens to the family. Then she fills in the community's assessment tool, a points checklist covering bathing, dressing, toileting, transfers, medications, cognition and behaviours. The score sets the level-of-care fee added to base rent and tells the community whether it can legally and safely admit the resident at all.

The checklist is not where the judgement happens. Families arriving after a crisis want a yes, and they under-report: the night-time wandering, the incontinence, the falls nobody saw. The sales counselor wants the move-in to fill a vacancy. An experienced wellness nurse reads past both. She notices the mother who lets her daughter answer every question, a sign of masked cognitive loss. She notices the grip on the chair arms before standing, the two medication lists that disagree, the refrigerator full of expired food. Then she raises the care level, recommends memory care instead, or quietly says the community is not the right fit. A newer nurse scores what the family tells her. Experienced wellness directors describe this as something they "just know" after hundreds of assessments, and they cannot write the rule down.

The cost shows up in two ways, months apart. A resident assessed too low receives more care than she pays for until the next scheduled reassessment, often 30 days after move-in and then quarterly or after a change of condition (operator practice, estimate). Aides absorb the unbilled minutes and other residents wait longer for help. A resident who should not have been admitted to assisted living falls, wanders or becomes aggressive. Within months she moves to memory care, a nursing home or the hospital, and the community refills the unit, often paying a referral agency again. The judgement is a cousin of the nursing-home aide's read of decline in [[problems/skilled-nursing-facilities/high-impact|The Resident Who Is "Not Right" Before the Vitals Say So]] and the ward nurse's in [[problems/general-hospitals/high-impact|The Nurse's Read of a Patient Going Downhill]]. It differs in what it predicts: not an acute change over days, but how much care and supervision a person will need over the next six months, priced before move-in.

## Why It's Unsolved
**Data collection** is the first wall. The nurse's real read is never written down. The assessment tool records a score per item, not the cues behind it: what the family avoided saying, the medication bottles on the counter, how the resident walked to the door. The care actually delivered after move-in is barely measured either. Caregivers tick tasks on a tablet or a paper sheet at the end of the shift, so the minutes spent on a resident who needs two prompts and a re-dress are invisible. Capturing the expert means recording the assessment itself, with voice notes, a structured "what I noticed" step and the hospital discharge papers, without stretching a 60–90 minute visit (estimate).

**Labelling** is the second. The quantity the nurse is predicting, true care need, has no clean label. Billed care level is the very thing being corrected. Logged care minutes are noisy. Outcomes such as a fall with injury, a transfer to memory care, a move-out or a hospital stay within 180 days are observable, but they also reflect staffing, family choices and luck. Nurses disagree with themselves as well: the same director scores lower on a Friday afternoon with a vacancy to fill than on a quiet Monday. Inter-rater reliability on cognition and behaviour items has to be measured, not assumed. Wellness nurses also leave, with RN turnover at 40.8% in Ziegler's 2026 survey, so the best labellers may be gone before a model is trained.

**Deployment** is the third. Any support has to work during or right after the visit, on a tablet, before the family signs, and faster than the nurse's own conclusion. It cannot simply raise every quote, or the community loses the move-in to a competitor. It must also respect fair-housing and disability law and each state's admission and retention rules, so the output has to be a care and pricing recommendation with reasons, not an approve-or-deny score (recalled legal constraint).

## What a Solution Looks Like
Capture first, then model. Give the wellness nurse an assessment flow that records voice notes and a short "what I noticed" checklist alongside the state form, and pulls in the hospital discharge summary, medication list and physician report. After move-in, capture care time without extra typing: timestamped task completion on caregivers' devices, call-light response logs, and, where installed, room-presence data from sensors such as Inspiren's AUGi or SafelyYou's cameras. Join each assessment to what happened over the next 180 days: care minutes per day, level changes, falls, incidents, transfers to memory care or a nursing home, hospital stays and move-outs.

Train models that predict care minutes per day at 30, 90 and 180 days, and the time to the first fall with injury, transfer or move-out, from assessment items, nurse notes and documents. At the assessment the nurse sees the predicted range against the level she has chosen, with the cues that drove the gap. After move-in, the system watches for drift between billed level and delivered care and proposes a reassessment when the gap opens rather than when the calendar says. Over time it tells each nurse, privately, how her assessments compared with what followed, which is the feedback the work has never had.

## Impact If Solved
An 80-unit community at 89% occupancy takes ~30–40 move-ins a year (estimate, from a ~22-month average stay) and bills ~$5.3M (derived from the CareScout median of $6,200 a month). If a fifth of residents are under-billed by ~$300–500 a month until reassessment (estimate), the community leaves ~$60,000–100,000 a year of delivered care unbilled. Each early move-out of a resident the community should not have admitted costs about a month of vacancy (~$6,200) and often a referral fee of roughly a month's rent and care (APFM's stated basis). Avoiding two or three a year is worth ~$25,000–40,000 per community (estimate). Across the ~1M people in residential care, better matching also means fewer falls and wandering incidents in the wrong setting. It gives the nurse's judgement, the most consequential decision a community makes about each resident, a record, a track record and a reason to be trusted over the sales pipeline.
