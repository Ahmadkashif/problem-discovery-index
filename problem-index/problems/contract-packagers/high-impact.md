# The Line Rate Nobody Can Defend

**Industry:** [[contract-packagers|Contract Packagers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every per-unit price a co-packer quotes rests on an experienced person's guess of how many units an hour a crew will make on a job that has never run, and that guess is neither written down with its reasons nor checked against the run that follows.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #cnns #feature-engineering #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
A brand's packaging buyer sends a request (an illustrative one): 40,000 club packs for a warehouse-club programme, four flavours banded into a tray, shrink-wrapped, cased six to a shipper, on pallets built to the retailer's pattern, shipping in eleven weeks. Attached are a spec sheet, a pallet drawing, and a box of samples. The co-packer's estimator, often the owner or the production manager in a small firm, takes the samples to the floor, tries to assemble a few packs and makes a judgement. How many people at which stations? Will the tray fold flat or need a jig? Will this film shrink cleanly over that shape, or wrinkle and need a second pass through the tunnel? How long will the crew take to reach rate? The answer becomes one number, units per labour-hour, and the number becomes the price.

The price is fixed per unit and often held for months. One confectionery co-packer publishes "fully loaded" prices of $0.75–1.25 per club pack and $30–50 per display build, with no separate charge for setup or changeover. At those prices a rate estimate that is 15% optimistic can wipe out the job's margin (estimate). The packager carries that risk on every quote. Brand owners outsource largely for short runs, new formats and surge capacity, so the run is usually a first run.

Experienced estimators are good at this, and they cannot say how. They know that a glossy carton slips in a hand-folding operation, that a three-up bundle of round containers runs slower than a two-up of rectangles, that a crew of returning temps reaches rate on the first afternoon while a new crew needs until the third day, and that a display with more than a certain number of facings will need a second QA check. This is tacit knowledge as the vault uses the term: pattern recognition from thousands of jobs, applied in minutes, never written down. When the estimator retires or leaves, the firm keeps the old quote spreadsheets and loses the judgement behind them.

The record that could teach it rarely exists in joined form. The quote lives in a spreadsheet or an estimating template. The run lives in labour sheets, line counts and a supervisor's shift notes. The component variance lives in a reconciliation file sent to the client weeks later. Nobody lines them up, so the firm cannot say which kinds of jobs it underquotes, by how much, or why.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem comes first. The estimator's judgement happens at a bench with a sample in hand, not in a system, and the features that drive it are physical: the carton's surface finish, the stiffness of a tray, how a product sits in a blister, how many hand motions a pack takes. To learn from it, a system has to capture what the estimator saw and decided at quote time, through photos, a short structured note and the assumed crew and station layout, and then the run's actual rate by hour, including the ramp-up. Most co-packers track labour by shift, not by job and hour, and many hand-pack lines have no automatic counts at all.

The labelling problem is that the run's actual rate is a noisy label. A missed rate can come from a bad estimate, a crew of first-day temps, a late component that split the run in three, a misprinted carton batch, or a supervisor pulled to another line. Experts also disagree with themselves: the same estimator, shown the same sample a month apart, will quote different rates depending on how full the plant is and how the last similar job went. An honest label separates the job's inherent rate from the crew and the disruptions around it, which needs the disruptions logged.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. Quotes are due in one to three days (estimate), and the estimator will not wait for a model or type twenty fields to feed one. Any assistance has to be faster than the estimator's own recall, starting from what they already have — a photo of the sample and the spec sheet — and it has to show the similar past jobs it is reasoning from, because an estimator will not sign a price they cannot explain to the owner.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start with a quote ledger rather than a model. Every quote is stored with its assumptions: rate, crew size, stations, expected ramp, changeover time, component scrap allowance and the estimator's own risk notes. Photos of the assembled sample and the spec sheet are attached. On the floor, job-level counts by hour (scan-based at pack-out, or a simple tally on a tablet) and a log of disruptions turn each run into a graded outcome: what rate the line reached, how long the ramp took, and which assumptions were wrong.

On top of the ledger, learn the estimator's read. A retrieval layer finds the most similar past jobs by format, component types, pack geometry and photo similarity, and shows what they actually ran at. A model trained on quote features, sample images and graded outcomes predicts a rate range rather than a point, so the estimator can see that a format has always come in 10–20% under the first guess. The tool does not set the price. It brings the firm's whole job history to the bench and makes the estimator's adjustments visible and teachable to a successor.

## Impact If Solved
A co-packer that knows its own rate error by job type can price the risky jobs higher, stop losing money quietly on the formats it consistently underquotes, and take on short runs and new formats with confidence. At industry receipts of ~$10.7B, a few points of margin recovered on mis-quoted jobs is worth hundreds of millions a year across the industry (estimate). The judgement of the one or two people who quote well also stays with the firm when they leave.
