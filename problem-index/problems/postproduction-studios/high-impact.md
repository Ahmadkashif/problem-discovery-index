# The Shot Bid Nobody Checks Against Its Actuals

**Industry:** [[postproduction-studios|Postproduction Studios]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A VFX supervisor prices hundreds of shots from a script breakdown and a few plates, and the studio records every hour each shot actually took without ever joining it back to the bid, so the judgement that decides the margin is neither graded nor kept.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #transformers #gradient-boosting #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
A streaming series sends a VFX package out to bid: a breakdown of ~300 to 1,500 shots (estimate), a script, a few sample plates, reference images and a delivery schedule. The vendor's VFX supervisor and VFX producer have days to return a price, shot by shot. Most vendors bid fixed prices per shot or per shot category, and the client awards on price and on the supervisor's reputation. Once awarded, revisions are absorbed unless the vendor can show that a note changed the scope.

The bid is a tacit judgement made at speed. An experienced supervisor looks at a plate and knows what the breakdown doesn't say. A handheld camera with no tracking markers means a hard matchmove. Fine hair against a bright window means a roto and edge problem that a key will not solve. Motion blur on a fast pan means the paint-out will need frame-by-frame work. A night exterior with rain will fight every comp. A set extension that looks routine will need a full 3D environment because the camera moves too far. They translate that read into artist-days by department (matchmove, roto, paint, lighting, comp), add a margin for the client's notes, and adjust for how this director and this showrunner behave. Junior producers bid by shot category and get burned. Senior supervisors bid by sight and are right more often, but nobody can say how much more often or on which shot types.

The outcome is recorded in detail. Every artist logs time against a shot in Autodesk Flow Production Tracking or ftrack, every version and every client note is kept, and the final hours per shot are known within weeks of delivery. The comparison is almost never made. Bids live in Excel templates, actuals live in the tracker, and the two use different shot names after the edit changes. When a show loses money, the post-mortem is anecdotal: "the creature shots ran over." The supervisor's read leaves when they do, and the margin structure of the business, with fixed bids, competitive underbidding and revisions absorbed, means a few systematic errors decide whether a vendor survives. Rhythm & Hues failed in 2013 after an Oscar-winning film. Technicolor's creative studios, including MPC, failed in 2025.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's read has to be captured at the moment of bidding, by the expert, during the most compressed week of the sales cycle. The supervisor's reasoning ("hair against a window, double the roto") is spoken in a bid meeting and reduced to a number in a cell. The plate they looked at may not even be the final plate; the edit changes after award, shots split and merge, and the shot that was bid is not always the shot that was delivered. Recovering a usable record means matching bid lines to tracker shots across renames and re-cuts, and capturing the visual features the supervisor reacted to, not just the category label.

The labelling challenge is that actual hours are a noisy label for the difficulty of a shot. Hours expand with the client's indecision, with artist seniority, with render farm outages and with whether the shot was done first (learning) or last (templated). Supervisors disagree with each other on the same plate, and with themselves: the same shot is bid differently in a slow month, when the vendor needs the work, than in a busy one. An honest target separates intrinsic shot difficulty from note-driven rework and from vendor-side inefficiency, and that requires the note history, not just the hours.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. A bid is due in days, often hours for a change order. A model that needs a clean breakdown and final plates is useless. It has to give an estimate from the plates and breakdown the supervisor already has, faster than the supervisor can form a view, and it has to show why ("motion blur and hair against bright background; similar shots took 2.1x category average"). Supervisors are hired for their eye and will reject a black box that overrules it; producers will distrust anything that hasn't been back-tested on their own shows.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A bid ledger first and a model second. Every bid line is linked to the plate, breakdown text and shot category it was priced from, and every delivered shot is linked back to its bid line through the edit's change history. A dataset of bid versus actual hours by department, with the note history attached, then exists for the first time.

On top of the ledger, learn the supervisor's read. Vision models extract the plate features that drive effort: camera motion, motion blur, hair and fine-edge content, occlusion, lighting complexity and the extent of frame to be replaced. Language models parse the breakdown and script text. A gradient-boosted or quantile model predicts artist-days by department with an uncertainty band, calibrated on the vendor's own history. The supervisor sees the model's estimate next to their own, with the nearest past shots and what they actually took. The disagreements are the useful output: they show where the expert sees something the data doesn't and where the expert systematically underbids.

## Impact If Solved
A vendor that knows which shot types it underbids can price them correctly or decline them, and on a 1,000-shot episodic package a 10–20% improvement in bid accuracy on the costliest shot categories is the difference between break-even and a ~10% margin (estimate). Keeping a departing supervisor's pricing judgement as a back-tested model turns the industry's most expensive recurring loss, the bid eye walking out of the door, into something the studio keeps.
