# The Burn Test Only the Chandler Can Read

**Industry:** [[consumer-products-makers|Consumer Products Makers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every new scented candle is matched to its wick by an experienced chandler burning test candles for days and judging flame, melt pool and glass by eye, so the number of new scents a maker can ship each season, and the odds that one comes back as a recall, rest on a judgement nobody has written down.

## The Problem
A candle is a small combustion system. The wax blend (paraffin, soy, coconut, or a mix), the fragrance oil and its load (~6–10% by weight, estimate), the dye, the vessel's diameter and glass, and the wick's construction and size all interact. Change one and the candle burns differently. A fragrance with heavy, slow-evaporating notes can choke a wick; a light, solvent-rich one can make the same wick flare. Retail buyers want a new holiday line every year, ~a dozen or more scents per programme (estimate), and each scent in each vessel needs its own wick.

The person who decides is the lab technician or chandler, usually one or two people at a mid-sized maker (estimate). They pour test candles with two or three candidate wicks, cure them for days, and burn them in cycles, commonly four hours at a time, logging melt-pool diameter and depth, flame height, flicker, soot, mushrooming, tunnelling, glass temperature and how the candle behaves near the end of its life. Experienced chandlers start near the right answer: they know that this supplier's soy needs a size up from its paraffin blend, that vanilla-heavy oils behave like a dye, that a three-wick tin with this diameter tunnels if the wicks are set too close. They cannot write the rule down, and a new technician takes seasons to learn it.

The stakes are safety and money. ASTM F2417, the voluntary fire-safety specification for candles, limits flame height and end-of-life behaviour, and retailers require third-party test reports against it. When the pairing is wrong in production, the candle can flare high enough to crack its glass. CPSC recalled about 653,000 Trader Joe's scented candles in August 2024 after 14 high-flame reports, a branded candle line of about 13,000 units in December 2024 and about 640 three-wick candles sold at Crate & Barrel in March 2025 (search summary). NFPA's 2014–2018 data still shows 7,610 home candle fires a year, 81 deaths and $278M in property damage, though most start with something flammable placed too close rather than a defective candle. The commercial cost is quieter: scents that miss the programme deadline because the burn test is still running, and lines that ship with an over-sized wick "to be safe" and soot the customer's wall.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge comes first. The expert's judgement is made while watching a flame, often across a bench of twenty burning candles, and recorded in a notebook as "flame a bit high at hour 3, good melt pool". The variables they react to (flame flicker, smoke on the glass, the shape of the pool) are visual and continuous. Capturing them means filming every test burn under controlled conditions, logging glass temperature and weight loss, and tying each candle to a structured recipe record that most makers keep in spreadsheets with free-text fragrance names. Fragrance oils are proprietary mixtures; the maker knows the supplier's name and flash point, not the composition.

The labelling challenge is that experts disagree with themselves. A chandler calls a melt pool "full" on one day and "a touch shallow" the next, depending on room draught and what they burned before. Pass and fail under ASTM F2417 is measurable for flame height, but the commercial judgement ("this will tunnel by the second burn in a customer's kitchen") is not. The outcomes that would settle it, customer complaints, returns and incidents, arrive months later through retailer feedback and CPSC reports and are rarely joined back to the lab record.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. A recommendation is only useful if it beats the chandler's first guess: if the model suggests three wicks and the chandler would have tested the same three, nothing is saved. It has to cut the number of test rounds per scent, run on a bench camera and a laptop in a small plant, and leave the chandler signing the result, because the maker carries the liability.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A burn-test record first. Each test candle gets a recipe record (wax lot, fragrance supplier and code, load, dye, vessel, wick series and size, pour and cure dates) and is filmed at fixed intervals. Computer vision segments the flame and melt pool in each frame, measuring flame height, flicker and soot, melt-pool diameter as a share of the vessel and time to full pool; thermocouple or infrared readings add glass temperature. The chandler confirms or corrects the automated read, which becomes the label.

On top of the record, a recommender proposes the first wicks to test for a new scent from its recipe features and the maker's own history, with an uncertainty band, and suggests the next test that would most reduce that uncertainty. Over time it flags fragrances that behave unlike their category and production lots whose burn profile drifts from the approved sample.

## Impact If Solved
Cutting one test round per scent saves roughly a week per fragrance in the run-up to a seasonal programme, which at a maker launching dozens of scents a year decides how many reach the holiday shelf (estimate). A standing, filmed burn record also gives the maker evidence for retailers and CPSC when an incident report arrives, and keeps the chandler's judgement inside the company when the chandler retires.
