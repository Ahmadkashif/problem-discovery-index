# The Pour Line From August to November

**Industry:** [[consumer-products-makers|Consumer Products Makers]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Candle and seasonal-goods plants build most of their year's output in a few months, so line workers lose their autumns to mandatory overtime and temp crews, and better forecasting and level scheduling would give them back predictable weeks.
**Tags:** #time-series-forecasting #exponential-smoothing #gradient-boosting #feature-engineering #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #automation

## The Problem
About 35% of US candle sales happen in the Christmas and holiday season, according to the National Candle Association, and the programmes for those weeks ship to retailer distribution centres from late summer. A candle plant's pour line therefore runs hardest from roughly August to November (estimate): melting wax, blending fragrance, setting and centring wicks, pouring, cooling, trimming, lidding, labelling and packing. Pen, marker and crayon plants have the same shape a few months earlier for back-to-school, and seasonal décor makers for spring and Christmas sets. Production workers are about 69% of employment across the industry (2022 Economic Census), and in the peak most plants add temporary workers and mandatory Saturday shifts (estimate).

The schedule is set late because the orders are set late. Retailer purchase orders for a seasonal programme firm up after line reviews and are revised as early sell-through comes in; a planner who cannot see that coming builds to the first order, then scrambles. The burden lands on the line. Shifts are announced on Thursday for Saturday, a wick-setting station that should run two people runs one, and a temp crew trained in an hour pours candles whose wicks drift off-centre.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The work is physical and repetitive: standing at a hot pour station, reaching to centre wicks in hundreds of vessels an hour, breathing a fragrance-laden plant all shift (estimate). Overtime is welcome money for some workers and a family crisis for others, especially parents arranging childcare around shifts confirmed two days ahead. The quality consequences fall on them too: an off-centre wick found at final inspection means a rework queue at the end of an already long day, and the experienced pourers carry the new temps.

The seasonal cliff is the other side. After the holiday programmes ship, hours drop and temps are released, and permanent staff wonder each January whether the next season will be bigger or smaller.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A plan the floor can see. Forecasts built from retailer point-of-sale history, programme commitments and early sell-through set the seasonal build months earlier and spread it across the summer, with buffer stock sized to the uncertainty rather than the first order. The schedule is published weeks ahead, workers bid for overtime blocks in an app instead of being assigned, and the plan flags the weeks when a temp crew will be needed so training happens before the rush. On the line, a camera check of wick centring at the cooling conveyor catches drift while it can still be corrected.

## Impact If Solved
Moving part of the peak build into summer turns forced overtime into chosen overtime for most of the crew, with shifts published ~3–4 weeks ahead instead of 2–3 days ahead (estimate). Fewer late changes mean fewer reworked candles at the end of a shift and a peak that feels like work rather than a siege.
