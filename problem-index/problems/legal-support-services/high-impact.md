# The Server's Read of an Address

**Industry:** [[legal-support-services|Legal Support Services]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A veteran process server looks at an address, a name and one failed attempt and knows when the defendant will be home, whether the address is dead and whether the person at the door is lying, and none of that judgement is recorded, graded or passed to the junior server who takes the next job.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Every civil case begins with a person standing at a door holding papers. The server gets a name, a last known address and a deadline, usually with little else. A routine serve is one to three attempts at varied times of day; a hard serve can be a week of stakeouts, neighbour conversations, a skip trace and a fresh address. The server is paid per serve, often a flat fee, so every wasted attempt comes out of their margin, and in the meantime the plaintiff's service window is running: 90 days in federal court under FRCP 4(m), shorter in many state proceedings, and a hard stop in an eviction or a temporary restraining order.

Experienced servers carry a working model of their territory that no system holds. The cues are of this kind (illustrative examples, not survey findings): which apartment complexes have a gate code that changes monthly, which neighbourhoods empty out by 7:30 a.m., that a pickup in the driveway on a Saturday morning suggests the subject is home while a car seat in the back suggests the person who answers may be the spouse. They read a door answered with "he doesn't live here" against the mail on the step and the shoes in the hall. They know a sudden change in a subject's routine means the subject has spotted them. They know which addresses from a debt buyer's file are years stale and which skip-trace hits are worth a drive. Vendor stakeout guides describe the practice — prior photo and vehicle, early-morning and weekend attempts, unmarked cars — but the judgement about which of those to deploy, and when to give up and request substituted service, lives in the server.

That judgement is also the only check on the evidence. The affidavit of service is the court's entire record of what happened at the door, signed under penalty of perjury. Where it is false the defendant never learns of the suit: in New York City only about 17% of 366,000 consumer-credit suits filed in 2019–2023 drew a response, courts entered roughly 152,000 default judgments against consumers in 2019–2024, and a 2015 class settlement vacated nearly 200,000 judgments tied to fabricated service. Since 2010 the city has required GPS devices and tamper-proof electronic logs. National networks now photograph and geotag every attempt. The data that would show what good serving looks like exists in quantity. It is used to prove an attempt happened, not to learn from.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's reasoning is invisible in the log. A GPS point says the server was at the address at 6:42 a.m. on a Tuesday. It does not say why they chose 6:42, what they saw that made them come back Saturday instead of Wednesday, or why they concluded the occupant was the subject's brother. Capturing that means getting a server who is paid per serve to record a few words per attempt from a car, without slowing them down, and joining it to the outcome days later. Most servers run one-person businesses on ServeManager or paper; their history is fragmented across networks they subcontract for.

The labelling problem is that "served" is a noisy label for a good decision. An address can be correct and the subject still evasive; a serve can succeed on a lucky fourth attempt that the expert would never have made. Experts disagree with themselves: the same server rates the same address differently after a long day or a recent dog bite. And the label that matters most — was the service valid — arrives only when a defendant moves to vacate a default or a traverse hearing is held, which is rare (656 traverse hearings across 434,000 NYC consumer-credit cases in 2019–2024) and biased toward the worst cases.

The deployment problem is speed and incentive. A recommendation must arrive on the server's phone before they pull away from the curb, and be better than their own instinct, or it will be ignored by exactly the people whose knowledge trained it. Volume filers who buy on price have not paid for better planning, and a model trained on servers' own logs has to be offered to them as a tool, not as surveillance, or they will stop recording the reasons it needs.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An attempt planner trained on GPS-stamped attempt histories, the address and occupancy signals the server saw, skip-trace freshness and the eventual outcome, which tells a server or dispatcher, for each open job, the next best time window, the probability the address is good and the point at which diligence is documented well enough to ask for substituted service. Veteran servers' attempt sequences are the training signal; a one-tap reason code per attempt ("vehicle present", "occupant denies", "no answer, lights on") captures what the GPS cannot. Juniors get the veteran's plan for a territory they do not know yet, and dispatchers can route hard jobs to the servers whose histories show they convert them. Checking finished affidavits against the device trail is a separate problem, taken up in the return-of-service and ML notes.

The deliverable for the server is fewer wasted drives. The deliverable for the attorney is an earlier, honest answer to whether service will happen inside the window, and a diligence record built attempt by attempt rather than reconstructed from memory.

## Impact If Solved
The arithmetic, all estimates: ~$2.0B of process-serving fees a year divided by ~$50–150 a serve is ~13–40M serves. If a wasted attempt costs a server ~$10–15 in time and fuel, removing one attempt from even a quarter of those serves returns ~$30–150M a year to servers paid per job, and shortens the path to service inside the statutory window. Better planning also attacks the pressure behind the industry's worst failure: a server who is not losing money on dead addresses has less reason to write up an attempt that never happened.
