# The Server Paid Only When the Door Opens

**Industry:** [[legal-support-services|Legal Support Services]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A process server spends the day driving to doors that mostly do not open, is paid when one does, and walks up to strangers who do not want what they are carrying with no one knowing where they are.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #optimization-fundamentals #descriptive-statistics #data-integration #worker-facing #automation #quick-win

## The Problem
A working server's day is a stack of papers sorted by deadline and a route built in their head. Routine jobs pay a flat fee that typically covers a few attempts; rush jobs and stakeouts pay more but not by the hour. The server plans attempts around the windows people are home — before 8 a.m., after 6 p.m., weekends — which means early starts, late finishes and Saturdays. Between doors they log each attempt, photograph the house, and at the end of the day type affidavits and diligence notes for the jobs that closed and the ones that did not. Jobs come from several sources at once: their own attorney clients, a national network paying a lower fee, a colleague's overflow, each with its own app and its own upload rules.

The work is also physically exposed. Servers are approaching people at the moment those people learn they are being sued, evicted or divorced. Dogs, threats and assaults are an occupational hazard, and many states have added process servers to protected-class assault statutes for that reason. Most servers work alone, and the only record of where they are is the GPS log their client will read later.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Income depends on attempt efficiency the server only partly controls. A stale address from a debt buyer's file costs the same fuel and hour as a good one and pays nothing until a skip trace turns up a new lead. A network that pays per completed serve pushes the risk of failed attempts entirely onto the server, which is the pressure behind the industry's worst habit — writing up an attempt that did not happen.

The hours eat family time and safety at once. The best windows to find people at home are the same hours other workers spend with their own families, and the most dangerous jobs (evictions, restraining orders, family cases) are often the most time-critical.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Three things the server would use every day, none of which needs a model of where subjects are. A single job queue that pulls work from every source — attorney e-mails, network apps, a colleague's overflow — into one list sorted by deadline and distance, with each source's upload and affidavit format handled behind the scenes so an attempt is entered once. A pay ledger that reconciles every job against what each source owes: completed serves, paid and unpaid attempts, rush and stakeout surcharges, mileage, and which network is late paying, so the server can see what a job actually earned and which sources are worth taking. And a safety check-in that flags high-risk papers (evictions, restraining orders, prior threats or dogs noted at the address), shares the server's location with a chosen contact while a flagged job is open, and raises an alert if the job is not cleared in the expected time.

## Impact If Solved
Entering each attempt once and reconciling pay automatically could return several hours a week of evening paperwork to a full-time server (estimate), show plainly which sources pay below the cost of the attempts they demand, and put a safety net under people who now walk to doors alone.
