# The Planner Who Knows Which Feeder Can Take It

**Industry:** [[electric-utilities|Electric Distribution Utilities]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A senior distribution planner can tell in minutes whether a feeder can take a new data centre, solar farm or EV depot and roughly what the upgrade will cost, while the utility's formal answer takes a months-long study, and the planner's read is never recorded, tested or kept.

## The Problem
Every request to connect something new to the distribution grid goes to a small planning group. That covers a 40 MW data centre, a 5 MW community solar array, a school bus depot with twenty chargers, a subdivision of all-electric homes and a battery behind a supermarket. For each one the utility must decide whether the feeder, the substation transformer and the protection scheme can take it. If they can't, it must work out what has to be built and who pays. The formal process is a study: build or update the feeder model in CYME, Synergi or OpenDSS, run load flow and short-circuit cases across seasons and contingencies, check thermal limits, voltage, reverse power flow and protection coordination, then write up the required upgrades and a cost estimate. Above fast-track sizes, that takes weeks to months. Large loads that need a new substation bank or transmission work wait years.

The senior planner usually knows the answer before the study starts. They know that feeder 1204 is fine for solar in spring but trips its regulator settings on a hot July afternoon. They know the Elm Street bank is at 85% of nameplate in summer peak, so anything over 3 MW means a second bank and a two-year wait for a transformer. They know a request on the rural end of a long feeder will hit voltage limits whatever the hosting map says, and that a developer who files at four utilities at once rarely builds at more than one. This is pattern recognition built across hundreds of studies, outages and energisations. It is tacit in the full sense: the planner cannot write it down as rules, and juniors take years to acquire it.

Load growth has made the gap expensive. Data centre demand alone is projected to rise from about 4.4% of US electricity in 2023 to 6.7%–12% by 2028 (LBNL for DOE). Utilities face large-load requests well beyond what will ever energise. Planning groups have not grown with the queue, and the senior planners who carry the judgement are close to retirement. Customers, commissions and economic-development agencies blame the utility for slow answers. The utility, meanwhile, risks over-building for phantom load or under-building for real load, and either error ends up in a rate case.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the planner's read happens in a hallway conversation or a first-pass email ("probably fine below 3 MW, needs a bank above that"), and is never recorded before the study. What survives is the study report: a PDF with tables of violations, upgrade lists and cost estimates, filed by project number, not by feeder. Joining it to the feeder's model, loading history, AMI data and what was later built means rebuilding years of records. The network models themselves change constantly and are often wrong about connectivity, so the features at the time of each study must be reconstructed, not read off today's model.

The labelling problem is that the "right answer" is unstable. Two planners screening the same request will disagree on borderline cases, and the same planner is more conservative after a recent failure. The study result is a better label than the quick read, but studies embed assumptions (seasonal peaks, DER output profiles, protection settings) that differ by planner and year. The final label, whether the project energised without violations and what it actually cost, arrives years later and only for projects that went ahead. Requests that were withdrawn after a high cost estimate are censored observations, not negatives.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. A screen that is slower than a planner's glance, or wrong in a way that later embarrasses the utility before its commission, will be ignored. It has to give an answer in seconds with a reason a planner recognises: which constraint binds, which comparable past studies it resembles, and how confident it is. It must also respect that network models are utility property and often protected critical infrastructure information, so it has to run inside the utility's environment.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A capacity screen trained on the utility's own history. Extract every past interconnection and large-load study into structured records: location, size, technology, binding constraints, required upgrades, estimated and actual cost, outcome. Join each record to the feeder and substation state at study time: topology, conductor and transformer ratings, peak and minimum loading from SCADA and AMI, existing DER, protection settings. Train models to predict study outcomes for a new request: probability that it fits without upgrades, the likely binding constraint, and an upgrade cost range. Calibrate them against the study that follows. Capture each senior planner's quick read as a one-click label on new requests, so the model learns from, and is graded against, the expert it is meant to scale.

Public data gets a version started without any utility's private records. EIA-861 gives each utility's customers, sales and distributed generation, and EIA-860 its generators. FERC Form 1 gives plant and cost figures for investor-owned utilities, and published hosting-capacity maps give feeder-level limits. Together they support a territory-level screen that a developer or a small co-op can use before any study is ordered.

## Impact If Solved
If even half of the requests that clearly fit, or clearly don't, are answered in days instead of months, planners can spend their time on the hard cases. Utilities can show commissions a documented, consistent screening process, and developers get early cost signals that cut speculative filings. The planner's judgement stays with the utility after retirement, recorded and graded rather than lost.
