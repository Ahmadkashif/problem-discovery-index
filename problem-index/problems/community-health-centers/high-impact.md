# The Care Team's Read of Which Patient Is About to Drop Out of Care

**Industry:** [[community-health-centers|Community Health Centers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced community health worker or nurse care manager senses which patient on a high-risk panel is about to vanish for a year, and calls in time, but the health center never records that read, never learns which ones were right, and loses it when the worker leaves.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #transformers #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A health center's chronic-care patients live on thin margins. Many work hourly shifts, share a phone, rely on a cousin for rides, move when the rent goes up, and lose Medicaid when a renewal letter goes to an old address. A patient with uncontrolled diabetes can keep three visits in a row and then disappear for eleven months, returning through the emergency department with an HbA1c of 12. The people who stop this are the care team between visits. In 2025, health centers employed 14,199 case manager FTEs, 3,337 community health worker FTEs and 2,591 outreach worker FTEs (UDS Table 5), alongside nurse care managers counted with nursing.

An experienced community health worker with a panel of ~100 high-risk patients (estimate) does not call them in list order. She calls the man who cancelled his ride twice, the woman whose refill sat unclaimed at the in-house pharmacy, and the patient who said "todo bien" a beat too quickly on the last call. She knows which patients come back on their own after a missed visit and which never do. She knows that a new phone number usually means a move, and that a teenager answering means trouble at home. Asked how she chooses, she says she knows her patients. That is tacit knowledge: pattern recognition built over thousands of calls and home visits, which the worker cannot write as a rule.

The outcome shows up in the numbers HRSA publishes for every center. In 2025, 5.57M health center patients had hypertension and only 69.1% had it controlled. Of 3.2M patients with diabetes, 26.3% had an HbA1c above 9% or no test during the year (UDS Table 7). A patient who drops out of care counts against both measures. It also costs the center the PPS payment for every visit not kept, at a Medicare base rate of $207.72 in 2026 (NACHC) and typically more under Medicaid. And it costs the quality pool in any value-based contract. The center records the missed appointments. It does not record the worker's judgement of who was about to go, what she did about it, or whether it worked. When she leaves, often for a better-paid hospital job (estimate), her replacement inherits a list sorted by last visit date.

This is a different judgement from the physician's in-visit read in [[physician-practices|Physician Practices]], where the question is who is sicker than they look. Here the question is who is about to stop coming. It is formed across weeks of calls, rides and refills, mostly by non-licensed staff, and the outcome is graded by a kept visit, a controlled reading or a lapse in coverage, not a diagnosis.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection challenge is that the worker's read is never written down as a prediction. It shows up only in who she chose to call first, how many attempts she made, whether she booked a ride or an interpreter, and the free-text note she typed afterwards ("pt sounds overwhelmed, will try again Fri"). Some of the signals she uses are in the record: missed visits, unfilled prescriptions at the center's own pharmacy, cancelled transport, eligibility responses showing a coverage end date. Others are not: the tone of a call, a home she visited, what a neighbour told her. To capture the expert, the system has to log her prioritisation decisions and call notes as data, joined to the outcomes that follow. Those outcomes are scattered across the scheduling system, pharmacy fills, state eligibility files and hospital admission feeds that most centers receive irregularly.

The labelling challenge is that her action changes the outcome. A patient she calls and rescues looks like a patient who would have come back anyway, and a patient she didn't call who later vanished may have been unreachable. Workers are also inconsistent with themselves: more cautious after losing a patient, less thorough in the week before UDS closes, and different with patients who share their language. Labels have to be outcome-based (no kept visit within the measure's clinical interval, uncontrolled at year end, coverage lost, emergency visit) and judged against similar patients who were not contacted.

The deployment challenge is that it must be faster and quieter than the worker. She already has a list. A tool that ranks 400 patients by a risk score with no reason will be ignored within a week. One that flags the same frequent no-shows she already knows adds nothing. It must surface the few patients she would not have picked, give the reason in her terms ("refill 21 days overdue, coverage renewal due next month, two cancelled rides"), work in English and Spanish at least, and fit a phone in a car between home visits. It must also not turn into a surveillance score on patients who are already wary of institutions.

## What a Solution Looks Like
First, a ledger of the worker's decisions. Every outreach attempt gets a structured record: who was chosen and in what order, the channel, the barrier found (transport, coverage, cost, language, housing, work hours), what was arranged, and the worker's own one-tap rating of how worried she is. Most of this can be drafted from the call log and note text, so the worker only confirms it. Each record is graded later against outcomes: kept visit within the clinical interval, blood pressure or HbA1c at the next reading, coverage status at renewal, and emergency visits from admission feeds.

Second, a model trained on the ledger and the outcomes. It learns which combinations of utilisation, pharmacy, coverage, transport and note language preceded a patient's dropping out, and which outreach actions brought similar patients back. Each morning it puts three to five patients at the top of each worker's queue that the worker had not prioritised, each with the reason and the action that worked for similar patients. It also gives a new worker the departing worker's pattern library, with its track record. The model supports her judgement; it does not replace it.

## Impact If Solved
About 1.72M health center patients had uncontrolled hypertension in 2025 (30.9% of 5.57M, UDS). Bringing even 5% of them back into regular care would mean ~86,000 more patients seen and treated each year (estimate). A center whose diabetes measure moves from 26% to 22% poorly controlled would move up its state peer ranking and its value-based quality payments. For a center with 20,000 patients, retaining ~500 more chronic patients for three extra visits a year is ~1,500 visits, roughly $300,000–$450,000 of PPS revenue at ~$200–$300 a visit (estimate).
