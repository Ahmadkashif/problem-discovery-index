# The Eligibility Worker Facing Six-Month Renewals and Work Requirements

**Industry:** [[community-health-centers|Community Health Centers]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** From January 2027 a health center's eligibility worker must help hundreds of patients prove 80 hours a month of work or an exemption, and renew twice as often, with state portals, paper pay stubs and a 30-day cure window, so she gets back time and the patients keep their coverage.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #logistic-regression #survival-analysis #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #automation

## The Problem
Health centers employed 5,228 eligibility assistance worker FTEs in 2025 (UDS Table 5). They sit in a small office off the waiting room, or at a folding table at a school or farmworker camp, helping patients apply for and renew Medicaid, CHIP and marketplace coverage. Almost half of health center patients, 15.7M, were on Medicaid or CHIP in 2025 (UDS).

Two federal changes arrive on the same day. From 1 January 2027, states must require adults aged 19–64 in the Medicaid expansion group to show 80 hours a month of work, school, community service or qualifying income, or an exemption, at application and renewal (CMS-2454-IFC). Someone who fails verification has 30 calendar days to cure it. From the same date, states renew most expansion adults every six months instead of every twelve (SMD #26-001). CMS projects the work rule alone will cut enrollment by 2.3M in FY2027 (search summary). For the eligibility worker, this means twice the renewals, plus a new kind of proof for patients paid in cash, working variable shifts for several employers, or caring for a relative.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
She already carries a caseload of renewals with deadlines that are not hers. Now each one needs pay stubs, a letter from a farm labour contractor, a school enrollment record or a doctor's note supporting a medical frailty exemption. The patient often reaches her after the state's notice has already gone to an old address. She knows that each case she cannot finish in time means a patient who loses coverage. That patient then pays on the sliding fee or stops coming, and the center loses the Medicaid PPS payment. She hears the patient's panic first-hand and has no way to see which of her ~300 open cases (estimate) is closest to the edge. The rules differ by state, change by guidance letter, and are learned on the job. A new hire takes months to become competent (estimate).

## What a Solution Looks Like
A caseload assistant that knows every patient's renewal and verification dates from the state's eligibility responses and the center's own records. It ranks cases by the risk of losing coverage before the deadline. It reads the documents the patient photographs (pay stubs, employer letters, school records) and fills the state's form. It checks the exemption criteria against the chart, for example pregnancy, disability or a qualifying condition documented by the patient's own clinician. It sends reminders in the patient's language by text. The worker reviews, signs and submits, and spends her time on the hard cases rather than on retyping.

## Impact If Solved
If the assistant cut the time per renewal from ~45 to ~20 minutes (estimate), each eligibility worker could clear about twice as many cases in a month. If it kept even 2% of a 10,000-member Medicaid panel enrolled through the first renewal cycle, a center would keep ~200 patients' coverage and roughly $150,000 a year of Medicaid visits (estimate).
