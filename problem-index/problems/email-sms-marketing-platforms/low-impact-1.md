# Send Timing and Frequency Optimisation

**Industry:** [[email-sms-marketing-platforms|Email & SMS Marketing Platforms]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Every platform ships send-time optimisation trained on opens, and none of them models the thing that actually matters, which is what an extra message costs in the recipient's future willingness to hear from you.
**Tags:** #survival-analysis #causal-inference #gradient-boosting #time-series-forecasting #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #hidden-markov-models

## The Problem
Send-time optimisation is a standard feature: predict when each recipient is most likely to engage and stagger sends accordingly. It is trained on open timestamps, which for a large share of the audience are pre-fetch events generated by a privacy feature rather than by a person. The feature continues to report lift.

Frequency is the larger and less examined decision. A brand sending three campaigns a week generates revenue from each and attrition from all of them — unsubscribes, complaints, and the quieter form where a recipient simply stops engaging and eventually stops being sent to. The revenue is measured immediately and attributed to the campaign; the attrition accumulates over months and is attributed to nothing. Every incentive in the system — campaign-level reporting, quarterly targets, volume-based platform pricing — points toward sending more, and the cost shows up as a gradually deadening list that someone eventually describes as a deliverability problem.

## What Already Exists
Send-time optimisation ships in Klaviyo, Braze, Iterable, Salesforce Marketing Cloud and essentially every competitor. Frequency capping exists as a rule — no more than N messages per week — set by a marketer's judgement. Engagement-based sunsetting is standard practice and is usually driven by open recency, inheriting the same broken signal. Braze and Iterable offer more sophisticated journey-level pressure controls. Some platforms provide predictive lifetime value and churn scores at the profile level.

## The Customisation Gap
The generic feature optimises the immediate response; the decision that matters is a trade between immediate revenue and the recipient's long-run tolerance, and that trade is specific to the brand, the product's purchase cycle and the individual. A weekly grocery message and a quarterly furniture message have entirely different tolerance profiles, and both platforms apply the same optimiser.

The right formulation treats each recipient as having a depleting resource — attention, goodwill, inbox tolerance — that messages consume and time restores, with attrition as a hazard that rises with recent contact. That makes frequency a per-recipient optimisation over expected lifetime value rather than a global cap, and it is a modelling problem that every platform has the data for and none has built, largely because the answer reduces volume.

Cross-channel pressure is the second gap. A recipient receiving email, SMS, push and a retargeting ad in the same day experiences one brand being relentless, and the caps are set per channel in separate systems. Total contact pressure is what the person actually feels and nothing measures it.

And the timing model needs rebuilding on signals that survive privacy protection — clicks, site sessions, purchases, SMS replies — and honestly reported: for many audiences the true send-time effect is small, and a feature claiming lift on inflated opens is reporting its own artefact.

## Impact If Solved
Frequency is the highest-leverage uncontrolled variable in the channel. A per-recipient policy optimising lifetime value instead of campaign revenue typically means sending less to the people closest to attrition and more to those who tolerate it — which raises total revenue while slowing list decay, the thing every brand loses to eventually. It also directly reduces complaint rates, which since the 2024 sender requirements is no longer a soft metric but a condition of delivery.
