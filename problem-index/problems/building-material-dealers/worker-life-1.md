# The Boom-Truck Driver at a Site With No Address

**Industry:** [[building-material-dealers|Building Material Dealers]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The driver gets back the hours lost circling unmarked lots, waiting on framers and hauling loads home, and stops carrying the blame for a load built wrong in the yard.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #gradient-boosting #cnns #optimization-fundamentals #large-language-models #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
A lumberyard driver's day starts at ~5:30 a.m. (estimate) with a load the yard crew built the evening before: a framing package banded in the order the framer will use it, or a stack of trusses, or sheathing and wrap for three jobs. The ticket says "Lot 47, Cypress Ridge," which is a dirt road off a highway with no house numbers yet, or a remodel in a cul-de-sac where the homeowner's car is in the drive. The driver finds the lot by memory or a phone call to the outside salesperson, who calls the builder, who calls the framer.

On site, the framer wants the load "by the garage, not the street," the ground is too soft for the outriggers after rain, the power line is in the way of the boom, or the site isn't ready and the load goes back to the yard. If the yard built the load in the wrong order, the driver unloads it in the wrong order and hears about it. If an item is missing, the driver gets the call a couple of hours later and the dealer sends an add-on truck. Each driver makes ~3–8 drops a day (estimate), and a failed delivery costs the whole morning.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Drivers are skilled and scarce: a boom or crane-equipped truck needs a CDL, a careful operator and someone who can read a site in a minute. Their pay often depends on completed drops, and their safety depends on ground conditions and overhead lines that nobody checked before dispatch. They absorb the frustration of builders and framers for mistakes made by a dispatcher's guess or a yard crew's pick list, and they spend unpaid time on hold trying to find a lot that a GPS pin would have solved.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A delivery app that gives the driver the drop pin, a photo of the site from the last visit, the framer's placement note and the site contact, captured by the outside salesperson or the builder's superintendent when the order is placed. Before dispatch it confirms the site is ready (a text to the superintendent the evening before), flags weather and ground risk, and checks the loaded items against the ticket by photo at the yard. On site, a photo proof of delivery with the load's position closes the ticket and settles later "it was never delivered" calls. Each failed delivery is coded by cause, so the dispatcher, not the driver, sees which builders and sites keep failing.

## Impact If Solved
If site confirmation and drop pins cut failed or returned deliveries from ~5–10% of drops to ~2–3% (estimate), each driver recovers several hours a week, the yard stops rebuilding loads, and the add-on truck that follows a missing item becomes rare. The driver's day becomes one of driving and unloading, not searching and apologising.
