# Work Cards Checked Against ADs From Memory

**Industry:** [[aircraft-mro-shops|Aircraft MRO & FBO Shops]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Maintenance tracking software keeps a due list of ADs by aircraft, but nothing reads the actual work card a mechanic is about to sign against the current AD revision, its applicability to this serial number, the approved method of compliance and the failure history in public Service Difficulty Reports.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #bert #evaluation-metrics #compliance #data-integration #automation #quick-win

## The Problem
Before a repair station signs off an inspection or repair, someone has to be sure the work package covers every airworthiness directive that applies to that aircraft, engine, propeller and installed component. That means using the right AD revision, accomplishing it by an approved method or an approved alternative method of compliance (AMOC), and recording it so the owner's AD status under 91.417 stays true. The FAA published 317 final ADs in 2025 and 249 proposed ones. Each is written against a model, a serial-number range, a part number, or a modification or service-bulletin status that is often found only in the aircraft's own records. Many recur at hour, cycle or calendar intervals; some are superseded or revised mid-interval.

At a business aviation or general aviation shop, the inspector or the records clerk prints the tracking system's due list, reads each AD on the FAA's Dynamic Regulatory System, and checks by eye that the work cards in the package reference the right paragraph and revision. At an airline MRO, engineering writes engineering orders, but the shop still checks task cards against customer packages. A missed recurring AD, a superseded revision or a misread applicability paragraph makes the aircraft unairworthy and the signature a violation. It usually surfaces later, in a pre-purchase records review or an FAA inspection.

## What Already Exists
CAMP, Traxxall and Veryon's Flightdocs track AD due items per aircraft from the vendor's AD research, and airline systems (AMOS, TRAX, IFS Maintenix) hold the AD library inside the maintenance programme. Aircraft Technical Publishers and OEM portals publish AD and service-bulletin libraries. The FAA posts every AD in its Dynamic Regulatory System (searchable, but with no bulk download or API found; final ADs also appear in the Federal Register) and every Service Difficulty Report as a yearly CSV (67,652 reports for 2025). Generic document AI and large language models can read PDFs.

## The Customisation Gap
The tracking tools answer "which ADs are due on this aircraft?" They don't check the document the mechanic signs. The aviation-specific logic is: parse AD applicability (serial ranges, "except those modified by SB…", part-number dash numbers) against the aircraft's configuration from its records; check that each work card cites the current revision and an approved method or an AMOC on file; catch recurring intervals computed from the wrong baseline; flag cards that cite superseded ADs; and pull the SDRs filed on the same part number and ATA chapter so the mechanic sees what failed on the fleet before. It has to show the exact AD paragraph behind every flag, because the inspector is signing, not the software. This is the owner's MVP idea. The rules are public (ADs and SDRs), but the aircraft's configuration, embodied service bulletins, AD status and work cards are customer records, so a demo runs on public ADs against a synthetic aircraft until a shop shares data. The SDR side is also thin for the shops this note targets: ~93% of 2025's 67,652 reports came from air carriers, and Cessna aircraft account for only 1,348, so for business and general aviation the checker leans on ADs, with SDRs as occasional context.

## Impact If Solved
A shop that turns a 2–4 hour AD reconciliation per annual or C-check package into a 20-minute review of flagged items saves ~150–400 inspector hours a year at ~100 packages (estimate). The bigger saving is the AD finding avoided: a missed recurring AD discovered at a pre-buy can knock tens of thousands of dollars off a business jet's sale price (estimate) and expose the signing mechanic to certificate action.
