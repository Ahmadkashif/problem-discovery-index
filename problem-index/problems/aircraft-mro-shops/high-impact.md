# The Borescope Call Behind Every Engine Removal

**Industry:** [[aircraft-mro-shops|Aircraft MRO & FBO Shops]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An inspector looks through a borescope at a turbine blade and decides by experience whether the damage is inside limits, needs a shorter re-inspection interval, or means pulling the engine. That one call commits a multi-million-dollar shop visit and months of shop time, and it is recorded as a sign-off, never as evidence a model or a successor could learn from.

## The Problem
Turbofan engines are inspected on wing through borescope ports: scheduled inspections from the engine manual, repeat inspections that ADs and service bulletins mandate on known problem areas, and unscheduled looks after a bird strike, an exhaust-gas-temperature trend or a pilot report. A certified inspector, usually at night on a line station or in an MRO hangar, feeds the probe through the port, turns the engine by hand or with a drive tool, and walks the stages blade by blade: combustor liners, high-pressure turbine blades and nozzles, compressor airfoils. For every finding (a nick, a dent, a burn, missing thermal barrier coating, a crack at the trailing edge, material loss at the tip) the inspector measures it against the manual's limits and decides. Serviceable. Serviceable with a reduced re-inspection interval. Or unserviceable, which means an engine change and a shop visit.

The limits are written, but applying them is not mechanical. The probe image is small, oblique and lit from one side. Depth is estimated from shadows or measured with stereo or 3D phase tips if the shop has them. Experienced inspectors know things the manual doesn't say. Coating loss on one blade row on this engine type tends to run into base metal within a few hundred cycles. A "crack" on a nozzle guide vane is often a coating craze line. Burn patterns on two adjacent combustor segments mean a fuel nozzle problem rather than two separate findings. One inspector's "within limits, re-inspect at 250 cycles" is another's "pull it now." A speaker quoted by Aviation Week in 2023 cited a 2022 University of Canterbury study putting human inspection accuracy at about 70%. Nobody keeps score inside a given shop.

Both errors are expensive, and both have got worse. A wrong removal sends an engine into a shop queue that Bain found 35% or more longer than pre-pandemic for legacy engines and over 150% longer for new-generation engines. A narrowbody performance-restoration shop visit runs to ~$3–7M (estimate), and the operator leases a spare at ~$100,000–$200,000 a month (estimate) if one can be found at all. IATA counted 648 GTF-powered aircraft on the ground at the March 2025 peak, waiting for shop visits, spare engines or parts. A missed or under-called defect is worse: an in-flight shutdown, a turbine blade release or an uncontained failure, followed by an AD and a fleet-wide inspection campaign. In between sits the reduced-interval call, which quietly multiplies inspection labour across a fleet when it is made too cautiously.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the evidence is rarely kept in a usable form. Modern videoscopes record images and video, but what gets stored is a few still frames attached to a work order, not the full stage sweep with each blade identified. The finding is written as text ("HPT stage 1 blade 23 TBC loss 15%, within limits") and signed. The inspector's reasoning — why 15% and not 25%, why this was a coating loss and not a burn — is never captured. Capturing the expert means asking them to mark the image at the moment of the call, during a night shift, with the aircraft due out at six, without adding a step that feels like paperwork.

The labelling problem is that experts disagree with each other and with themselves, and the truth is late. The same inspector will call borderline coating loss differently at the start and end of a shift, and two shops reading the same video will disagree. Hard ground truth arrives only when the engine is next opened, at a shop visit months or years later, often at a different MRO, where the blade is measured, repaired or scrapped. That outcome sits in a teardown report the line inspector never sees. Without joining the two records, any model trained on dispositions learns the average inspector's habits, not the blade's condition.

The deployment problem is that the call legally belongs to a certificated person working to approved data. A model cannot sign anything. It can only be a second reader, a measurement aid or a consistency check against the shop's and fleet's history. It also has to work at the engine, on a tablet or the borescope's own screen, faster than the inspector can rotate to the next blade. Engine manufacturers control the inspection limits and are building their own AI tools (GE Aerospace with Waygate Technologies, among others), so an independent tool must fit inside OEM-approved procedures rather than compete with them.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start with capture. Record every borescope inspection as structured video: engine serial, stage, blade index from the turning tool's position, probe type and calibration. Let the inspector tap a finding on screen, pick the damage type and drag a measurement, which is no more work than writing it up. Store the disposition against that blade. Then build the link that nobody holds today: pull the shop visit teardown findings for the same engine serial and blade positions, from the MRO or the lessor's records, and attach them as delayed labels.

On that library, train segmentation models for damage type and extent per engine family, with transfer learning from general defect imagery and the OEM's damage catalogue. Fit survival models for damage progression, so that "15% coating loss at 4,200 cycles since shop visit" turns into a probability of crossing the limit before the next scheduled look. Run the result as a second reader. It flags findings the inspector didn't mark, measures consistently, shows the last ten calls on this stage across the fleet and what the teardown later found, and sends the hardest borderline calls to a senior inspector or the operator's powerplant engineer. The inspector still signs.

The same library becomes a training tool. For a newly certificated mechanic it shows "here are 300 HPT stage 1 calls on this engine type, and how each held up."

The capture problem has two neighbours in the vault. Upstream, the part makers' final inspector makes the same kind of call on new castings and rotating parts in [[aerospace-parts-suppliers|Aerospace Parts Suppliers]]. On the ground, the diagnostic bottleneck in [[auto-repair-shops|Auto Repair Shops]] is the car-repair version of the senior mechanic's judgement. Aviation differs from both: the inspected part stays in service, so the outcome label arrives on wing or at the next teardown.

## Impact If Solved
If an operator or MRO with ~300 engines under its care avoids even three unnecessary removals a year (estimate), it saves ~$9–20M in shop visits and spare-engine leases at the values above, and returns engine capacity to the tightest part of the chain. The larger value is the record itself: borescope calls joined to outcomes, held by the shop, outlasting the retirement of the inspectors — 41% of certificated mechanics are already over 60 (ATEC 2025).
