# The Optometrist's Call on a Glaucoma Suspect

**Industry:** [[optometry-practices|Optometry Practices]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced optometrist reads an optic nerve, an OCT scan and a visual field and knows whether to watch, treat or refer, but that call is recorded only as a follow-up date, so it can't be graded, taught or kept when the doctor leaves.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #survival-analysis #transfer-learning #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Every comprehensive eye exam ends with the optometrist looking at the back of the eye. Most nerves are plainly healthy. A meaningful minority are not plainly anything: a cup that looks large, a rim that seems thin in one sector, an OCT printout with red and yellow segments, intraocular pressure in the low twenties, a visual field with a few depressed points that may be learning effect. The OD has to decide in the room whether this patient is normal, a glaucoma suspect to re-test in six or twelve months, early glaucoma to start on drops or SLT, or a referral to an ophthalmologist. That decision sets years of visits, tests and medication, and it is the gateway into the medical side of the practice.

Experienced optometrists make this call on patterns they struggle to put into words. They know that the OCT's normative database paints many healthy high myopes red because their discs are tilted and their nerve fibre layers sit differently. They know a scan whose segmentation line has jumped onto a floater is wrong, that a field defect which respects the horizontal midline and matches the thin rim sector is real, and that a single unreliable field means nothing. They weigh family history, race, corneal thickness and how the patient's nerve looked five years ago against today's printout. A newer OD with the same printouts tends to do one of two things: refer everything that flags red, which floods ophthalmology with healthy myopes and loses the patient from the practice, or reassure too readily, which lets early glaucoma progress until the field loss is permanent. About half of Americans with glaucoma are thought not to know they have it (~1.5M of ~3M, estimate).

None of this reasoning is captured. The chart records the measurements, a diagnosis code if one was chosen, and a recall interval. It does not record which features drove the call, how confident the doctor was, or what they would need to see to change it. The outcome, progression or stability, appears years later, sometimes in the same practice and often in an ophthalmologist's chart the optometrist never sees. When an owner sells to a platform, or an associate moves on, the practice keeps the scans and loses the judgement that interpreted them.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's judgement has to be captured at the moment of the exam, inside a fifteen- to twenty-minute visit, by a doctor who is also the business owner. Asking an OD to annotate each scan with the features they relied on and their confidence is a tax with no visible return. The raw inputs (OCT volumes, fields, photographs, pressures) do exist, but they are scattered across device viewers from Zeiss, Topcon, Heidelberg and Optos and are rarely joined to the decision in one record.

The labelling challenge is severe. Glaucoma progression is defined differently by structure and by function, the two often disagree, and the clinical truth takes three to five years to emerge. Experts disagree with each other on cup-to-disc ratios and with themselves on the same image months apart. Treatment also contaminates the label: a suspect started on drops who never progresses may not have needed them, and the doctor's own decision prevents the outcome that would have proved it right. Referral outcomes are censored, because a referred patient's course is recorded in another organisation's system.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. A decision aid has to read the scans from whichever device the practice owns, flag segmentation artefacts and myopic false positives before the doctor sees red, and present a progression risk in the seconds between the slit lamp and the conversation with the patient. If it is slower than the OD's own glance, or if it flags every myope, it will be switched off in a week. Liability adds weight, since the licensed optometrist signs the diagnosis.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A suspect ledger first and a model second. Each time the OD codes a glaucoma suspect, starts treatment or refers, the system drafts a short structured record from the scans and the note: the features that drove the call (rim sector, RNFL thinning pattern, field cluster, pressure trend), a confidence level and the stated interval. The doctor confirms it in seconds. Over time each record is graded against what happened: progression on guided progression analysis, conversion to treatment, the ophthalmologist's finding on referral.

On top of the ledger, train models that reproduce the experienced reader. A convolutional network pretrained on large public fundus and OCT sets is fine-tuned to read the practice's own scans, to recognise segmentation errors and the myopic disc pattern, and to predict time to progression with a survival model that combines structure, field series, pressure history and risk factors. The output is a ranked risk, with the evidence highlighted, shown inside the exam. Platforms can then compare referral and treatment patterns across hundreds of ODs against outcomes and coach on evidence rather than anecdote.

## Impact If Solved
If even a fifth of glaucoma progression were flagged one visit earlier, practices would preserve visual field in patients who are otherwise caught after irreversible loss. Cutting unnecessary referrals of healthy myopic eyes would keep a recurring medical patient, and roughly ~$300–$600 a year of testing and visit revenue (estimate), in the practice instead of sending it to ophthalmology. A judgement that currently leaves with each retiring or selling owner would become an asset the practice, or the platform that buys it, can keep.
