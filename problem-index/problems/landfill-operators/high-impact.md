# The Wellfield Tech's Read of a Landfill Gas Well

**Industry:** [[landfill-operators|Landfill Operators]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A senior landfill gas technician can look at a month of readings from a well and know whether it is pulling air, drowning in liquid or starting to heat, and that judgement, which decides how much methane is captured, sold or leaked, leaves with the technician and is never recorded as anything more than a valve position.

## The Problem
A large landfill has a few hundred vertical and horizontal gas wells connected by header pipes to a blower that holds the field under vacuum. Each wellhead has a valve. A wellfield technician walks or drives the field with a portable analyser (QED's Landtec GEM is the common one), reads methane, carbon dioxide, oxygen, balance gas, temperature, static and differential pressure at each well, and opens or closes the valve. The federal NSPS and emission guidelines require every well to be checked at least monthly: positive pressure or gas above 55 °C must be corrected within 5 days, and an unresolved temperature exceedance needs a root-cause analysis within 15. On top of that, the surface must be walked every quarter on a 30-metre grid, and any reading 500 ppm above background re-monitored within 10 days and fixed with a new well within 120 days if it keeps failing. One technician may cover ~100–300 wells a month (estimate).

The valve setting is a trade-off with no formula. Open it too far and the well pulls air through the cover, oxygen rises, methane falls below what the RNG plant needs, and in the worst case air feeds subsurface combustion. Close it too far and gas escapes through the surface, fails the quarterly walk and shows up on an aerial survey: Carbon Mapper's flights found point-source plumes at 52% of the 200+ landfills surveyed, at rates averaging 1.4 times what operators reported. Experienced technicians make the call from patterns. They know a well whose balance gas creeps up in dry weather is short-circuiting through a cracked cover, that a well whose flow collapses after rain is watered in and needs pumping rather than more vacuum, that a slow rise in temperature with carbon monoxide and falling methane is a heating event, and that wells near the edge of the cap behave differently from wells in deep waste. They know which wells the last storm changed and which neighbours of a problem well to adjust together.

None of this is written down. The analyser stores the readings and the new valve position. It does not store why the technician moved the valve, what they expected to happen or what they ruled out. When a senior technician retires or moves to another site, the next one starts again from the readings, tuning by the rulebook and learning which wells lie. The cost shows up three ways: methane that never reaches the RNG plant, which now sells into a D3 RIN market that averaged ~$2.45 in 2025 (search summary); surface exceedances and unreported emissions that regulators and satellites now see; and, rarely but catastrophically, heating events like the one at Chiquita Canyon, which drew ~410 odour violations and ended the landfill's working life.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the technician's judgement has to be captured while they work, in the field, wearing gloves, with a handheld that has no room for reasoning. A month of readings per well is a thin time series, and the variables that drive behaviour — rainfall, barometric pressure, cover condition, recent waste placement, liquid levels, settlement — sit in other systems or nowhere. The most valuable cases, the early signs of heating or a cracked cap, are rare at any one site, so learning them needs data pooled across many landfills, which belong to different owners.

The labelling problem is that the outcome of a valve change is confounded. Methane at the plant moves with the whole field, the weather and the blower, not one valve. Two senior technicians will set the same well differently, and the same technician sets it differently in March and August. Experts also disagree with themselves about what a reading means: early heating and a simple air leak look alike for weeks. Honest labels come from what happened next — whether oxygen fell, whether the surface reading cleared, whether temperature and carbon monoxide kept rising — and those take a month or more to arrive.

The deployment problem is speed and liability. A recommendation must reach the technician at the wellhead, faster than their own judgement, as one adjustment and one reason. It must never tell them to do something that creates an exceedance, and every recommendation becomes part of a compliance record that an agency can request. Technicians distrust tools that second-guess them. Automated wellheads (Loci Controls adjusts valves as often as hourly, search summary, and reports a 15% gain in methane capture) remove part of the manual work, but a controller tuned on flow and methane alone does not carry the expert's read of heating, liquids and cover failure.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A well history first and a model second. Every reading, valve change, pump-out, surface exceedance and repair is joined to rainfall, barometric pressure, waste placement, cover inspections and the RNG plant's inlet gas, so each well has a continuous record. The technician confirms in a tap why they moved a valve — air, liquid, heat, surface, plant demand — and the system records what happened next. Models trained on that record, across many sites, suggest the next adjustment for each well with a predicted effect on oxygen, methane and surface readings, flag wells whose pattern matches the early stages of heating or watering-in, and schedule the follow-up readings the regulations require.

The goal is not to replace the technician but to give a new technician the senior technician's read on day one, to tell the senior one which of three hundred wells need them this week, and to give automated wellheads the judgement layer they lack. For the landfill manager and the RNG developer, the same record shows where collection is being lost and why.

## Impact If Solved
On a landfill sending ~3,000 standard cubic feet a minute of gas to an RNG plant (estimate), capturing 5% more methane is ~150 scfm more, ~40,000 MMBtu a year at ~50% methane, worth on the order of $1M a year with D3 RINs at ~$2.45 and gas value included (estimate). Catching a heating event or a cracked cap weeks earlier avoids surface exceedances, odour violations and, at the extreme, the multi-year abatement order and early closure that Chiquita Canyon shows is possible.
