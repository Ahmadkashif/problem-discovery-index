# The Scale-House Attendant at the Gate

**Industry:** [[landfill-operators|Landfill Operators]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The scale-house attendant tickets every truck, decides what each load is and what it costs, and takes the anger when the answer is wrong, and gets back calm and confidence when the system identifies the truck, the load and the rate before the window opens.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #large-language-models #cnns #object-detection #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
Every vehicle entering a landfill stops on the inbound scale. The attendant identifies the hauler or customer, the vehicle and its stored tare weight, asks what the load is, chooses the material and rate code — municipal solid waste, construction debris, tyres, contaminated soil, asbestos, sludge, yard waste, appliances — checks that any special waste has an approved profile and the paperwork that goes with it, takes payment from cash customers, and sends the truck to the right part of the site. On the way out it is weighed again and the ticket printed. A busy landfill handles hundreds or thousands of vehicles a day (estimate), in morning surges when route trucks all arrive at once.

Most of the judgement lands on one person in a booth. Is that roll-off really clean concrete, which is cheaper, or mixed debris? Is the homeowner's trailer carrying a mattress, paint cans or a propane tank? Does this industrial customer's load match its approved profile, and has the approval expired? Does this hauler's account still have credit? Which of four rate schedules applies — contract, municipal, out-of-county, posted gate rate — and which host-community or state surcharge goes on top? The attendant often makes the call from a glance through a window or a camera, while the line backs up onto the road and drivers lean on their horns.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Scale-house work is customer-facing in the hardest way. Drivers paid by the load resent every minute in line; cash customers dispute prices; haulers argue about weights and material codes; and when a prohibited item gets through and starts a fire or a non-conforming load is found on the face, the question is who let it in. Attendants are usually paid as clerks, work long shifts alone, and are blamed both for slowing the line and for not looking closely enough. The rules they apply — rate codes, profiles, surcharges — change and live in binders, emails and memory.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Licence-plate and RFID reads identify the truck and its account before it stops, pull its tare weight and its approved profiles, and propose the material and rate code from the customer's history. Cameras over the bed classify the visible load and flag likely mismatches or prohibited items, so the attendant looks at the right loads instead of every load. Expired approvals, credit holds and the applicable surcharges are shown on screen with the reason, and a dispute screen shows the photo, weight and rate rule the attendant can point to. Cash customers can pre-register and pay at a kiosk.

## Impact If Solved
Cutting average ticket time from ~90 to ~45 seconds (estimate) at a site handling 800 vehicles a day takes ~10 hours of queueing off drivers daily and removes most of the morning surge the attendant absorbs; flagging mismatched loads with a photo gives the attendant evidence instead of an argument.
