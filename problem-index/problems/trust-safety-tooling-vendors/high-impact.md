# Every Vendor Reports Accuracy on Its Own Exam

**Industry:** [[trust-safety-tooling-vendors|Trust & Safety Tooling Vendors]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Classification accuracy is reported on internal test sets whose composition nobody discloses, so buyers cannot compare products and the failures that matter are hidden inside an aggregate.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #bayesian-inference #transfer-learning #bert #compliance #transformers

## The Problem
A platform choosing a content classification vendor receives accuracy figures. Precision, recall, area under a curve, per category. Each vendor computes them on its own test set, constructed by that vendor, with composition that is not published — which languages, which communities, which content types, in what proportions, labelled by whom under what guidelines.

That makes the numbers incomparable and, more importantly, uninformative about the failures that matter. A classifier with strong aggregate performance can be unusable in a specific language, systematically wrong about a particular community's speech, or unable to distinguish discussion of a harm from advocacy of it — and none of that appears in a single figure. The documented moderation failures of the past decade cluster almost entirely in exactly those places.

Buyers have no route to verify. Evaluating a classifier properly requires a labelled test set the buyer trusts, in their own content distribution, in their own languages — which is a significant project most platforms cannot undertake, and precisely the platforms that cannot are the ones most dependent on vendor tooling.

Regulation has now made this acute. Reporting regimes require platforms to publish moderation accuracy statistics, and the platforms are reporting numbers generated by systems whose accuracy is asserted by the vendor. The regulatory requirement has arrived before the measurement infrastructure that would make compliance meaningful.

And there is no shared benchmark. Other machine learning fields have benchmark datasets that make claims comparable and progress visible; trust and safety has none of consequence, partly because the content is sensitive and cannot be freely distributed, and partly because no party has had an interest in creating one.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data is genuinely difficult to share. A benchmark for harmful content classification contains harmful content, and distributing it raises legal, ethical and practical problems that do not arise in most machine learning domains. This is a real obstacle and it is not insurmountable — held-out evaluation services where models are submitted rather than data distributed are an established pattern.

The commercial incentives are straightforward. Vendors reporting strong self-administered numbers have nothing to gain from a comparison that might rank them lower, and the first vendor to participate in a shared benchmark would be volunteering to be measured against competitors who are not.

Labelling is the deeper problem. A benchmark requires ground truth, and ground truth for contested content is contested — reasonable experts disagree, and the disagreement is concentrated in exactly the cases that matter. A benchmark that pretends a single right answer exists would encode one set of judgements as truth, which is a real risk and an argument for measuring against expert panels rather than single labels.

And the platforms who would benefit most are the ones with least leverage. Small and mid-sized platforms depend on this tooling and cannot individually demand better measurement.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Build a shared benchmark as an evaluation service rather than a distributed dataset. Vendors submit models, the benchmark holder runs them against held-out content, and results are published per segment. That structure resolves the data distribution problem and has precedent in other sensitive domains.

Report per segment, always. Per language, per dialect, per content type, per category, with confidence intervals — because the aggregate is the mechanism by which the failures that cause documented harm remain invisible. A vendor performing well on average across fifty languages may be unusable in fifteen, and that statement is the one a buyer needs.

Measure against panels, not single labels. Ground truth for contested content should be a distribution of expert judgement, with the inter-annotator agreement reported as the ceiling — because a benchmark claiming a single right answer on genuinely contested cases is measuring conformity rather than accuracy.

Let the regulatory requirement create the demand. Reporting regimes now require accuracy statistics, and a regulator or a standards body specifying how those statistics must be produced — with per-segment disclosure and independent evaluation — would create the pull that the commercial market cannot generate on its own.

## Impact If Solved
This tooling decides what billions of people see and what happens to their accounts, and it is bought on self-administered exams. A shared per-segment benchmark would let platforms choose on quality, let regulators verify the accuracy claims their regimes now require, and make the language and community failures that produce most documented moderation harm visible for the first time — which is the precondition for anyone fixing them.
