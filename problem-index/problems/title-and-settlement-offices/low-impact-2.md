# Seller Identity Verification Built for Title

**Industry:** [[title-and-settlement-offices|Title & Settlement Offices]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Identity verification is a solved product for banks and marketplaces, but a title office needs to know something narrower: whether the person selling this parcel is the person the recorded vesting deed names.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #logistic-regression #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #compliance #automation #data-integration #revenue-impact

## The Problem
In a seller impersonation fraud the criminal poses as the owner of a property, usually vacant land or a second home with no mortgage and an absentee owner, lists it below market, accepts a fast cash offer, signs remotely or through a complicit notary, and collects the proceeds. The real owner discovers the sale months later, and the title office that closed it faces a claim and a damaged underwriter relationship. ALTA's 2026 study found 59% of title firms saw at least one attempt in 2025, more than double the 2024 share, with vacant land named by 82% of respondents. The defence today is the escrow officer's instinct and a checklist: compare signatures, call the number on file, ask questions only the owner would know.

## What Already Exists
General identity verification vendors check a government ID against a selfie, run liveness detection and screen against fraud databases; remote online notarisation platforms add knowledge-based authentication and credential analysis; wire verification services confirm that a payee owns the bank account. These are mature, and many title offices already pay for at least one of them, but the products answer "is this a real, live person with this ID?"

## The Customisation Gap
The title question is ownership, not identity. A useful check ties the verified person to the vesting deed: the grantee's name as recorded (with middle initials, trusts and LLCs), the property tax mailing address and its history, prior signatures and notary journals on recorded instruments, the age of the ownership, and whether the owner's known contact details match those on the listing. It needs the risk pattern specific to impersonation — vacant or free-and-clear parcel, long-held, absentee owner, below-market price, cash buyer, remote signing, rush, payout to a new account — and outreach to the owner of record at addresses derived from public records rather than from the person claiming to be the seller. Entity sellers need officer and authority verification against state filings.

## Impact If Solved
An ownership-linked check run at order open, rather than at the signing table, lets an office stop an impersonation before commissions and buyer funds are committed, documents the diligence the underwriter will ask about, and removes the officer's dependence on noticing that an email "felt wrong".
