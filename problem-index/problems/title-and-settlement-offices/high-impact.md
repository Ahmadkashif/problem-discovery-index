# The Examiner's Read of a Chain of Title

**Industry:** [[title-and-settlement-offices|Title & Settlement Offices]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A senior title examiner reads a chain of title and knows which defects will hold up a closing, which can be insured over, and which deed is a forgery, and none of those calls is ever recorded with its outcome, so the judgement retires with the examiner.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #large-language-models #transformers #gradient-boosting #graph-neural-networks #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #compliance

## The Problem
Every file in a title office passes through one person's reading. The searcher delivers a stack: the vesting deed and the deeds behind it back to the office's search standard (often 30 to 60 years, estimate, or to a prior policy it can start from), open mortgages and assignments, judgments and liens indexed against the owners' names, probate and divorce records, easements, plats, tax status and HOA filings. The examiner reads it and writes the commitment. Each item becomes a Schedule B-I requirement (something that must happen before closing), a Schedule B-II exception (something the policy will not cover), or nothing at all.

The craft is in the judgement calls. A judgment against "Robert J. Miller" is probably not the seller Robert A. Miller, but the examiner decides whether to demand a name affidavit or let it pass. A 1998 mortgage with no recorded release is almost certainly paid; the question is whether this servicer still exists and how long a release will take, or whether the underwriter will let the agent insure over it on a payoff letter. A deed from an estate with no probate filing might be curable by an affidavit of heirship in this state and not in the next. A quitclaim on a vacant lot from an owner with an out-of-state mailing address, a new email and a cash buyer in a hurry is the pattern ALTA's members now see most often in seller impersonation fraud: 82% named vacant land as the most common target in the 2026 study. Experienced examiners describe this as knowing which items "matter" — pattern recognition built over thousands of files, specific to their county's recording habits and their underwriter's appetite.

None of it is written down with its result. The commitment records the requirement, not why it was raised; the decision to insure over sits in an email to the underwriter; how the requirement was cleared, and how long that took, sits in file notes; and the outcome that would grade the read — a claim, a closing that slipped, a fraud caught or missed — arrives months or years later at the underwriter. The examiner's own feedback loop is the occasional claim notice. When an examiner retires, the office keeps the commitments and loses the reasoning. ALTA's time study shows what that costs: 36% of files are "difficult", and a difficult file takes 45 hours of work against 22 for a standard one, most of the gap spent on curative work that a better first read would have scoped on day one.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the examiner's reasoning has to be captured while they examine, in a production queue where every minute counts. A commitment is assembled from a requirement library in the production system; the choice of requirement is captured, the reason is not, and items deliberately ignored leave no trace at all. Historical files hold search packages and final commitments, but rarely the intermediate read, the underwriter's approval, or which raised requirements proved unnecessary.

The labelling problem is severe. The label the industry cares about — a claim — is rare and late, arriving years after the policy and concentrated in a few defect types, so any model trained on claims alone is starved. Usable proxy labels exist — whether a requirement was cleared, how many days it took, whether the underwriter approved an insure-over, whether the closing slipped, whether a fraud was flagged — but each is noisy, and examiners disagree with each other and with themselves. The same examiner will raise a name affidavit on Monday and pass the same pattern on Friday afternoon at month-end. Local practice matters: what is routine in a Texas plant county is a red flag in a New York search.

The deployment problem is speed and liability. An examiner can clear a clean refinance chain in minutes. A tool that adds review time to clean files will be ignored, and a tool that misses a live defect puts the agent's underwriting agreement at risk. Any assistance must arrive before the examiner opens the file, be faster than their own read on clean files, and point at the specific instrument when it disagrees.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Capture first, model second. The examination screen records each item's disposition — requirement, exception, insure-over request, or "reviewed, no action" — with a one-click reason code, pre-filled by a language model that reads the instrument and proposes the disposition the office's senior examiners have historically chosen for similar items. Each disposition is joined downstream to its outcome: requirement cleared or waived, days to clear, underwriter decision, closing on time or late, post-closing claim notice if any.

On top of that record, three models. A chain model treats the property's history as a graph of parties and instruments and flags breaks, unreleased liens and name mismatches, with the examiner's past dispositions as labels. A curative model predicts, at order open, which requirements a file will need and how long each will take, so the escrow officer knows on day one whether the closing date is realistic. And a fraud model scores seller-side transactions on the patterns the office's examiners have learned to distrust. Each surfaces the instrument it is reacting to, ranked by how often senior examiners acted on that pattern.

## Impact If Solved
If an office scopes its difficult files on day one, much of the 23-hour gap between a difficult file and a standard one becomes planned work rather than a last-week scramble, and the senior examiner's read becomes an asset the office keeps. For an industry where 60% of firms have fewer than five people, a recorded, transferable read is the difference between an office that survives its founder's retirement and one that does not; and graded dispositions give underwriters the first evidence of which agents examine well.
