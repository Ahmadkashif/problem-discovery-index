# The Employee on the Remedial List

**Industry:** [[security-awareness-training|Security Awareness Training]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Someone clicked a well-crafted fake email during a busy afternoon and is now on a list, assigned training, and in some organisations discussed with their manager.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #confidence-intervals #bayesian-inference #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #compliance #hypothesis-testing

## The Problem
Simulated phishing produces a list of people who clicked. What happens to that list varies enormously: in better programmes it triggers a short piece of training and nothing else; in others it goes to line managers, appears in performance conversations, generates repeat-offender designations, or in documented cases has been connected to disciplinary processes.

The employee's experience is of being caught out by a deliberate deception designed by their own employer, during a working day, at a moment when they were busy. Well-constructed simulations are difficult by design — that is the point of them — and clicking one is evidence of being a normal person with attention divided across a job.

The distribution of who clicks is not random in ways that matter. People under time pressure, in high-volume communication roles, in customer-facing functions, and those less familiar with the organisation's conventions click more — which means the remedial list correlates with workload and with tenure rather than with carelessness, and in some organisations with role types that skew demographically.

And the lesson taught is frequently the wrong one. An employee who clicks and immediately realises should report it, and the thing that makes reporting less likely is knowing that being identified has consequences. Punitive programmes optimise the visible metric by teaching silence.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is a person being tested without consent by their employer, in a way designed to succeed, with consequences attached to failing. Whatever the security rationale, the experience is of a trap, and it is widely described that way by the people who fall into it.

The consequences can be material. Where results reach performance management, a moment of divided attention on a Tuesday afternoon affects someone's standing, and there is usually no mechanism to contest the characterisation.

The distress from emotionally significant lures is documented and has been the subject of public controversy — simulations promising bonuses that did not exist, or referencing job security during layoffs, have caused real harm to people who were already anxious.

And the person who most needs to feel able to raise a mistake — someone who clicked something real — is the one the programme has taught to expect consequences.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Decouple results from discipline as a design principle, and measure what it buys. Reporting rates under punitive and non-punitive programmes are comparable, and demonstrating that punishment reduces reporting is the evidence that would change policy where a values argument does not.

Make reporting the celebrated behaviour. A programme that recognises reporting — including reporting after clicking — teaches the behaviour that limits real harm and removes the incentive to hide.

Control the templates and their cost. Emotionally significant lures should require explicit approval with the harm acknowledged, and the resistance benefit they provide should be measured rather than assumed, because if it is small the case for them collapses.

Account for exposure in the results. Someone in a high-volume communication role receives more simulations and has more opportunity to fail, and a raw click count that ignores this mischaracterises them. Exposure-adjusted rates are straightforward and would change who appears on the list.

And give people a route to contest. An employee who believes a simulation was unreasonable, or that their result was mischaracterised, should have somewhere to say so — which most programmes do not provide.

## Impact If Solved
Millions of employees are tested without consent by systems designed to succeed, with consequences that fall hardest on the busiest and the newest. Decoupling from discipline with evidence rather than assertion, celebrating reporting including after a click, controlling harmful templates and adjusting for exposure would make these programmes teach the behaviour that actually protects an organisation — instead of teaching people not to say anything.
