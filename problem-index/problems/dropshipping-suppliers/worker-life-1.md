# Supplier Sourcing Analyst

**Industry:** [[dropshipping-suppliers|Dropshipping Suppliers]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A sourcing analyst approves suppliers onto the platform from documents, a sample order and a video call, then never learns whether the approval was right.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #bayesian-inference #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #evaluation-metrics #change-point-detection #compliance #worker-facing

## The Problem
Before a supplier appears in the catalogue somebody vets them. The analyst checks business registration, reviews product listings for obvious infringement and prohibited items, places a sample order, examines what arrives, and holds a call.

That is a thin basis for a decision affecting thousands of merchants. The sample order is fulfilled knowing it is a sample. The registration confirms an entity exists. The product photographs may not be of the supplier's own goods. The analyst cannot visit the facility and frequently cannot verify that a facility exists.

Then the supplier is approved and the analyst moves on. Performance afterwards — fulfilment times, defect rates, disputes, merchants who churn after a bad experience — is not routed back to the person who approved them, so the analyst has no way to calibrate their own judgement across hundreds of decisions.

Volume pressure runs one way. Catalogue breadth is a competitive metric, more suppliers means more products, and the analyst is the constraint on growth.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Consequential decisions with no feedback is the defining feature of the role. An analyst who has approved five hundred suppliers cannot say which signals predicted good outcomes, because outcomes never reached them.

The commercial pressure is one-directional. Approving expands the catalogue and rejecting is a cost, so the analyst is the only party in the process arguing for restraint, on evidence they know is inadequate.

Fraud is adversarial and improving. Suppliers presenting well and performing badly is the specific failure mode, and the vetting process is public enough that it can be prepared for. The analyst is competing with parties who know exactly what will be checked.

And their accumulated pattern recognition has nowhere to live. Any intuitions they develop are personal, undocumented, and leave when they do.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Close the loop. Supplier performance after approval — fulfilment reliability, disputes, merchant churn attributable to them — should reach the analyst who approved them as a routine report. This costs nothing and is the only mechanism by which vetting judgement improves.

Learn what actually predicts outcomes. With hundreds of approvals and measured subsequent performance, which vetting signals predict reliability is a directly supervised question, and it would let the process concentrate on the checks that matter and drop the ones that do not.

Continuous rather than point-in-time assessment. A supplier approved two years ago may have changed entirely, and performance decline is detectable in operating data long before it reaches the vetting team.

Relationship signals across the platform. Suppliers sharing addresses, contacts, bank details or listing patterns are a network structure, and a supplier removed for cause reappearing under a new entity is a recurring problem detectable in the graph and not in a document check.

Sample orders that are not identifiable as samples, which is a straightforward change and materially improves what the sample tells you.

## Impact If Solved
Supplier vetting gates the quality of a catalogue thousands of merchants rely on and is performed on thin evidence with no feedback, under pressure to approve. Closing the loop and learning which signals predict performance turns an unverifiable gate into a measurable one, and the network view catches the re-registration pattern that document checks structurally cannot.
