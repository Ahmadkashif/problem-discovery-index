# The Trade Walkaround Nobody Grades

**Industry:** [[powersports-and-boat-dealers|Powersports & Boat Dealers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** The used-unit manager prices every trade-in boat, bike and side-by-side from a ~15-minute walkaround (estimate) and a guide book, the dealer later records exactly what the unit cost to recondition and what it sold for, and the two are never joined, so the store can't tell which appraisals lose money or keep the appraiser's eye when they leave.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A customer pulls in with a 2017 21-foot bowrider on a trailer and wants a trade allowance on a new boat. The used-boat manager has ~15 minutes (estimate) and a buyer waiting. They look up the J.D. Power or BUC value, then walk the boat. They press the deck by the console and the transom for soft spots, tap the hull with a phenolic hammer listening for a dull note that means delamination, run a moisture meter over the transom, pull the engine cowl, read the hours off the gauge or plug a laptop into the engine to download hours and fault codes, check the lower unit oil for milky water, look at the gelcoat, the upholstery, the trailer bunks and tyres, and ask where the boat was kept and whether it saw salt. The powersports version is faster and just as tacit: the clutch and belt on a side-by-side, the swingarm bearings, the hour meter against the odometer, aftermarket parts that hide hard riding, a frame weld that says it was rolled.

Then they write a number. The number decides the deal, because the allowance is what the customer sees. Too high, and the store has paid for reconditioning it didn't price: a new transom, a powerhead, a CVT clutch, upholstery. Too low, and the customer walks to the dealer down the road. In a boat store the mistake is large. A wet transom or a cracked stringer turns a retail unit into a wholesale one, and the difference can be thousands of dollars (estimate). Used units matter more as new sales soften: new boat retail sales fell 8.8% in 2025 to 215,237 units (NMMA), and stores lean on pre-owned gross.

What the manager knows best is written down least. That boats kept on a lift in fresh water age differently from boats kept in a wet slip. That a particular outboard family with these hours needs a power-head check. That this brand's stringers rot where the foam was poured. That a clean side-by-side with new plastics was probably rolled. That a seller who won't take it for a sea trial has a reason. This is tacit pattern recognition in exactly the sense of a craft: built over thousands of walkarounds, hard to specify, and gone when the manager moves to another dealer. Meanwhile the outcome is recorded precisely — the repair orders written against the unit, days on the lot, the final retail or wholesale price — and nobody joins it back to what the appraiser saw. The closest twin in the vault is the RV trade appraisal ([[rv-dealerships|RV Dealerships]]), where water damage plays the same role; boats add engine data the manager can download, and powersports adds abuse that hides under new plastic.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the evidence is physical and fleeting. The hammer note, the soft deck, the moisture reading, the smell in the bilge exist only during the walkaround, and the manager has no reason to record them. They record a number. Capturing the expert means recording what they look at and touch — photos and short videos of the hull, deck, engine and trailer, moisture and compression readings, the engine's own data — in under fifteen minutes, on a phone, in the sun, without slowing the deal. No dealer will tolerate a capture step that costs a sale.

The labelling problem is that the honest label is noisy and arrives late. Realised price depends on season (a boat traded in October sells in April), on the reconditioning the store chose to do, and on the sales team's effort, not only on condition. Experts disagree with themselves: the same manager reads the same boat differently in March, when they need inventory, and in September, when the lot is full and the floorplan is aging. Units the store refused or wholesaled immediately are a selected sample. And condition labels ("soft transom", "rolled") are rarely recorded consistently enough to train on without a fresh labelling effort.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. The estimate must arrive inside the walkaround, before the manager has written a number, and it must beat a guide-book lookup plus the manager's own judgement on the boats where it matters, or it will be ignored. The manager must also be able to argue with it: an appraisal that says "deduct $2,400" without showing the moisture map and the comparable sales won't survive a customer conversation.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An appraisal record first and a model second. A phone workflow guides the walkaround — hull, transom, deck, helm, engine, lower unit, trailer, or the powersports equivalents — capturing photos and short videos, a moisture-meter reading by zone, compression numbers where taken, and the engine's downloaded hours and fault history. It drafts the condition notes from the captures for the manager to confirm in seconds, and stores the manager's allowance and reasoning. Every record is joined later to the unit's repair orders, days to sale and realised price, adjusted for season.

On top of the record, learn the manager's read. Computer-vision models flag gelcoat cracks, stress fractures, oxidation, corrosion, upholstery damage and frame repairs. A gradient-boosted model combines them with readings, hours, storage history, region and season to predict reconditioning cost and the realised wholesale and retail price, with a survival model for days to sale. The output is a recommended allowance range with the evidence behind it, and a grading of every past appraisal so the store can see which units, managers and conditions lose money.

## Impact If Solved
A dealer selling a few hundred used units a year who closes even part of the gap between allowance-plus-reconditioning and realised price, say $500–1,000 a unit on the worst decile (estimate), recovers tens of thousands of dollars a year per store. Floorplan lenders and groups get a defensible used-inventory valuation. And the store keeps an experienced manager's judgement when they leave, which in a 10-person dealership is often the only appraisal expertise it has.
