# The Impostor at the Signing Table

**Industry:** [[notary-signing-services|Notary & Signing Services]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** The notary is the last human check before a forged deed records and the proceeds are wired, and the experienced agent's sense that "this is not the owner" is neither recorded, measured nor available to the thousands of new agents doing the same signing tomorrow.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #gradient-boosting #object-detection #evaluation-metrics #confidence-intervals #compliance #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A title company orders a seller signing on a vacant lot. The owner of record is an absentee owner in another state; the "seller" found the buyer online, wants a quick cash sale and has asked for a mobile notary to come to a rented office. The signing agent arrives with the deed and closing package, asks for identification, and is handed a driver's licence. They compare the photo with the face, check the name against the documents, look at the expiry date, write the details in the journal and, in California, take a thumbprint. The whole identity check takes about a minute. If the signer is an impostor with a good fake, the deed is notarised, recorded and the proceeds wired, and the real owner finds out when the tax bill changes hands or a builder appears on the land.

This is no longer rare. ALTA's 2026 survey found ~59% of title firms encountered at least one seller impersonation attempt in 2025, up from 28% in its 2024 survey; 23% saw three or more. A quarter of firms that saw an attempt paid a claim, and half of those disclosing amounts said the average payout exceeded $100,000. A seller asking for a remote signing or a notary of their own choosing was the second most common red flag. The FBI's IC3 logged $275M in real estate fraud losses in 2025, up from $173M in 2024.

Experienced signing agents describe what they notice before they can say why. The licence is freshly issued in a state the owner has no link to. The lamination feels wrong, the microprint is soft, or the barcode will not scan. The signer cannot name the cross street, hesitates over a middle name, signs slowly and copies the printed name letter by letter. A companion answers the questions. The same pattern appears in estate signings, where the question is not identity but whether a frail signer knows what they are signing and wants to: the daughter who will not leave the room, the signer who looks to someone before each answer. Agents who have done thousands of signings refuse a handful a year on this feel. New agents, who take most of the low-fee orders, rarely refuse anyone.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection challenge is that the judgement happens in a minute, in a stranger's home, with no recording. The journal captures the ID type and number, not what the notary saw or felt, and most states do not require a journal at all. Capturing the expert doing the task means imaging the credential front and back, capturing the signer's live face and signature, and logging the questions asked and the answers, at a kitchen table, without slowing a signing the agent is paid a flat fee for. Any capture also creates a record of a stranger's identity data in an independent contractor's phone, with state privacy and retention rules attached.

The labelling challenge is severe. Confirmed impostor outcomes are rare, delayed by months and stored elsewhere: in title claim files, police reports and quiet-title suits that are never joined back to the notary's journal. Most signers who trigger an expert's unease turn out to be genuine — nervous, elderly, newly moved — and the expert does not agree with themselves; an agent refuses after a near miss in the news and waves through the same signer a month later. Refusals are themselves unlabelled, since a refused impostor simply finds another notary. Capacity and coercion judgements have even softer labels: a probate court's ruling years later.

The deployment challenge is speed and fairness. Any assistance must return an answer in seconds, on a phone, offline in a basement, and be faster than the agent's own glance, or it will be skipped. It must never become a reason to refuse elderly, immigrant or thin-file signers whose IDs and manner differ from the norm, because a wrongful refusal denies someone a legal act and can breach state notary law. And the notary keeps the legal duty: a model can raise a flag, never clear a signer.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start with structured capture, then learn. At the table, the agent photographs both sides of the credential and the signer's face; the app reads the barcode and machine-readable zone, checks security features and expiry, matches the portrait to the live face, and compares the name and address with the owner of record and the documents. The agent then answers four or five prompted questions in a sentence each, mirroring what experts check: did the signer know the property, who else was present, did they read before signing, did anyone answer for them. Everything goes into a tamper-evident journal entry.

On top of that record, build two models. One scores credential authenticity from images, trained on known genuine and forged samples and on the agent's own flags. The other scores the transaction's impostor risk from context the title company already holds — vacant land, absentee or deceased owner, recent ID issue, seller-arranged notary, cash buyer, remote location — combined with the agent's answers, calibrated against the outcomes title insurers record. The output is a three-level flag with the reasons, delivered before the signer signs, plus a routing rule: high-risk signings go to an approved notary with a call to the owner of record at a number not supplied by the seller.

## Impact If Solved
Seller impersonation claims commonly run to six figures each (half of firms disclosing payouts put the average above $100,000), and the notary is the only party who meets the impostor face to face. Raising a new agent's hit rate toward an expert's on the small set of high-risk signings would prevent a large share of those losses at a cost of seconds per signing, and give every notary a defensible record of what they checked when an act is later challenged.
