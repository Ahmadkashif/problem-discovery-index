# The Copyright Coordinator in the Conflict Queue

**Industry:** [[music-publishers|Music Publishers]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A copyright coordinator spends the day assembling the same proof (agreements, split sheets, letters of direction) for one share conflict after another across a dozen society portals, and would get back the part of the job that needs judgement.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #random-forests #k-nearest-neighbors #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #automation

## The Problem
The coordinator's queue is a list of conflicts. The MLC's Overclaims Tool shows works where claimed shares add up to more than 100%. A PRO flags a counterclaim on a song the publisher registered years ago. A foreign society sends a dispute notice in its own format. A sub-publisher's local registration contradicts the original. YouTube's Content ID shows another claimant on a writer's biggest song. Each case has to be researched: who signed what and when, whether an administration deal has expired, whether a writer's share was assigned to a new publisher, whether two registrations are the same song under different titles.

The evidence is scattered across the rights database, scanned agreements, email threads with co-publishers and old split sheets. The coordinator pulls together a PDF of documents, uploads it to one portal, writes to the other claimant and waits. Help pages describe a resolution time of several weeks to several months, and HFA escalates a song-level conflict to the MLC if it is still open after two weeks. Meanwhile the disputed share's money is held in suspense. Job ads for the role list exactly this work, from researching counterclaims and resolving split disputes to documenting claims to the MLC under its dispute policy, and ask for three to five years' experience.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Most of the work is clerical and repetitive, yet it carries real stakes. Writers call to ask why their money is frozen, and the coordinator has to say the matter is pending with a society they cannot hurry. A mistake, such as conceding a share that was the publisher's or claiming one that was not, is visible and lands on them. The parts of the job that use their expertise get the least time: reading an ambiguous 1990s co-publishing deal, spotting that a "new" claimant is a successor company, judging when to escalate. That expertise is chain-of-title reasoning, and it takes years to build.

## What a Solution Looks Like
One queue across all societies and platforms, ranked by money in suspense and by deadline. For each conflict, the likely cause is classified (duplicate registration, expired administration term, assignment to a successor, wrong split), the relevant clauses and documents are pulled from the archive, and a draft evidence pack and message to the counterparty are prepared in the format each society wants. Similar past conflicts and how they ended are shown alongside, so the coordinator decides in minutes what used to take an afternoon of digging. Cases the system resolves the same way every time are batched for one-click approval.

## Impact If Solved
A coordinator who spends a quarter of the day assembling evidence instead of most of it clears several times as many conflicts a week (estimate). Suspended money reaches writers sooner, and the hard cases get the attention they need. The job becomes chain-of-title analysis instead of document courier work.
