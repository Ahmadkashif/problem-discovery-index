# The Sync Brief Answered From Memory

**Industry:** [[music-publishers|Music Publishers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A publisher's sync rep answers a 24-hour brief by recalling a handful of songs out of a catalogue of tens of thousands, and the record of what was pitched, shortlisted and licensed, which is the only way to learn what a supervisor actually buys, is never kept as data.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #transformers #contrastive-learning #word-embeddings #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
A brief arrives by email at 4 p.m. from an ad agency's music producer. A car launch needs something "uplifting but not cheesy, a seventies soul feel, female vocal, builds to a big moment at 0:25, budget around $60,000 all-in, all media, one year, US." Songs are due by noon tomorrow. A trailer house, a series music supervisor or a game studio might send the same kind of request with a three-hour turnaround. The publisher's sync team, between three and fifteen people at most companies (estimate), handles several such briefs a day on top of pitching proactively to supervisors they know.

The rep answering the brief reads past its words to what the client means. "Seventies soul feel" from this producer means a specific era of Philadelphia strings, not funk. "Not cheesy" means no lyric about driving. This supervisor always says she wants something unknown and then licenses a song her audience half-remembers. This agency's budgets come in at half the stated figure once the client sees a quote. And the song that fits perfectly has four writers, two co-publishers and a master owned by a major label that refuses car ads. None of this is in the catalogue's metadata. It sits in the rep's head, built over years of pitches that mostly went nowhere.

That record is what makes a rep good, and it is the least written down. Industry guides describe an average placement as the visible tip of dozens of pitches that did not connect. Supervisors rarely give feedback, and a decision can take weeks or never come. What a publisher does keep is the licence: title, fee, media, term. It does not keep the brief the licence answered, the other songs that were sent, which of them were shortlisted, or why the winner won. When a senior rep leaves for a rival, the publisher keeps the catalogue and loses the map of it.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection challenge is that the expert's judgement has to be captured while the rep is working against the clock. A rep under a noon deadline will not fill in a form explaining why song A went in and song B stayed out. The raw material does exist: briefs in email, pitch playlists in DISCO or Synchtank, quotes and licences in the rights system. But it lives in three places with no shared key, and nobody joins a brief to the pitch it produced and the outcome that followed. Rejections are the most informative signal, and they are mostly silence.

The labelling challenge is that taste is unstable and outcomes are noisy. A song not chosen may have been a fine fit that lost on budget, on clearance, or because the director's partner preferred another track. Reps disagree with each other about which songs fit a brief, and with themselves from one month to the next, depending on what has recently worked. A licence is a clean positive label. A silence after a pitch is not a clean negative, and shortlist information arrives only when a supervisor asks for a quote.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. A tool that returns two hundred mood-tagged tracks is slower than the rep's memory, not faster. Audio-similarity search and AI tagging already produce that kind of list, and reps describe it as a starting point at best. To be adopted, a system has to return a short list the rep would have chosen, with clearance status and a fee band attached, inside minutes. It has to show why each song is there in the terms reps use: this supervisor licensed something like it last year. And it must never surface a song that cannot be cleared for the use.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start with a pitch ledger and build the model second. Each brief is parsed into structured fields (use, media, term, territory, budget, references, mood language, deadline) and linked automatically to the playlist sent, the follow-ups, any quote request and the final licence or lapse, all drawn from email and the pitch tool rather than typed by the rep. Over a year this gives the publisher thousands of brief-to-outcome records. Each one says what was asked, what was sent, what was shortlisted, what was licensed and at what fee.

On top of the ledger, learn the rep's matching. Encode briefs from their text and reference tracks, and encode songs from audio embeddings, lyrics, writer and era metadata, and their own licence history. Train a ranker on which songs this team pitched, which reached a shortlist and which were licensed, weighted by recipient, since the same words mean different things from different supervisors. Join it to clearance data, so every suggestion carries its co-publisher approvals, master restrictions and a fee band drawn from comparable past licences. The rep stays the decision-maker. The system makes a senior rep's pattern recognition available to a junior, and keeps it when the senior leaves.

## Impact If Solved
Sync is about a quarter of US publishing revenue, roughly $1.75B in 2025 on NMPA's mix (estimate). A publisher that lifted its shortlist rate on briefs even modestly would add placements without adding staff. The bigger effect is that its deep catalogue starts earning. Catalogue songs no current rep remembers are the ones most likely to be pitched by a system that has learned what each supervisor buys. Keeping the pitch ledger also turns the rep's departure, the costliest recurring loss on a sync team, into a handover rather than a reset.
