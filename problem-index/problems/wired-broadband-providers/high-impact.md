# The Plant Fault Only the Senior Tech Can Place

**Industry:** [[wired-broadband-providers|Wired Broadband Providers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced maintenance technician reads a modem's pre-equalization taps, a spectrum capture or an OTDR trace and knows which span, tap or connector is failing, while the rest of the workforce sends a truck to the customer's house and closes the ticket as no trouble found.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #graph-neural-networks #gradient-boosting #change-point-detection #feature-engineering #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A customer calls: the connection drops every evening, video calls freeze, speed tests are a third of what they pay for. Tier 1 support reboots the modem, sees nothing conclusive and books a technician. The installer arrives, finds signal levels inside specification at the house, swaps the modem, replaces a splitter and leaves. Three days later the customer calls again. In a hybrid fibre-coax plant a large share of these symptoms come from shared plant: a loose seizure screw at a tap, a corroded connector, water in a splice, a cracked hardline span or ingress leaking in through a neighbour's damaged drop. Every home downstream sees it, intermittently, and the premises visit is the wrong dispatch.

The data to see this exists. DOCSIS modems report pre-equalization coefficients, and CableLabs methods use the micro-reflection echo in those coefficients to judge an impairment's severity and narrow down where it sits. Full-band capture shows ingress and suck-outs across the spectrum. On fibre, an OTDR trace shows every splice, connector and bend along the strand. A senior maintenance technician looks at these and "just knows": the echo on these eight modems on the same leg points at the tap two spans past the amplifier; that notch is a damaged drop on the street, not the house; that OTDR event is a macrobend in a slack loop, not a bad splice. A 1985 NCTA technical paper already noted that technicians with fewer callbacks had learned to fix problems the first time. They still do it by sight, and they can't fully explain how.

The result is measured everywhere and attributed nowhere. Windstream reports repeat trouble reports to Texas regulators against a 22% corrective-action benchmark and lists technicians, cable conditions, weather and customers as causes; cable operators file trouble-report rates per 100 working lines with California. A 2021 SCTE/NCTA paper notes that manually analysing spectrum captures across thousands or millions of modems is impractical, and that when many homes show an impairment, a premises dispatch is almost always wrong. The senior technician who can place the fault is the bottleneck, and the people who can do it are retiring faster than they are replaced.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's read is never captured. Telemetry is recorded continuously, but the label — what was actually wrong, where, and what fixed it — is a resolution code picked from a dropdown at the end of a shift, often by an installer who didn't find the real fault. The senior technician's reasoning ("the echo delay matched the distance to that tap") goes into a radio call or a text to a colleague, not a record. Plant maps are often wrong about which tap feeds which home and how long each span is, and that topology is exactly what a localisation model needs.

The labelling problem is that experts disagree with themselves and the ground truth is indirect. Two maintenance technicians shown the same pre-equalization plot will name different spans; the same technician will read it differently after a week of storm damage. The honest label is a natural experiment: the impairment signature on the affected modems disappears after a repair at a specific location. But a single visit often fixes several things at once, and some impairments are thermal or intermittent and vanish on their own, so even that label needs care. Fibre adds its own ambiguity: an OTDR event's cause is confirmed only when someone opens the closure.

The deployment problem is speed and organisation. A suggestion has to arrive before the dispatch is booked, or on the technician's phone in the bucket truck, and it has to be faster than the senior technician glancing at a PNM screen, or it is ignored. Maintenance, installation and customer care are separate departments with separate metrics; installation is frequently contracted out; and the dispatch decision belongs to a call centre whose script ends in "book a technician". A model that is right but routes to the wrong queue changes nothing.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A fault ledger first and a model second. Every repair is recorded with its location on the plant map, what was found, a photo and a meter reading, in under a minute on the technician's phone, and the ledger automatically attaches the telemetry of every modem or ONT downstream for the days before and after. The before-and-after change becomes the label: this signature, on this topology, cleared when this tap was replaced.

On the ledger, learn the senior technician's read. Convolutional models over pre-equalization taps, spectrum captures and OTDR traces classify the impairment type; a graph model over the plant topology triangulates which span or device the affected set of modems shares; a gradient-boosted triage model decides whether a new trouble call should go to the premises, to a maintenance crew at a named location, or nowhere because the impairment is already being worked. Senior technicians review the model's top suggestions during a calibration period and their corrections become training data, so the system learns their read while they are still there to teach it.

## Impact If Solved
An operator with 500,000 subscribers dispatching on the order of 0.2–0.3 trouble trucks per subscriber per year handles ~100,000–150,000 dispatches (estimate). If even 10–20% are wrong-location or repeat visits avoided at ~$150–$500 each (vendor estimates), that is several million dollars a year, before counting churn from customers who called three times. The larger gain is keeping the retiring technician's judgement: a junior technician with the model's ranked suggestions works closer to the senior's first-visit rate, and the fault ledger turns every repair into training data rather than a closed ticket.
