# The Process Knowledge a New Munitions Line Doesn't Have

**Industry:** [[defense-systems-manufacturers|Defense Systems Manufacturers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A qualified munitions line hits specification because a few senior operators and inspectors know how to read and adjust it, and the new lines built for the 2026 ramp start without that knowledge, so they miss rate for months or years.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #semantic-segmentation #gradient-boosting #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A solid rocket motor or a loaded artillery round passes through a chain of process steps where the outcome can't be fully specified in advance. Operators judge from the mixer's process record when a batch is ready to cast, and engineers judge when a cure needs a deviation. After cure a Level III radiographer grades each motor's CT and decides whether an indication is rejectable, reworkable or acceptable under the drawing's criteria. On a projectile line the press operator and heat-treat engineer judge forging and hardness drift, and fill lines have equivalent acceptance calls that radiography later grades.

The rules for these calls are written, but the procedure doesn't tell an operator when a batch is drifting before it fails or which CT indication will matter in flight. Senior people "just know". They've seen thousands of batches, remember which ingredient lots behaved oddly, and recognise a process record that will end in a rejected motor. That knowledge is concentrated in a few plants (the motor plants at Northrop Grumman and L3Harris's Aerojet Rocketdyne, and the Army ammunition plants and their contractor operators) and in people near retirement after decades of low-rate production.

The 2026 ramp exposes it. The Army made about 36,000 155mm rounds a month in March 2026 against a 100,000 goal, and the DoD Inspector General found that General Dynamics' $469M Mesquite line had produced no projectile metal parts meeting specification, partly because it adapted tooling built for the older M107 shell instead of the M795 (Defense News, July 2026). New motor makers (Anduril, Ursa Major, X-Bow) must qualify propellants and motors whose acceptance hinges on the same judgements, and a single closed ingredient supplier can force reformulation and requalification, as Nammo found in 2025 (Breaking Defense, January 2026). The drawing package transfers. The judgement does not.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's calls happen on the floor, in hazardous bays where electronics and cameras are restricted, and are recorded as a stamp or a deviation number. Process historians record each batch at most modern plants, and CT systems save every scan, but the link between a curve, the operator's decision and the eventual CT finding or static-fire result is rarely kept. Capturing it means instrumenting the decision itself: what the operator saw, what they changed and why, without slowing a line under rate pressure.

The labelling challenge is that the outcome label is rare, delayed and destructive. Most motors pass; static fires and lot acceptance tests destroy samples and arrive weeks later. CT dispositions are labels, but experienced radiographers disagree with each other near the acceptance boundary and with themselves over time, especially on low-contrast bondline indications. A model trained on one plant's dispositions learns that plant's habits, and defects are by design uncommon, so the classes are severely imbalanced.

The deployment challenge is qualification and speed. A munitions process is qualified as a whole; any tool that changes how a batch is accepted or adjusted must be accepted by the customer and often triggers requalification, so the first deployments can only advise the qualified operator and inspector. The advice must arrive faster than the expert's own read (during the mix, before the cast) or it will be ignored. All of it is ITAR-controlled and some is classified, so it has to run on premises, inside the plant's network.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start with a process record, not a model. For every batch and motor, join the ingredient lot certificates, the process records, the operator's interventions and deviations, the CT volume and the radiographer's disposition, and the downstream static-fire or lot acceptance result. Make capture a by-product: a tablet prompt at end of mix asking the operator to confirm the call with one tap and a free-text reason, and a disposition screen that saves the marked-up CT slices.

On that record, build two assistants. A process model learns from process records and ingredient lot data which batches drift toward rejectable outcomes, and shows the operator, mid-mix, the most similar past batches and how they ended. A CT segmentation model pre-marks voids, cracks and bondline separations, measures them against the drawing's criteria and ranks slices for the radiographer, who still signs. For a new line, the same record becomes the training material: the old plant's batches, decisions and outcomes, available to the new plant's engineers before their first qualification lot.

## Impact If Solved
A new line that reaches qualified rate even a few months sooner adds thousands of rounds or hundreds of motors to an industry whose output is the binding constraint on a ~$49.5B one-year munitions request (CNAS's count for ten munitions). On existing lines, earlier detection of drifting batches cuts scrap of motors worth tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars each (estimate), and a radiographer who reviews ranked, pre-measured slices instead of whole volumes can disposition several times more motors per shift. The knowledge stays when the last senior operator retires.
