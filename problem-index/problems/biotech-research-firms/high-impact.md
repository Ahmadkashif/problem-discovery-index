# The Chemist's Eye in the Design–Make–Test Cycle

**Industry:** [[biotech-research-firms|Biotech Research Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A senior medicinal chemist decides which of hundreds of candidate molecules is worth making each cycle, on a feel for structure nobody has written down, and when generative models now propose thousands of designs a week, that unrecorded judgement is the bottleneck of the whole programme.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #graph-neural-networks #perceptron-and-mlps #contrastive-learning #bayesian-optimization #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A small-molecule programme moves through repeated design–make–test–analyse cycles. On Monday the project chemists meet and pick the next batch: perhaps twenty to forty molecules from a list of several hundred ideas (estimate), drawn from their own sketches, a computational chemist's docking results and now a generative model's output. The chosen designs go to a chemistry CRO, frequently in China or India, which synthesises them over roughly two to four weeks (estimate). Biologists then run potency, selectivity and ADME assays, and the data comes back to the same meeting. A programme may cycle like this dozens of times and make ~1,000–5,000 compounds before it nominates a development candidate (estimate).

The choice of which molecules to make is where the programme's experience lives. A senior chemist looks at a structure and knows it will be a solubility problem, that this amide will be metabolised, that the route will take the CRO six steps and fail at the fourth, that the series has a hERG liability nobody has measured yet, or simply that it "looks like a drug". These are the patterns of thousands of compounds seen and made over a career. They are also inconsistent and personal. When Novartis asked 35 of its own chemists to choose between pairs of molecules, they agreed with one another at a Fleiss' κ of only 0.32–0.40, and with their own earlier answers at a Cohen's κ of about 0.6.

None of this reasoning is recorded. The electronic lab notebook stores what was made and what it measured. The registration system stores the structure. The slide deck from Monday's meeting holds the shortlist, but not the hundreds of designs rejected or why. When the senior chemist leaves, or a layoff removes the discovery team (several 2025 layoff rounds eliminated discovery research entirely), the programme keeps the assay table and loses the eye that read it.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's judgement has to be captured while the expert performs the task, during a meeting already short of time. Chemists will not annotate hundreds of rejected ideas with reasons. The workable unit is the comparison — "which of these two would you make?" — which a chemist answers in seconds. Novartis showed this works: over 5,000 pairwise answers from 35 chemists trained a model that matched held-out preferences at an AUROC of about 0.75. But harvesting those choices continuously, inside the design meeting rather than in a separate web exercise, is a product nobody has built. Historical design meetings are lost, so a programme starts with no labels.

The labelling challenge is that experts disagree with themselves. A κ near 0.6 on a two-way choice means a chemist's own answer to the same pair changes roughly one time in five (derived: with ~50% chance agreement, κ 0.6 implies ~80% agreement), with mood, recent results and framing. There are several judgements bundled together — synthesisability, developability, novelty and potency — and a single "preference" label mixes them. Outcome labels exist but are delayed and selected: only the compounds that were made have assay results, and the counterfactual (what the rejected molecule would have measured) is never observed. Any honest evaluation has to separate agreement with the chemist from agreement with the assay.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. A ranking has to be ready before Monday's meeting, faster than the chemist's own scan of the list, and shown in a form the chemist can argue with — this design was ranked down because it resembles twelve past compounds that failed solubility. A black-box score is ignored, and a model that only reproduces medicinal chemistry rules of thumb adds nothing a filter cannot.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A design-triage layer embedded in the weekly cycle. Every candidate design is scored on predicted potency and ADMET from the programme's own data and on predicted synthetic tractability from route-planning tools. On top, a preference model learned from the programme's chemists ranks designs the way the team's most experienced members would, with uncertainty shown. The meeting interface captures each choice and rejection as a labelled comparison at no extra effort. Over time the model learns which of the chemists' preferences predict assay outcomes and which are habit.

The second output is an institutional memory of design reasoning: why a series was abandoned, which motifs the team learned to avoid, what the senior chemist preferred and how often that preference was vindicated. A partner's diligence team, an acquirer or the chemist who inherits the series can query it. The same preference model can filter generative output before a human sees it, so chemists review fifty plausible designs instead of five thousand.

## Impact If Solved
If a programme reaches its candidate in fewer cycles, it saves months at a CRO's FTE rates and months of a biotech's runway. At ~4–8 weeks a cycle (estimate), removing even three wasted cycles saves most of a quarter. Capturing the senior chemists' judgement as a measured, reusable asset also changes what a biotech owns when people leave, and gives generative design a selection step that reflects the team's actual experience rather than generic drug-likeness rules.
