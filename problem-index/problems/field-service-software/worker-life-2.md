# Technician Administration at the Truck

**Industry:** [[field-service-software|Field Service Software]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Technicians stop losing an hour a day to typing up what they just did, because the visit record assembles from photographs, parts scans and a spoken summary before they pull out of the driveway.

## The Problem
A service technician's day is a sequence of visits, and each one ends with paperwork done in the truck. Record what was found and what was done. List parts used. Photograph the work. Note the equipment condition. Complete the safety or code checklist. Build the invoice and take payment. Write the recommendation for follow-up work. Close the ticket.

It runs ten to twenty minutes per call, on a phone or tablet, in a vehicle, often in bad weather and bad light, by someone whose skills are mechanical rather than clerical. Across six to eight calls it is an hour or more a day, and it is almost entirely unpaid — technicians are typically compensated on billable hours or on a commission structure that does not count administration.

The predictable outcome is that it is done badly. Notes read "checked system, working now." Parts get missed, which means they are not billed and inventory is wrong. Photographs get taken and never captioned. The recommendation for the failing part that should be replaced next season never gets written, which is a lost sale nobody knows about.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Technicians in the skilled trades are scarce and expensive to develop, and the administration is a reliable irritant in an otherwise well-liked job. It extends the working day past the last call, it is done alone in a truck, and it produces nothing the technician values.

It also creates a persistent friction with the office. Incomplete notes generate follow-up calls from dispatch, billing disputes and warranty claim denials, all of which come back to the technician as complaints about paperwork they were not paid to do properly. The technician's view — that they were hired to fix equipment — is entirely reasonable and structurally in conflict with the business's need for a good record.

And the trades cannot hire. Anything that makes the day shorter and the job more like the job people signed up for has a direct effect on retention.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Capture rather than entry. The technician photographs the equipment, the data plate, the failed component and the completed work — which most already do — and speaks a two-sentence summary. The system produces the visit record from that: the equipment identified from the plate, the parts recognised from photographs and scans, the narrative written from the spoken summary in the format the office needs, the checklist pre-filled where a photograph evidences the item.

The invoice builds from the tasks and parts, priced from the book, ready to present. The follow-up recommendation drafts itself from what was observed — this capacitor is out of tolerance, this unit is fifteen years old — which is the highest-value sentence in the whole record and the one most often omitted.

The technician reviews, corrects and signs. Two minutes instead of fifteen.

## Impact If Solved
An hour a day per technician is a seventh of the working day returned in a trade that cannot recruit, and the records get better rather than worse because they are grounded in what was actually captured. The follow-up recommendations alone — currently lost to fatigue at the end of a call — are a measurable revenue line the business is not collecting.
