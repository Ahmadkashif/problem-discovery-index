# The Location Call Nobody Grades

**Industry:** [[oil-and-gas-producers|Oil & Gas Producers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An asset team decides where to drill, how close to the neighbours and against which analog wells, then measures the result every month for years, and never joins the two, so the engineer's judgement on analogs and spacing is neither graded nor kept.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #gaussian-processes #bayesian-inference #causal-inference #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Every undrilled location in an E&P company's inventory carries a forecast. A geologist maps the target interval and its thickness, porosity and pressure. A reservoir engineer then builds the type curve: picks offset wells that look like analogs, normalises their production for lateral length and proppant, and adjusts it for the things that make this location different. Those include how close the new well sits to producing wells, whether it lands above or below them, how long the neighbours have been producing, and how big the completion will be. The type curve drives the well's net present value, its rank in the drilling schedule, the annual capital budget and, summed across thousands of locations, the "inventory" that an acquirer pays for. With the Permian making about 48% of US crude in 2025 (EIA) and the best acreage drilled first, the remaining locations are increasingly infill and lower-tier benches, where spacing and parent-child effects decide the answer.

The judgement that matters most is the least written down. An experienced engineer "just knows" that offsets drilled before a certain year used smaller completions and understate today's wells. They know a child well 600 feet from a parent that has produced for five years will lose a share of its oil, but one landed in a different bench may not. They know a competitor's reported volumes run high because of how its leases are allocated, and that a type curve built on the top quartile of a play is a sales document. They apply these adjustments as haircuts and weights, often in a spreadsheet beside ARIES, ComboCurve or Enverus, and the final curve is what gets saved.

The outcome is recorded in detail. The well's monthly volumes go to the state commission and into the operator's production system, and within roughly 12 to 24 months (estimate) it is clear whether the well beat or missed its curve. What is not recorded is the reasoning: which analogs were used and which were rejected, what spacing and timing haircut was applied, and what the engineer thought would happen to the parent well. When the engineer moves to another operator, or the asset team is merged into an acquirer's, the company keeps the curve and the outcome and loses the judgement that connected them.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the reasoning has to be captured at the moment the location is approved, by engineers who see documentation as a tax. Analog sets are built interactively and discarded. The haircut is a number typed into a cell. Development-planning tools store the final type curve and its decline parameters, not the path to them. Reconstructing old decisions from slide decks and email is possible for a few hundred wells and impractical for a whole inventory.

The labelling problem is that a well's production is a noisy label for the quality of the call. A child well can miss its curve because the geology changed, the completion was cut short, the parent was refracked, a neighbour's frac hit it, or the gathering system choked it back. A well can beat its curve for reasons nobody predicted. Experts disagree with themselves: the same engineer applies a deeper spacing haircut after a disappointing pad and a shallower one after a good year. Honest labels need production normalised for completion size and choke management, compared with what the curve said at the same months on production, and tagged with what changed after approval.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. Location ranking happens in planning cycles under capital deadlines, and a model that takes days to update, or cannot say which analogs drove its answer, will be overridden. Engineers are rewarded for wells that perform, not for forecasts that are calibrated, and a system that grades their calls threatens them unless it also helps them make the next one. To be adopted, it must propose analogs and haircuts faster than the engineer can assemble them, and show its evidence in the engineer's terms.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A location decision ledger first and a model second. Every approved location gets a short structured record, drafted automatically from the planning tool and the engineer's analog selection: target bench, spacing and stagger to each neighbour, parent ages, completion design, the analog set with inclusions and exclusions, the haircut applied and a one-line reason. The engineer confirms it in seconds instead of writing it. Each record is graded later against the well's normalised production at matching months, and against what the parent wells did after the child was completed.

On top of the ledger, learn the experts' adjustments. Train models on the full public and private well panel to predict a new well's normalised production from geology, completion and the geometry and timing of its neighbours, with calibrated uncertainty. Learn from the ledger which analogs experienced engineers choose and how their haircuts compare with what happened. Surface both to the next engineer: "these eight analogs, this predicted parent-child loss, and the last three times this team haircut a location like this by 15% it still came in 10% short." The goal is not to replace the engineer's call but to make an experienced engineer's pattern recognition, with its track record, available to the junior who inherits the acreage.

## Impact If Solved
A Permian development well costs ~$6–9M to drill and complete (estimate). An operator drilling 200 wells a year that removes a systematic 10% optimism from its type curves avoids misallocating a material share of a ~$1.5B budget (estimate) and prices acquisitions on curves it can defend. Keeping the analog and spacing judgement through a departure or a merger turns the most expensive recurring loss in the industry, the asset team's memory, into something the company keeps.
