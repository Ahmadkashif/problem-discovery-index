# The Raw-Land Go/No-Go

**Industry:** [[land-developers|Land Developers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every land deal turns on an experienced land manager's read of how many lots a tract will yield, whether and when it will be entitled, and what each lot will cost to develop, and that read is never recorded in a form that can be graded or handed to the next manager.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #large-language-models #bayesian-inference #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A land acquisition manager at a regional developer, or in a national builder's land department, looks at dozens of tracts for every one that closes. For each, a broker's package arrives: an aerial, an asking price per acre, the current zoning and a few lines about utilities. Within days the manager has to decide whether the tract is worth tying up, and within a 30–120 day feasibility period (estimate) has to bring the land committee three numbers: the lot count, the date the lots can be delivered, and the horizontal cost per lot. Those numbers set what the developer can pay for the land, what it will quote a builder per lot, and whether the deal works at all.

The experienced manager produces them partly from analysis and mostly from judgement. He drives the tract and sees that the creek on the north line will take a buffer, that the frontage road will force a left-turn lane, and that the soils by the pond mean undercut and select fill. He knows this council approved the last two rezonings in the corridor only after the density came down a notch, that the same neighbourhood group packed the planning commission against the last apartment case, and that the city's sewer plant is near capacity, so a utility agreement will add a year. He knows the civil engineer's first yield is always optimistic by ~5–10% (estimate) once detention ponds and lift stations are placed. None of this is in the package. Much of it is not in any document, and the parts that are sit across hearing minutes, staff reports, engineers' memos and his own deal files.

The outcome of each judgement is eventually known. The final plat shows the real lot count. The council vote, the continuances and the permit dates show the real entitlement path and timeline. The pay applications show the real cost per lot. Builder takedowns show whether the lots sold at the quoted price and pace. But nobody joins the prediction to the outcome. Land committees approve deals on the manager's numbers, the project is run by a different team, and two years later nobody asks how far the yield, the date or the cost moved, or why. When the manager retires or moves to a competitor, which in a cyclical business happens at every downturn, the developer keeps the closed deals and loses the read that found them.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection challenge is that the expert's read has to be captured at the moment of decision, from a person who is in a truck or a council chamber, not at a desk. A land manager's underwriting is a spreadsheet he built himself, a few notes in an email to the committee, and conversations. The considerations that drove his number (the creek buffer, the council's density habit, the sewer capacity) are rarely written as structured fields. Capturing them means asking a busy, commission-minded person to record reasons he does not think he needs, during the weeks when he is busiest.

The labelling challenge is that outcomes are noisy and confounded, and experts disagree with themselves. A rezoning that passed may have passed because the developer conceded density, which the yield label then absorbs. A delivery that slipped may have slipped because a dry utility was late, not because the entitlement read was wrong. Only tracts that were bought produce outcome labels, so the passed-over tracts (the counterfactual) are unobserved. The same manager will read the same council differently after a recent loss. Labels need to separate the components (yield, entitlement path, timeline, cost) and record what the developer chose to concede.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. The manager decides in days, sometimes on the drive back from a site visit, and a broker with three bidders will not wait. Any assistance has to be faster than the manager's own recall: a screening memo in minutes, a comparable-case list for the council in seconds. It also has to be honest about its error. A land committee that has been burned by an optimistic yield will not take a model's lot count without a range and a track record.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A deal ledger first and a model second. For every tract screened, the system drafts a structured underwriting record from the broker package and the manager's notes: lot count with its constraints, entitlement path (by-right, plat only, rezoning, planned development, district creation), expected hearing dates, utility source, horizontal cost per lot, and the manager's confidence in each. The manager confirms it in a minute rather than writing it. Each record is graded later against the final plat, the hearing record, the pay applications and the takedowns.

Around the ledger sit three assistants. A constraint screen reads public floodplain, wetlands inventory, lidar slope and soils layers and the zoning code, and proposes a yield range. A hearing-record model reads the jurisdiction's agendas, staff reports, votes and continuances, and estimates approval odds and months to approval for comparable cases. A cost model prices the horizontal scope from the developer's own pay applications. All three show their comparables, so the manager can overrule them and the override is recorded. Over time the ledger learns which managers' reads are calibrated, and on which components.

## Impact If Solved
A developer that knows how far its yields, dates and costs drift, and on which kinds of tracts, can bid land on evidence rather than on the most confident manager in the room. The stakes per deal are large. On a 300-lot tract, a 5% yield miss is 15 lots, ~$1.6M of lot revenue at Forestar's fiscal 2025 average of $108,400 a lot. A six-month entitlement slip on land carried at ~$5M costs ~$0.25M at a ~10% annual cost of capital (estimate). The judgement that finds good tracts and avoids bad councils survives a manager's departure, and builders and land bankers buying lots on option get a defensible delivery date, which is what their takedown schedules are priced on.
