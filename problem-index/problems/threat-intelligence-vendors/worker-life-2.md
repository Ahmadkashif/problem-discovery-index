# The Defender Whose Alert Queue Is Someone Else's Feed

**Industry:** [[threat-intelligence-vendors|Threat Intelligence Vendors]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Intelligence subscriptions generate the alerts, the security analyst investigates each one, and most are stale indicators matching ordinary traffic.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #graph-neural-networks #survival-analysis #confidence-intervals #change-point-detection #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation

## The Problem
A security operations analyst works a queue. A substantial share of it is generated by intelligence feeds matching indicators against the organisation's telemetry, and a substantial share of those are false positives — stale indicators, reassigned infrastructure, hashes of files that also appear legitimately, addresses belonging to shared hosting.

Each must be investigated, because the one that matters looks the same at first glance. The investigation is the same sequence every time: what matched, what is the asset, what was the traffic, is there corroborating activity, is the indicator current, has this matched before.

The volume is a direct consequence of purchasing decisions made elsewhere. More feeds means more matches means more queue, and the person working the queue had no input into which feeds were bought and cannot easily turn one off, because turning off a feed is a decision nobody wants to own.

Alert fatigue is the documented and predictable result. Analysts working high-volume low-precision queues become slower to treat any individual alert as real, which is a rational adaptation and is precisely the failure mode that lets a genuine incident through.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is the role that absorbs the imprecision of the entire security stack, and intelligence feeds are a large and growing contributor. The analyst carries the consequence of a miss and has no control over the volume.

The work is repetitive in a way that is corrosive given the stakes. Hundreds of near-identical investigations that resolve to nothing, punctuated rarely by one that matters, is a concentration problem that human attention handles badly and that the shift structure makes worse.

Career progression is also blocked by the queue. Analysts want to do detection engineering, threat hunting and investigation work, and the queue consumes the time, so the role has high turnover among exactly the people who would get good at it.

And nobody reports the cost back. The hours consumed by a given feed's false positives are computable and are not computed, so the purchasing decision is never revisited with the operational cost attached.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Attribute the queue to its sources. Alerts generated per feed, investigation hours consumed, and true positive rate per feed is a straightforward measurement that would let a security leader see what each subscription costs in analyst time — and would make turning off a feed a decision supported by evidence rather than one nobody wants to own.

Suppress the known-stale before it becomes an alert. Indicators that have decayed, infrastructure that has been reassigned and matches that have been investigated and dismissed before should not generate a fresh investigation, and all three are detectable.

Pre-assemble the investigation. What matched, asset context, traffic detail, corroborating activity, indicator provenance and prior dispositions are all retrievable automatically, and presenting them turns a twenty-minute investigation into a two-minute judgement.

Rank by the chain, not by the match. An indicator match on an internet-facing asset with corroborating behaviour is a different event from a match on a laptop browsing a shared-hosting address, and ranking the queue on the full exposure picture rather than on indicator severity puts attention where it belongs.

## Impact If Solved
Intelligence feeds generate a large share of a security operations queue and a large share of its false positives, and the cost is borne by analysts whose attrition is the sector's chronic problem. Per-feed cost attribution makes the purchasing decision accountable; stale suppression and pre-assembled investigations remove most of the manual work; and exposure-aware ranking addresses the alert fatigue that is the actual risk in a high-volume low-precision queue.
