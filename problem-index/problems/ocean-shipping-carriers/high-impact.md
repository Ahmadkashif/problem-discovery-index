# The Allocation Call Nobody Records

**Industry:** [[ocean-shipping-carriers|Ocean Shipping Carriers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A container line decides every week which cargo to accept, price and roll on a ship that sails full or not, and the trade managers' read of which bookings will really load is never written down or graded.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #logistic-regression #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A weekly Asia–North Europe loop puts a ship of 15,000–24,000 TEU (estimate) on the berth every Tuesday. Three to four weeks out, the trade desk for each origin region is allocated a share of the ship and starts accepting bookings: contract cargo from beneficial cargo owners at the annual rate, index-linked cargo, NVOCC allocations, and spot cargo at whatever the week's rate is. Nobody knows which of those bookings will turn up. Shippers book with two carriers and use one, forwarders hold space for customers who have not yet confirmed, and factory delays move containers to the next week. Maersk once put the global average at 20% no-shows and 35–40% on some trades (2011 statement). So the desk overbooks. When too much cargo does arrive, it decides whose containers to roll to the next sailing.

The people who make these calls know things they cannot write down. That this forwarder always books 40 boxes and delivers 25. That this retailer's bookings are firm in September and soft in January. That when a competitor blanks a sailing, spot bookings double two days later and half of them evaporate. That a booking made at a rate well below the week's market is the one most likely to be cancelled if the shipper finds space elsewhere, and the first to roll if the carrier is full. This is pattern recognition built over hundreds of sailings. It is tacit knowledge in the vault's sense: an experienced worker acting on patterns they cannot specify.

The outcome of every call is recorded precisely: containers gated in, loaded, rolled, revenue per slot, utilisation on each leg. The calls themselves are not. The overbooking percentage, the decision to hold space for a key account, the choice of which bookings to roll and the reasons all live in spreadsheets, chat threads and the weekly allocation meeting. When a trade manager retires or moves region, or when a new alliance network changes every port rotation at once, the carrier keeps the outcome history and loses the judgement that produced it.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's read has to be captured at the moment of decision, by people working against a sailing cut-off. Booking systems record the booking, the rate and the eventual status. They do not record the desk's private estimate of whether it will load, the overbooking target for the sailing, or why a particular customer's cargo was protected. Reconstructing those decisions after the fact from chat logs and allocation sheets is slow, and the reasons are rationalised. Capture has to be a by-product of a tool the desk already uses, or it will not happen.

The labelling problem is that "did the booking load on this sailing" is a noisy label for the quality of the read. A booking that fails because the factory missed a date is different from one the shipper moved to a cheaper carrier, and from one the carrier rolled itself. Experts also disagree with themselves: the same trade manager reads the same customer differently after a month of heavy no-shows, and different desks set overbooking from different instincts. Honest labels need the cause of each failure, which is rarely coded, and repeated judgements on a sample to measure each expert's consistency.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. The read must arrive inside the allocation meeting and the booking-acceptance screen, before the desk commits space, and it must beat the trade manager's own recall of the customer. Trade managers are judged on utilisation and revenue and are wary of a system that scores their overbooking calls. Liner revenue management has also been tried before as a central optimiser that regional desks overrode (recalled industry history), so a new tool has to start as advice the desk can see working, not as a replacement.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A decision ledger first and a model second. Every booking-acceptance, overbooking-target, protect and roll decision is logged with the sailing, the desk's estimate and a one-line reason drafted from the booking context and confirmed in a click. The ledger is graded automatically against what loaded, with the cause of each failure coded where the documents show it: shipper cancellation, cargo not ready, customs hold, carrier roll.

On top of the ledger, learn the desk's read. Model each booking's probability of loading on its sailing, and the expected shrinkage from booked to loaded quantity, from customer history, booking lead time, rate relative to the week's market, competitor capacity changes and the trade managers' own past annotations. Show the probability beside each booking and roll up a recommended overbooking level for each sailing, with the specific customers driving the uncertainty. Measure the desk against the model, and the model against the desk, every week.

## Impact If Solved
On a weekly service, one point of headhaul utilisation on a 20,000 TEU loop is 200 TEU a sailing. Over 52 sailings at ~$1,200 net revenue per TEU (estimate), that is ~$12M a year on a single loop (estimate). Every slot that sails empty because of a no-show, and every high-paying box rolled because the ship was oversold with low-paying bookings, is part of that. Keeping the trade managers' documented judgement through retirements and network changes turns the most repeated commercial decision in liner shipping from folklore into an asset the carrier owns. That matters most when, as in late 2026, the return to Red Sea routings releases capacity into a market already expected to be oversupplied.
