# Shipping Instructions to a Draft Bill of Lading

**Industry:** [[ocean-shipping-carriers|Ocean Shipping Carriers]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Generic document extraction reads invoices well, but turning a shipper's shipping instructions into a correct draft bill of lading needs carrier, cargo and trade-lane rules no off-the-shelf tool carries.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #automation #data-integration #quick-win

## The Problem
After a booking is confirmed, the shipper or its forwarder sends shipping instructions: shipper, consignee and notify parties, cargo description, HS codes, marks and numbers, container and seal numbers, weights including the SOLAS verified gross mass, freight terms, and the number of original bills required. Many arrive through the carrier's portal or INTTRA. A large share still arrive as emails, PDFs, scanned forms and spreadsheets (estimate). Documentation agents, often in a shared-service centre, key them into the carrier's system against the SI cut-off. A draft bill of lading goes back to the shipper, who corrects it, and the final B/L is issued. Each correction after issue is an amendment, which carriers typically charge for (~$50–150, estimate). A wrong consignee or cargo description can hold the box at destination customs.

## What Already Exists
General-purpose intelligent document processing from the cloud platforms and vendors such as ABBYY, Rossum and Hyperscience extracts fields from invoices and forms. LLMs read free-form emails well. Carriers' own portals and the DCSA booking and eBL standards give a clean target schema. Forwarder-side tools (WiseTech CargoWise, and AI vendors such as Reform, which extracts from bills of lading, commercial invoices and packing lists) automate the forwarder's half.

## The Customisation Gap
A draft B/L is not a set of extracted fields. It must be reconciled with the booking (equipment, routing, cut-offs), checked against cargo rules (dangerous goods declarations, reefer settings, out-of-gauge dimensions, sanctioned-party screening), and fitted to trade-lane requirements: advance manifest filing rules such as US 24-hour and ISF data, EU ICS2, and destination-specific consignee and tax-ID formats (recalled). Cargo descriptions have to be specific enough for customs and still match the commercial invoice. Shippers repeat the same lanes with small variations, so the most useful signal is the shipper's own history of what was corrected last time. None of this sits in a generic extraction model. It has to be layered on as carrier-specific validation, with an exception queue that tells the agent why a field is doubtful. Data ownership decides how this is trained. Booking requests and shipping instructions are the shipper's or forwarder's submissions; the US import manifest copy becomes public through CBP's AMS release (recalled), which is what [[niches/customs-brokers/import-export-transaction-data/profile|Import & Export Transaction Data Providers]] resell. Booking confirmations, draft and master bills and amendment history are the carrier's records, and house bills belong to the NVOCC that issues them. A vendor therefore trains per carrier under that carrier's service agreement, a model pooled across carriers needs each carrier's consent, and the demo runs on synthetic SIs and the public DCSA schemas.

## Impact If Solved
An agent that turns an SI email into a validated draft B/L in the carrier's system would cut keying time per bill and the share of bills amended after issue. With ~45 million bills a year industry-wide (search summary), even small rates matter: fewer amendment fees disputed, fewer customs holds, and documentation teams that work exceptions rather than retyping. It is also the cleanest two-week MVP in the industry, buildable on synthetic SIs and the public DCSA schema without carrier data.
