# The Festival Bid Nobody Grades

**Industry:** [[film-distributors|Film Distributors]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A distributor commits millions on one festival screening, based on an acquisitions executive's read of the film and the room, and never records that read in a form that can be checked against what the film earned in each window, so the firm's most expensive judgement is neither measured nor kept when the buyer leaves.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #bayesian-inference #transformers #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
An acquisitions team arrives at Sundance with a list of ~40 to 60 films (estimate) that sales agents have screened for them or that the programme has flagged. Over ten days they see as many as they can, most in public premieres with a paying audience, a few in private screenings arranged by the producer's rep. When a film plays, the senior buyer is reading three things at once: the film itself, the audience's response to it, and the market. Is this a film that opens in four theatres in New York and Los Angeles and expands on reviews, or a film that has to go wide on a trailer? Will it travel to France and Japan, or only to English-speaking territories? Does it have an awards path, and what will that campaign cost? What will a streamer pay for the second window once the theatrical run is known?

Within a day or two, sometimes overnight in a bidding war, the executive turns that read into a number: a minimum guarantee, a print-and-advertising commitment, a release plan and the territories and windows the deal must cover. Experienced buyers describe the decision in the vocabulary of craft rather than models. The question is "do we love the film?" and whether the audience in the room "played" the way a theatrical audience will. They remember how a comparable title did, which reviews moved it, and which markets it expanded into. The best of them can tell a festival-crowd film from a theatrical film in the first twenty minutes, and a film that will sell internationally from one that will not, and cannot say exactly how.

The outcome of every one of these bids is eventually recorded, but in pieces. Opening weekend and per-screen averages arrive within days. Premium VOD, subscription licence fees, international minimum guarantees, ad-supported revenue and television sales arrive over the next one to three years, on statements from dozens of licensees in different formats. No one at the distributor reassembles those pieces into "what did this film earn against what we thought it would earn when we bid, and why were we wrong?" The acquisitions executive moves to another company every few years, and the firm keeps the contracts and loses the judgement.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's read has to be captured at the moment it is formed, inside a festival schedule that leaves no time for it. The meaningful inputs are the buyer's own reaction scene by scene, the audience's laughter, walkouts and stillness, the comparables they reached for, and the release plan they had in mind. Most of these exist only in the buyer's head, a few notes typed in a cab, or an email to the CEO. A post-mortem written two years later is rationalised. A structured form filled in at the festival is a tax nobody will pay during a bidding war.

The labelling challenge is that a film's revenue is a noisy label for the quality of the bid. A good film can underperform because of a crowded release weekend, a bad trailer or a strike, and a weak one can be rescued by an awards run. Outcomes are also selected. The firm observes only the films it bought, so the films it passed on, which went on to gross for a competitor or to sink, are the counterfactual that the record never contains. Experts also disagree with themselves. The same buyer rates the same film differently at a 9 a.m. press screening and at a raucous midnight premiere, which is why audience context belongs in the record rather than being treated as noise.

The deployment challenge is speed and status. Help has to arrive during the festival, before the bid, and faster than the executive's own recall of comparables, or it will be ignored. And the people being modelled are senior, paid for their taste, and wary of a system that scores it. The first useful product is a memory and a comparison set, not a prediction.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A bid ledger first and a model second. For every film screened and every bid made or declined, the system keeps a short structured record drafted from the buyer's own voice notes and emails. It covers the release plan imagined, the comparables cited, the territory and window assumptions, the expected range of outcomes, and a note on the audience in the room. The buyer confirms the record in seconds. Each record is later joined to the statements and box office the film actually produced, window by window, including films the firm passed on wherever their performance is public.

On top of the ledger, learn the buyer's recurring reads. Pair screening context (audience response captured with permission, critics' early reactions, the programme slot) and film features (genre, runtime, cast, the film's own audio and visual content) with the buyer's annotations and the eventual ultimates. Then produce a calibrated range of outcomes by window, with the nearest comparables and an honest uncertainty band. The aim is not to replace the buyer. It is to make an experienced buyer's pattern recognition, with its track record, available to the next one and to the CFO signing the guarantee.

## Impact If Solved
A distributor that can say which kinds of films, audiences and release plans it systematically over- or under-bids can reprice its acquisitions on evidence. At a firm spending tens of millions a year on guarantees and P&A, a few points of pricing accuracy is worth more than any operational saving. Retaining a departed buyer's documented judgement turns the industry's most expensive recurring loss, the taste that walks out of the door, into an asset the firm keeps.
