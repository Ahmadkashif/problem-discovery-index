# Ten Hours for One Finished Hour

**Industry:** [[recording-studios|Recording Studios]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** An audiobook narrator is paid per finished hour, but a newer narrator spends about ten hours recording, editing, proofing and fixing pickups for each one, most of it alone, listening for their own mistakes.
**Tags:** #transformers #seq2seq #hidden-markov-models #cnns #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation

## The Problem
A narrator records a 9-hour book in a home booth or a production house's studio. Recording is the visible part. Then comes editing out retakes and mouth noise, proof-listening against the manuscript for missed words, added words and misreads, re-recording the pickups and matching their tone to the original, and mastering to the retailer's spec: on ACX, every file between -23 and -18 dB RMS, peaks below -3 dB and a noise floor below -60 dB. Backstage puts a newer narrator at about ten hours of work per finished hour and an experienced one at about six. A separate proofer, where the budget allows one, adds roughly 1.5 hours of listening per finished hour.

The pay doesn't move with the hours. The SAG-AFTRA minimum on ACX is $250 per finished hour, and many non-union and royalty-share deals pay less, so a narrator doing their own editing and proofing at ten hours per finished hour earns $25 an hour or less before equipment and booth costs (estimate). AI narration has made the rate harder to defend. Audible opened AI narration to publishers in May 2025.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The work that pays for the craft, the performance, is a fraction of the day; the rest is hours of listening to one's own voice for errors with headphones on. Fatigue produces missed misreads, which come back as pickups weeks later when the narrator has moved on to another book and has to recreate the same voice, mic position and energy. A file rejected for a noise floor problem means re-mastering a whole book.

Narrators are also the people most exposed to the claim that AI is good enough. The one way a human narrator stays competitive is to be faster and cleaner per finished hour, and right now that means working longer for the same rate.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A proofing and editing assistant that aligns the narration to the manuscript word by word, flags missed, added and misread words with the audio and text side by side, detects mouth clicks, breaths and room-tone breaks, and proposes edits the narrator approves with a keystroke. It builds the pickup list automatically, with the surrounding sentence played back so the narrator can match their earlier delivery, and checks every file against the retailer spec before upload. Pozotron already does part of the proofing step; the gap is the whole loop from record to spec-checked delivery in one place.

## Impact If Solved
Cutting post-production from roughly four extra hours per finished hour toward one or two would give a narrator on a 9-hour book twenty or more hours back (estimate). It raises their effective hourly rate on the same per-finished-hour fee, and it keeps human narration competitive on turnaround, which is where AI narration is aimed.
