# Credits and Royalty Directions That Never Leave the Room

**Industry:** [[recording-studios|Recording Studios]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Who played, sang, produced, mixed and engineered each track is known on the day of the session and lost by the day of release, and that record decides who gets paid by SoundExchange and the session-performer funds.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #bert #k-nearest-neighbors #data-integration #compliance #workflow-orchestration #revenue-impact

## The Problem
On a tracking day, the facts are plain: a session bassist, two backing vocalists, the producer, the recording engineer and an assistant. Weeks later a mix engineer and a mastering engineer touch the record. By release, the credits that reach the distributor are whatever the artist typed into an upload form, often just the artist's own name. The studio held the record for a moment and never wrote it down.

Those credits carry money. SoundExchange pays producers, mixers and engineers a share of digital performance royalties only when the featured artist files a Letter of Direction naming the recordings and the percentage, and the letter isn't retroactive unless it says so. Non-featured musicians and vocalists are paid through the AFM & SAG-AFTRA Fund, which keeps a list of credited performers it cannot locate and investigates claims from performers who were never credited. Engineers' careers also run on credits. Clients choose a mixer by the records they've worked on, and the largest dedicated credits database, Jaxsta, closed in November 2025.

## What Already Exists
Distributors' upload forms collect contributor roles in the DDEX format the streaming services accept. Muso.AI and Discogs hold credits for engineers and producers, partly verified and partly crowd-edited. SoundExchange runs its own registration and letter-of-direction forms. Studios use spreadsheets, Notes apps and, at best, a session sheet on paper.

## The Customisation Gap
What's missing is capture at the source. The booking already knows who was in the room. The session file knows which tracks were recorded on which day, and a union session report, where one is filed, knows who was paid scale. A studio-side record needs to join those into per-track credits with roles in DDEX vocabulary, ISRCs once assigned, and contact details for every contributor. It should generate draft Letters of Direction and producer agreements for the artist to sign, and flag performers who should register with the session-performer fund. Matching names to existing contributor identities (the same bassist spelt three ways) needs entity resolution, and the record must travel with the files to mix, master and distributor rather than be retyped at each step.

## Impact If Solved
Engineers and session players get the credits that build their careers and the royalties they're owed, with letters of direction filed at release rather than years later. A studio that hands every client a release-ready credits file has a concrete service to sell that a home studio doesn't offer.
