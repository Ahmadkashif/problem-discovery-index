# The Note That Says "Make It Warmer"

**Industry:** [[recording-studios|Recording Studios]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A mix engineer turns every vague client note into concrete moves by ear, and because the note, the move and the approval are never joined, the skill that decides how many revision rounds a mix takes is neither measured nor kept.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #transformers #contrastive-learning #large-language-models #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
A mix engineer receives a song as a folder of stems or a Pro Tools session: sixty to a hundred and fifty tracks (estimate), the producer's rough mix, and two or three reference tracks the artist loves. The first pass takes most of a day. Then the notes arrive, by email, text or timestamped comments in a review tool: "the vocal doesn't sit", "kick needs more punch", "it feels a bit dark", "the chorus feels small", "the rough had more vibe". A flat-fee mix usually includes two or three rounds of changes (estimate). Every round after that is either a fee the engineer is reluctant to charge or hours given away.

The notes describe how the listener feels, not what to change. "Warmer" could mean taking 2–5 kHz out of the vocal, adding low-mid weight to the mix bus, more tape saturation, or a darker reverb. "Punch" could mean the kick's transient, the bass's sustain or simply level. Engineer-facing guides say the same thing: every guess at what a vague note means costs a revision round. A senior mixer gets it right more often, and they can't fully say how. They know that this artist's "warmer" has always meant less vocal edge, that an A&R person's "make it pop" means the vocal up a decibel and a brighter top, and that "the rough had more vibe" means the artist is attached to the demo balance and the fix is to put it back, not to add anything. This is tacit knowledge in the full sense, built over thousands of notes and approvals.

The consequence lands on margin, repeat business and the pipeline of skill. Freelance mixes sell for ~$75–400 a song, so a mix that takes five rounds instead of two can lose money. Artists who feel misunderstood don't come back. And the way this skill used to pass on, an assistant sitting behind a senior mixer for years, is disappearing as large rooms close and mixing moves to home studios. A junior engineer meets "make it warmer" alone and learns by burning rounds.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's judgement has to be captured while they work, and the record is scattered. The moves are in the session: every fader, automation lane and plug-in setting is saved, but versions are saved over, plug-in parameters sit in proprietary state blocks, and nothing marks which changes answered which note. The notes are in email threads, text messages and half a dozen review tools. The link between them exists only in the engineer's head. Asking engineers to annotate their moves during a session is a tax nobody will pay while the client is waiting.

The labelling problem is that approval is a noisy label. Clients approve out of fatigue or because the release date has arrived; an A&R person overrules the artist; the "approved" mix is sometimes the second one with a single change. Engineers disagree with themselves too. The same note is answered differently on a Monday morning and at the end of a twelve-hour day with tired ears, and genre conventions shift what any word means. A model trained on raw approvals would learn deadline pressure as much as taste.

The deployment problem is speed, trust and ownership. A suggestion is useful only inside the session and only if it arrives faster than the engineer's own instinct, which for a senior mixer is seconds. Engineers are protective of their sound and wary of tools that push every mix toward the same curve, which is the reputation generic auto-mix tools have. And the session usually belongs to the artist's or label's project, so using it to train anything needs permission that nobody currently asks for.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A revision ledger first, a model second. Each round is captured automatically: the client's note with its timestamp in the song, a parameter-level diff of the session between bounces (which tracks, which plug-ins, how far), the new bounce, and the client's reply. The ledger lives as a DAW extension plus integrations with the review tools clients already use, and it asks the engineer for nothing more than a one-click "this change answers that note".

On top of the ledger, learn the engineer's translations. Pair the text of each note and the audio around its timestamp with the moves that eventually got approved, per engineer and per client, and surface them at the moment a new note arrives: "the last four times this artist said warmer, you cut 3 kHz on the lead vocal and pulled the bus compressor back; three were approved first time." Begin with retrieval from the engineer's own history, which they will trust, before any model proposes moves. Add a clarifier that turns an ambiguous note into two short A/B options for the client before the engineer opens the session. Studios that keep the ledger then hold something they've never had: an account of how each senior engineer works, which they can use to teach juniors.

## Impact If Solved
A mixer delivering ~150 songs a year who saves one revision round of ~2–3 hours per song gets back several hundred hours a year (estimate), and turns flat-fee mixes that lose money back into ones that pay. For a studio, a documented record of how its best engineers answer notes is the first way to teach the skill that keeps artists coming back, and to keep part of it when a freelancer moves to another room.
