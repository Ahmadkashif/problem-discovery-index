# The Assistant Engineer's Recall Night

**Industry:** [[recording-studios|Recording Studios]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** After a twelve-hour session, the assistant engineer photographs and writes down every knob on the console and the outboard rack so the mix can be rebuilt next week, and one missed setting costs the engineer an hour and the assistant their standing.
**Tags:** #cnns #object-detection #transfer-learning #feature-engineering #worker-facing #automation #quick-win

## The Problem
At a studio with an analog console and a rack of outboard compressors and equalisers, the mix exists only as the physical position of hundreds of controls. When the session ends, someone has to record it. That someone is the assistant engineer: photographing every channel strip and every unit, writing settings onto recall sheets, labelling patch-bay cables, and saving the DAW session alongside. Engineers describe about an hour to document a full console and outboard setup and 15–20 minutes to recall a complex mix, longer when a sheet is ambiguous. Even consoles with recall memory still require every changed control to be reset by hand.

The recall comes back when the artist's notes arrive. The assistant resets every knob from the photos while the engineer waits. Then the engineer listens and says something is different. It's usually one setting, a compressor's release or an EQ boost read wrong from a blurry photo, and finding it means comparing the new print against the old one by ear.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Assistant engineers are at the bottom of the ladder, often on low pay or day rates, working the longest hours in the building: in before the session to set up, out after it to document and clean down. Recall is the job's most tedious task and its most exposed. A mix that comes back wrong is noticed by the senior engineer, and an assistant's reputation for reliable recalls decides whether they're booked again and whether they ever get to engineer.

It also takes time from learning, which is the reason they took the job. The hour spent photographing knobs at 2 a.m. is an hour not spent watching how the engineer answered the artist's notes.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A phone or fixed camera that reads the console and outboard gear: it identifies each unit from a library of common models, detects the position of every knob, switch and fader, and writes a structured recall file tied to the session and song. On recall day, the assistant points the camera at the gear and sees, unit by unit, which controls differ from the stored state, highlighted on the live image, until there are no differences left. Patch-bay connections are captured the same way. The tool runs offline on a phone, because studios are often basements with no signal.

## Impact If Solved
Documenting a mix drops from about an hour to a few minutes of filming, and a recall from fifteen-plus minutes with a final search by ear to a guided pass that ends when nothing differs. That is several hours a week back for an assistant on a busy room (estimate). The task that most often damages an assistant's standing becomes one they can show is right.
