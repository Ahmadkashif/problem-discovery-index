# Selling Attention Nobody Can Measure

**Industry:** [[esports-organizations|Esports Organizations]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Almost all revenue comes from sponsors buying audience attention, that attention is spread across a broadcast, a dozen streams, several short-form platforms and social accounts, and the organisation cannot demonstrate any of it.
**Tags:** #causal-inference #bayesian-inference #confidence-intervals #cnns #gradient-boosting #hypothesis-testing #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact

## The Problem
An esports organisation's revenue is sponsorship, content and merchandise. The sponsorship is sold on reach: a jersey logo seen during a broadcast, a mention on a player's stream, a branded video, a social post, an activation at an event. The deck presented to a sponsor contains peak concurrent viewers, follower totals and impression estimates, assembled by hand from a dozen incompatible sources.

None of it measures what a sponsor is buying. Peak concurrents describe a moment, not a delivery. Follower counts are a stock accumulated over years. Logo exposure vendors report seconds of visibility, which is a measure of the broadcast's camera direction rather than of anyone noticing. And the outcome — whether the sponsorship produced awareness, consideration or sales — lands in the sponsor's own systems and is never returned in a form the organisation can learn from.

The fragmentation compounds it. Attention is distributed across a tournament broadcast the organisation does not control, player streams on three platforms with different metrics, clips that travel on short-form surfaces with no attribution back to the team, and social accounts belonging partly to players and partly to the organisation. Aggregating that into a single honest statement of delivered attention is genuinely difficult and nobody has done it.

The consequence became existential after the contraction. Sponsors who previously bought a growth narrative began asking for evidence, and the sector's inability to produce it contributed directly to the funding withdrawal, the organisational closures and the title exits of the last few years. Teams were unable to defend their value because they had never measured it.

## Why It's Unsolved
The measurement surfaces belong to other people. Twitch, YouTube and Kick each report their own metrics on their own terms; tournament broadcasts are run by publishers and organisers; short-form clips travel without attribution. An organisation trying to assemble a total is dependent on parties with no obligation to help.

The outcome side is the familiar problem of this whole segment. The sponsor observes the effect and does not return it, so the organisation cannot learn which activations worked, cannot price accordingly, and cannot improve. Brand measurement studies exist and are expensive relative to the deal sizes in this sector.

There was also, for several years, no commercial pressure to solve it. During the growth period sponsors were buying access to a young audience on a narrative basis, and better measurement could only reduce the number. The incentive to build it arrived at precisely the moment the money to build it left.

And the unit of attention is genuinely ambiguous. A viewer with a stream open on a second monitor, a clip watched for three seconds, a logo visible in a corner during a replay — these are not comparable and summing them produces a number that is impressive and meaningless, which is what the industry has been doing.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Build an honest attention ledger. Aggregate across every surface with a consistent unit — attention-seconds weighted by a defensible measure of prominence and likely notice — reported with its methodology and its uncertainty, and explicitly refuse to sum incomparable things. An organisation that reports a smaller, defensible number with a published method is in a better position with a sceptical sponsor than one reporting a large indefensible one, which is the opposite of the sector's instinct.

Measure delivery per activation, not per deal. A sponsor buys a package of placements; reporting what each placement actually delivered — this jersey exposure, this stream mention, this content piece, with the attention each accumulated — turns a renewal conversation into a specific discussion about which elements to keep.

Get outcome data back where it exists. Sponsors with trackable outcomes — a code, a link, an app install — can return them, and the organisations that have negotiated for this are in a materially stronger position at renewal. Pooling those returns across deals builds the first genuine evidence base about what esports sponsorship actually does.

Value the audience properly rather than by size. Esports audiences are demographically specific and highly engaged, which is a real and defensible proposition, and it is undersold by presenting raw reach against mass-media comparisons the sector will always lose. Composition and engagement depth are measurable and are the actual product.

## Impact If Solved
This is the whole business. The sector's contraction was driven substantially by an inability to answer a reasonable question about what sponsors were receiving, and organisations closed rather than because audiences left — the audiences are still there. An honest, methodologically published attention and delivery measure is the precondition for the category being investable again, and the first organisations to produce one will be the ones that keep their partners.
