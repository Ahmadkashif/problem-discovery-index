# The Developer Who Has to Be a Marketer

**Industry:** [[indie-game-studios|Indie Game Studios]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The commercial outcome depends on eighteen months of consistent marketing performed by someone who became a developer specifically because they did not want to do that.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #gradient-boosting #time-series-forecasting #bert #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Indie marketing is a long, public, continuous obligation: posting development updates, building a following before there is a game to show, maintaining a Discord, writing press pitches nobody answers, contacting creators, preparing festival submissions, making trailers, and appearing personally as the face of the project because personal presence is what performs.

It runs in parallel with making the game, for years, and it cannot be deferred — wishlists accumulate over time and a campaign started three months before launch has already failed. So the developer spends a portion of every week on work they are usually bad at, dislike, and cannot evaluate.

The evaluation gap is its own burden. Posts get a few dozen impressions, a trailer gets some views, wishlists move by an amount nobody can attribute. There is no way to tell whether the effort is working, which means no way to stop doing the parts that do not work, which means the anxiety never resolves.

And it is exposure. Building an audience means being visible, which means receiving whatever the internet sends, from a position of having your livelihood attached to the reception.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is a structural mismatch between what the job requires and who takes it. People become independent developers to make things, and the industry's economics require them to also be a consistent, personable, public content producer for two years. The result is a large population doing sustained work outside their competence, under commercial pressure, with no feedback.

The consequences are not evenly distributed. Developers who are comfortable being publicly visible do better commercially than those who are not, independently of the quality of the game — which means the industry's filter is partly on personality rather than on craft, and everybody involved can feel that.

The isolation makes it heavier. Small teams have no colleague to hand this to and no one to compare notes with about whether a quiet month is normal. And because the marketing is personal, a poor reception lands personally.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Make the effort legible. Attributing wishlist movement to specific activities — a trailer, a festival, a creator video, a post — is achievable with basic instrumentation and benchmarks, and it converts a formless obligation into a set of activities with known returns. Knowing that creator outreach produces most of the movement and that daily posting produces little is worth more than any amount of encouragement.

Automate the production. Development updates, press pitches, store copy variants and social posts assembled from the studio's own build notes, screenshots and footage leave the developer editing rather than composing, which is most of the volume and the part they find hardest.

Target the outreach. Which creators cover this genre, at what audience size, with what history of covering small titles, is a matching problem over public data and is currently a manual spreadsheet exercise performed badly.

Give them a plan with a cadence. The single most useful artefact is a calendar derived from what actually works for this genre and launch window — when to announce, when to release a demo, which festival to target, when the trailer matters — so the work is finite and scheduled rather than an open obligation competing with development every day.

## Impact If Solved
Marketing is the requirement that determines commercial survival and is performed by the people least suited to it, without evidence, for years. Attribution turns anxiety into decisions, automation removes the production load, and a genre-specific cadence makes the obligation bounded — which is the difference between a developer who ships their second game and one who does not.
