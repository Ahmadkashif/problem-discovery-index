# The Scout's Projection Nobody Grades

**Industry:** [[sports-teams|Sports Teams & Clubs]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A club spends its signing-bonus money on what an area scout saw in a teenager, and because nobody joins the scout's report to the player's career years later, the club cannot tell which scouts see something real or keep what they knew when they leave.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A Major League Baseball club employs an amateur scouting department of roughly 15–30 area scouts (estimate), each covering a territory of high schools, junior colleges and college programmes. Through the spring a scout sees a prospect several times, then files a report: present and future grades on the 20–80 scale for each tool, a fastball velocity band, a body and delivery description, a "makeup" read from talking to coaches and watching the player fail, and an overall future value. Cross-checkers and the scouting director then see the top players, and in July the draft room turns all of it into a board. A first-round bonus can run to several million dollars, and each club's draft bonus pool is ~$5–17M a year, with a separate international pool of ~$5–8M (estimates). NFL and college football staffs run the same loop against the April draft and the transfer portal.

What experienced scouts know best is the part least written down. That a pitcher's arm action will hold up when he adds 20 pounds. That a shortstop's "feel" for the barrel will carry to wood bats. That a catcher's leadership in a losing dugout predicts how he will handle Double-A. Scouts describe it as having seen a thousand players and knowing what this one looks like. It is tacit knowledge in the strict sense: pattern recognition built from thousands of looks that the scout cannot fully specify, and that a junior scout does not yet have.

The outcome of every one of these bets is eventually recorded in detail: minor-league stat lines, Statcast and Hawk-Eye measurements, debut dates, wins above replacement, arbitration salaries. The report that produced the bet is not graded against them. Reports sit in a scouting system as free text and grades, filed under inconsistent scales across scouts and years, and the club's R&D group builds draft models largely from performance and tracking data. When a scouting department is cut, or a veteran scout retires, the club keeps the reports and loses the scout's calibration: which of his "plus makeup" calls meant something and which were noise.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection challenge is that the expert's read must be captured while he is doing the task, in the stands, on a phone, between pitches. A report written that night is shorter and more rationalised than what the scout saw, and a report written for the director is shaped by what the director wants to hear. Video and tracking from amateur parks is patchy. TrackMan and Hawk-Eye cover many college stadiums, but high-school looks are often a radar gun and the scout's eyes. Linking a report to the exact pitches and swings it describes needs timestamps that nobody records today.

The labelling challenge is that outcomes are slow, selected and confounded. A player's career is known four to seven years after the report, only drafted and signed players produce professional outcomes, and the development system in between can make or ruin a prospect regardless of what the scout saw. Scouts also disagree with themselves: the same scout grades the same hitter differently after a good or bad look, and grades drift with the strength of the year's class. Honest labels need time-to-event outcomes, a counterfactual for players the club passed on (other clubs' picks are public), and repeated grading of the same player to measure each scout's own consistency.

The deployment challenge is speed and status. Help has to arrive at the field before the cross-checker books a flight, and in the draft room within the few minutes between picks, or it will be ignored. Scouting directors are paid for judgement and are wary of a system that scores their scouts. They will also resist a model that seems to replace the scout rather than show what each scout's read is worth.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A report ledger first, a model second. Scouts dictate notes at the park, and the system drafts a structured report from them, with grades, the specific observation behind each grade, and a stated confidence. Each report is linked to the video clips, pitch-tracking rows and game logs it refers to. Every report, including those on players the club did not draft, is later graded against time-to-debut and career value, using other clubs' picks as the comparison set.

On top of the ledger, learn each scout's recurring reads. Pair the free-text descriptions ("loose arm, projectable frame", "plays with an edge") with the measured traits and the outcomes, and estimate which phrases and grades from which scouts carry information beyond what tracking already shows. Surface that to the scouting director as each scout's calibrated track record, and to a new area scout as "the last six times your predecessor wrote this about a pitcher from this region". The goal is not to replace the look. It is to keep a veteran scout's pattern recognition, with its record attached, when he leaves.

## Impact If Solved
Across 30 MLB clubs the amateur and international bonus pools alone are ~$300–450M a year (estimate), and the bigger cost is the roster spots and development years spent on picks that never pay off. A club that can tell which scouts' projections carry information can move cross-checker visits and bonus dollars toward them. A few percentage points better hit rate on the top 10 rounds is worth tens of millions of dollars in surplus value per draft class (estimate). It also turns the department's most expensive recurring loss, a veteran scout's eye leaving with him, into something the club keeps.
