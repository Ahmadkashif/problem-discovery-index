# The Circulation Plan Built From a Broker's Memory

**Industry:** [[direct-mail-agencies|Direct Mail Agencies]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** The decision that sets a direct mail campaign's return — which lists and segments to mail, how deep and when — is made by a strategist's feel and graded by a matchback report that cannot tell mail's effect from buyers who would have come anyway, so the judgement is neither measured nor kept.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #causal-inference #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Every prospecting campaign starts with a circulation plan. A strategist at the agency, often working with an outside list broker, chooses from dozens of possible sources: response lists rented from other mailers, compiled lists, models built by a data cooperative from pooled transaction data, lapsed buyers or donors from the client's own house file, and carrier-route saturation for local offers. For each source they decide which selects to use (recency, dollar amount, product category), how many names to take, which offer and creative version each segment receives, and which week the pieces should land. A national cataloger, insurer or nonprofit may run this plan a dozen or more times a year, and a mid-size agency runs it for dozens of clients.

The best strategists carry knowledge they cannot fully state. That a particular rental list has been "over-mailed" this season and will soften. That a co-op model beats rentals down to the fourth decile for this client and loses below it. That a lapsed segment responds to a free-shipping offer but not to a percentage discount. That a February drop for this nonprofit always underperforms the model because of the client's own email calendar. They learn these patterns across hundreds of campaigns, the way a craftsman learns a material, and they apply them in the merge/purge priority order and the quantity column of a spreadsheet.

The outcome arrives late and blurred. Responses trickle in over 60–90 days, through phone, web, retail and marketplace channels, and are credited back to the mailing by matchback: the client's buyers are matched by name and address against the mail file, and anyone who was mailed and bought is counted. That credits mail with every customer who would have bought anyway, penalises sources whose buyers respond online without a key code, and says nothing about whether the twelfth decile of a model was worth its postage. When a strategist leaves, the agency keeps the spreadsheets and loses the reasoning that made them.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's reasoning is captured, if at all, as a final quantity and a merge/purge priority — not as the alternatives considered, the selects rejected, or the expectation held for each segment. Reconstructing why a strategist cut a list from 80,000 to 30,000 names means interviewing them, and the most useful judgements ("this list is tired") are made in a phone call with the broker that leaves no record. Any capture tool has to fit into a planning session already squeezed between the list due date and the client's approval.

The labelling problem is that the observed response is a noisy and biased label for the quality of the decision. Matchback credit overstates mail's effect by an amount that varies by source — high for house-file segments that would buy anyway, low for cold prospects — and only a holdout tells the difference, which most clients refuse to fund. Response also depends on things the strategist did not choose: the creative, the offer, the season, the client's digital spend, and postal delivery timing. And experts disagree with themselves: the same strategist rates the same list differently after a strong or weak recent campaign, so their historical decisions carry their own recency bias.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. Circulation plans are built in the days before the list due date, with brokers waiting for orders and list owners waiting for approval. A model that arrives after the order is placed is ignored, and one that recommends cutting a list a strategist trusts must show its track record on that client, in that strategist's terms, or it will be overridden without being read.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A circulation ledger first, and models second. Every campaign is recorded as a set of decisions — source, select, quantity, offer, version, in-home date, and the strategist's expected response for each cell — drafted automatically from the merge/purge report, the list orders and the planning spreadsheet, and confirmed by the strategist in minutes. Responses are joined at the cell level, and where clients allow even small holdouts (a few percent of each source), incremental response is estimated alongside matchback credit, with the gap between them reported as a standing correction factor by source type.

On top of the ledger, learn the strategist's recurring judgements and test them. Response and uplift models by segment estimate where each source's marginal names stop paying for their postage, and the strategist's own past calls — "tired list", "co-op beats rentals to decile four" — become features and are scored against what happened. The next planner on the account sees the recommended depth for each source, the evidence behind it and the original strategist's track record on similar calls.

## Impact If Solved
Postage and production make up most of a campaign's cost, so cutting the bottom deciles that do not pay back, and deepening the sources that do, moves campaign return directly — on a ~$36B annual direct mail market, a few points of better allocation is worth billions to mailers and decisive for the agency that can prove it. Keeping the strategist's documented judgement through a departure turns the agency's most fragile asset into one it owns.
