# The Technician's Read of a Failing Opening

**Industry:** [[locksmiths|Locksmiths]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A senior door technician looks at a commercial opening that won't latch and knows in seconds whether the closer, a hinge, the strike or the exit device is at fault, and brings the right part; most technicians can't, and that read is never recorded where the next technician could use it.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #object-detection #transfer-learning #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A school district's work-order system sends the locksmith a ticket: "Door 114B won't lock at night." That is all the information there is. The opening behind it is a set of parts that fail together. There is a hollow-metal door in a welded frame, three hinges, a mortise lock or a rim exit device, a surface closer, perhaps an electric strike or electrified trim wired to the access panel, and a fire label if the door sits in a rated wall. The same complaint has half a dozen causes. The closer's latch-speed valve may be closed down so the door drifts and stops short of the strike; a hardware supplier calls it the most commonly misadjusted valve on commercial doors, because "won't latch" gets read as a latch problem. The top hinge may have worn and let the door sag onto the strike lip. The frame may have spread. The exit device's latch may be worn, or its dogging left on. Or the electric strike may be fail-safe and unpowered.

An experienced technician tells these apart from a short look. They notice the rub mark on the head of the frame, the drift in the last six inches of swing, the shiny wear on the strike lip, and the gap that varies top to bottom. They arrive with a closer arm, a hinge or a strike already in the truck. A technician two years in arrives with a screwdriver, diagnoses on site and comes back two days later with the part. They may also fix the symptom, by filing the strike, and leave the cause. The door fails again in a month, and the callback is logged as a new job. Nobody counts it against the first visit.

The cost is not only the second truck roll. On a fire-rated opening, a field fix such as an oversized strike, a removed closer or a door propped to stop it slamming is itself a deficiency under NFPA 80. That standard has required an annual inspection of every fire door assembly since its 2007 edition, and CMS has enforced it in surveyed health facilities since July 2017. On an egress door, a lock that won't release is a life-safety failure. And on a multi-site contract, a vendor's first-visit fix rate is what the facility director remembers at renewal. The diagnostic skill that decides all of this lives in a few senior technicians per firm. In an industry where 74% of firms have fewer than five employees, it walks out the door when they retire.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's read happens in a glance and is never written down. The ticket says "won't lock"; the invoice says "adjusted closer, replaced strike, 1.5 hours." Nothing records what the technician saw that led to the closer, in what order they ruled things out, or which photo would have shown it. To capture it, the expert has to be recorded doing the task: a phone video or a few photos of each opening taken before work starts, with the technician's spoken reasoning. That has to cost seconds, on a ladder, often with gloves on. The outcome then has to be joined to it, and that means linking callbacks to the original opening, which most firms' job systems cannot do because they key jobs to the address, not the door.

The labelling challenge is that experts disagree, even with themselves. A sagging door with a closer that slams can be labelled hinge, closer or frame depending on which fix the technician favours. Several senior technicians will rate the same photo set differently, and the same technician rates it differently after a week of closer callbacks. Causes also chain: the slamming closer wore the hinge that sagged the door that rubbed the strike. Honest labels need a cause hierarchy, repeated annotation on a sample to measure each expert's consistency, and the outcome (no callback within 30 or 90 days) as the arbiter of which call was right.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. The technician already has an answer within seconds of walking up. A tool that takes longer than that, or that is wrong on the obvious cases, will be ignored. It has to work on a phone, offline in a basement stairwell. Its most valuable moment comes before the truck leaves the shop: telling the dispatcher, from the facility's own photo or the last visit's, which parts to load. Hardware identification is a hard vision problem in its own right. There are thousands of closer, lock and exit-device models across Allegion, ASSA ABLOY and dormakaba brands, many of them painted over or decades old.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An opening record first, and a model second. Every door a firm services gets a persistent record keyed to the opening rather than the address: photos, the hardware set identified from them (manufacturer, model, function, finish, fire label), and every visit, fault, fix and callback. Technicians build it as a side effect of work. The app asks for three photos and a ten-second voice note at the door and drafts the rest from the invoice lines.

On top of the record, learn the senior technician's read. A vision model detects and identifies the hardware and the visible wear signs: rub marks, hinge gaps, strike wear, closer arm geometry, missing labels. A ranking model takes those signs, the ticket text and the opening's history and proposes likely causes with the parts to bring. Each suggestion is graded by whether the job closed without a callback. Fire and egress rules run alongside, so a proposed fix that would create an NFPA 80 or egress deficiency is flagged before it is made. The aim is not to replace the senior technician. It is to make their read, with its track record, available to the junior on the next call and to the dispatcher loading the truck.

## Impact If Solved
Raising first-visit fix rates on commercial door calls from an estimated ~60–75% toward 90% removes a second truck roll on roughly one call in four. That is worth an estimated ~$100–$200 of unbilled labour and fuel each time, and the multi-site contract that follows at renewal. Keeping a departing senior technician's diagnostic record turns the trade's most expensive recurring loss, the experienced eye retiring, into something the firm keeps. It also gives facility clients something they have never had from a locksmith: evidence, opening by opening, that their fire and egress doors were fixed properly.
