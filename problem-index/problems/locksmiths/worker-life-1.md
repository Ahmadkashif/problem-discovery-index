# The Midnight Lockout and the Stranger at the Door

**Industry:** [[locksmiths|Locksmiths]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A mobile locksmith alone at 1 a.m. has to decide whether the person asking to be let into a house or car is entitled to it, collect payment from a stressed stranger, and stay safe, with nothing but a phone and their own instinct.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #logistic-regression #large-language-models #worker-facing #compliance #automation

## The Problem
Night lockouts are a large share of a mobile locksmith's calls and the most stressful ones. The caller is locked out of a house, an apartment or a car, often in a parking lot or on a street the technician doesn't know. The technician arrives alone. The person waiting may be the owner, a tenant whose name is not on anything yet, a partner in a domestic dispute who has been locked out deliberately, or someone trying to get into a place that isn't theirs. The ID is inside the locked house. The car registration is in the locked glovebox. The technician has to decide whether to open the door, and in licensing states they have to document that decision. California, for example, requires a signed work order with the requester's ID for vehicle and registered-property jobs.

Then there is the price. In an industry whose reputation is shaped by call centres that quote $19 on the phone and charge several times that on site, every honest technician gets treated as a suspected scammer when the bill arrives at midnight. Disputes, chargebacks and one-star reviews follow.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The risk is personal. A technician who opens a door for the wrong person can be named in a police report or a lawsuit and, in a licensing state, face a complaint against their licence. A technician who refuses a legitimate customer loses the job and gets an angry review. Being alone at night with a stranger who may be drunk, upset or hostile is a safety risk that the job's pay does not reflect. The median locksmith earned $48,380 in May 2023. Many locksmiths stop taking night calls altogether, giving up the best-paid work, because the judgement and the exposure fall entirely on them.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A pre-arrival and at-the-door assistant on the technician's phone. Before dispatch, it captures the caller's name, address and vehicle, checks the name against public property and vehicle records where lawful, and flags mismatches and the call patterns experienced dispatchers learn to distrust: a name not matching the address, or a request to open one specific unit. At the door, a guided capture takes the ID or proof-of-residence photo and the customer's signature on the quoted price before work begins, and files them with the work order. A check-in timer alerts the shop if the technician has not cleared the job. When there is no ID, it walks through the accepted alternatives (a neighbour, a utility bill retrieved after entry, police standby) and records which one was used.

## Impact If Solved
The technician stops carrying the whole decision alone. A documented, consistent verification step protects them in disputes and licence complaints, and a signed price ends most midnight billing arguments; price disputes and chargebacks could plausibly fall from ~3–5% of night jobs to under 1% (estimate). Night work is worth taking back. After-hours lockouts carry a premium of ~$50–$150 a job (estimate), so a solo operator who resumes ~3–5 night calls a week recovers roughly ~$650–$3,200 a month in premium alone (estimate), work many now turn down.
