# The Customer Nobody Can Vouch For

**Industry:** [[telecom-resellers|Telecom Resellers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** The decision to let a new account send voice traffic is made by an experienced onboarding or NOC engineer's read of a sign-up and a first day of calls, and the day that read is captured and scored is the day a wholesale or SIP-trunk provider stops learning about a bad customer from an FCC traceback.

## The Problem
A SIP-trunk or wholesale voice provider takes on new accounts every week: a 40-seat call centre, a healthcare reminder service, a "marketing agency" that wants 200 calls per second and a block of local numbers in 30 area codes, a foreign carrier wanting to terminate traffic into the US. Each one is revenue. Some are robocall operations that will send millions of calls with spoofed or rented caller IDs, collect complaints for a few weeks, and disappear. The onboarding specialist checks the business registration and the website, asks for a traffic description, and decides what limits to set. The NOC then watches the account's first days of traffic.

Experienced staff carry a read the forms don't capture. A website registered three weeks ago with stock photos. A request for caller IDs the customer can't show it owns. A traffic description that says "appointment reminders" when the first day's calls average 11 seconds, with almost none answered and dialled in sequential number blocks. An upstream foreign carrier that has supplied a bad account before under a different name. USTelecom's best practices say vetting depth should match the service granted and that a provider should deny or limit volume "when in doubt". Doubt is exactly what the experienced engineer has and the junior doesn't.

The consequence of a wrong read has changed. Every provider must answer a traceback within 24 hours. The Industry Traceback Group ran 3,606 tracebacks in 2024 and named 714 providers in call paths. A provider whose Robocall Mitigation Database certification is removed is cut off from every downstream network: the FCC removed more than 1,200 providers in August 2025 and another 14 in September 2026. A false or inaccurate RMD filing now costs $10,000 per violation. The provider's survival depends on a judgement held in two or three people's heads.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's judgement is made in conversation and in the head, not in a system. The onboarding record holds the documents the customer supplied and a pass/fail. It doesn't hold the reasons the reviewer hesitated, the limit they chose and why, or the account they compared it to. NOC decisions to throttle or terminate an account are made in chat and executed in a softswitch, and the reason is rarely logged. To capture the read you have to record the expert while they work: which signals they looked at, what they decided, and what they would have decided with less information.

The labelling problem is that the ground truth is sparse, late and attached to the wrong unit. A traceback arrives weeks after the calls, names the provider rather than the end account, and covers only the campaigns that someone reported and the ITG chose to trace. Most bad accounts are terminated for non-payment, complaints or carrier pressure without ever being traced, and some terminated accounts were legitimate customers with a bad week. Experts also disagree with themselves. The same reviewer approves a borderline lead-generation account in a slow month and rejects its twin when the last traceback is a week old. Labels need adjudication and an estimate of each reviewer's consistency.

The deployment problem is speed and incentive. An onboarding score that takes a day loses the customer to a competitor that activates in an hour. A traffic alert that fires after the campaign has run is a post-mortem. The model has to score at sign-up, before the first call, and again within the first minutes and hours of traffic, faster than the NOC engineer would notice. Sales teams paid on new revenue will route around any gate that blocks good accounts too often.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An account trust ledger first, a model second. Every onboarding decision is recorded with the evidence reviewed, the limits set (calls per second, caller-ID permissions, destinations), the reviewer's confidence and a one-line reason drafted from the review and confirmed in a click. Every later event joins the same record: traffic statistics by day, complaints, carrier notices, tracebacks, terminations and their causes. That record is also the evidence a provider needs for its RMD mitigation plan and for a 24-hour traceback response.

On top of the ledger, learn the experienced reviewer's read. A gradient-boosted risk model scores the sign-up from registration age, payment method, requested caller IDs and their provenance, stated use against requested capacity, and graph features linking the account to known bad accounts through shared contacts, IP addresses, payment instruments and upstream carriers. A time-to-event model then updates the risk as traffic arrives, using short-call ratios, answer rates, caller-ID churn, dialling patterns and destination spread. Accounts are released in graduated tiers: low-risk accounts get full capacity at once, borderline ones get capped capacity and a human review, and high-risk ones get the questions the experienced reviewer would have asked.

## Impact If Solved
A provider that can show who it onboarded, why, and what it did when traffic turned has the strongest available defence of its RMD certification. Removal ends the business, and the FCC has shown it will remove providers by the hundred. Catching a bad account in its first hours instead of after a traceback cuts the complaints, blocking and carrier disputes that follow it. Recording the reviewers' read turns the one person who "can smell a robocaller" into a documented, transferable control. For platforms that host thousands of white-label resellers, the same ledger scores each reseller's own customer base, which is where the next traceback will come from.
