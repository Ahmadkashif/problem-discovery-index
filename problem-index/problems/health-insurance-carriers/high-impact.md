# The Nurse Reviewer's Read of the Authorization Packet

**Industry:** [[health-insurance-carriers|Health Insurance Carriers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Experienced utilization management nurses know within minutes which requests are clearly approvable and which denials will collapse on appeal, but plans record only the final determination, so they cannot speed up the easy approvals or find the denials that do not hold.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #large-language-models #transformers #gradient-boosting #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #compliance #worker-facing

## The Problem
A prior authorization request reaches a health plan as a fax, a portal upload or, increasingly, an electronic transaction: an order, a few progress notes, imaging reports, a medication list and a form. A utilization management nurse opens it, finds the licensed criteria that apply (an MCG or InterQual guideline, the plan's own medical policy, or for Medicare Advantage the traditional Medicare coverage rules), and checks the record against each one. If the criteria are met, the nurse approves. If they are not, or the record is silent, the case goes to a medical director, who alone may deny. Concurrent review of inpatient and post-acute stays works the same way, daily: is another day in the hospital, the rehabilitation facility or the skilled nursing home medically necessary?

Medicare Advantage insurers alone made 52.8M of these determinations in 2024 and denied 4.1M (7.7%) in full or in part (KFF). The experienced reviewer's skill is not reading the criteria, which any new nurse can do. It is reading the packet. A seasoned reviewer knows that a physical therapy note documenting failed conservative treatment in a certain way almost always means the surgery request is sound. They know a referring practice's notes are thin but its requests are nearly always justified. And they know that a denial for "insufficient documentation" on this kind of case will be reversed the moment the surgeon's office sends the operative plan. None of this is written down. The system records the request, the criteria cited, the determination and the reviewer's ID.

The consequence runs both ways. Clearly approvable requests wait in the same queue as genuinely doubtful ones, which delays care and now risks missing the 72-hour and seven-day clocks CMS-0057-F imposed from January 2026. And denials that should never have been issued go out. Of the MA denials that were appealed in 2024, 80.7% were overturned. But only 11.5% of denials were appealed at all, so the plan has no idea how many of the other 88.5% would also have fallen.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the reviewer's reasoning has to be captured while they work through a long packet against a timer. Today the system captures a criteria checklist and a free-text note written to satisfy an auditor, not to explain the read. Which page decided the case, which missing element mattered and how confident the reviewer was are never recorded. Getting them means building capture into the review screen so that marking the deciding evidence is faster than not marking it.

The labelling challenge is that the obvious label is sparse and biased. Appeals happen on about one denial in nine, and who appeals is not random: large provider groups with revenue-cycle staff appeal, and frail members without help do not. Peer-to-peer reversals, resubmissions and downstream outcomes (was the member readmitted, did the condition worsen, was the service delivered anyway on a later request) are better labels but live in other systems. And experts disagree with themselves. The same nurse reads a borderline post-acute case differently on a Monday with a full queue than on a quiet Thursday. Any honest training set needs repeated review of a sample to measure that variance, and it will set the ceiling on agreement.

The deployment challenge is legal and practical. Regulators and plaintiffs are hostile to algorithm-driven denials. CMS requires MA plans to make individualised medical-necessity decisions. California's SB 1120 bars denials made by an algorithm alone. And class actions since 2023 have targeted model-assisted post-acute decisions. A tool is therefore only adoptable if it moves cases toward approval or toward a human, never toward an automatic denial. It must also be faster than the nurse's own read, or it will be clicked past.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An approval-side model and a denial-grading ledger. For each incoming request, language models extract the clinical facts each applicable criterion needs (diagnosis, duration and type of prior treatment, imaging findings, functional scores) and link each one to the page it came from. A second model, trained on the plan's history of reviewer determinations, scores the probability that an experienced reviewer would approve on sight. High-confidence approvals are issued with the evidence attached for a nurse to confirm in seconds or, where the plan's policy and state law allow, automatically. Everything else goes to a reviewer with the missing or contrary evidence highlighted, so the nurse reads the deciding pages first.

Behind it sits a ledger that joins every denial to what happened next: appeal, peer-to-peer reversal, resubmission and approval, the service delivered elsewhere, readmission. Each criterion, denial reason, reviewer and medical director is graded on how often their denials survive. Unappealed denials are weighted by modelled appeal propensity, so the plan learns where it denies care that would not have survived a challenge.

## Impact If Solved
If even a third of the ~48.7M approvals MA plans issue each year (KFF's count of fully favourable 2024 determinations) were confirmed in seconds rather than queued, review capacity would move to the doubtful cases and the CMS clocks would stop being a staffing crisis: ~16M requests a year, ~30% of MA volume, confirmed in seconds instead of fully reviewed (estimate). Grading denials against appeal outcomes would let a plan withdraw the criteria and reasons that reliably fail. That cuts provider abrasion and appeal workload, and it removes the denials most likely to become regulatory findings or lawsuits.
