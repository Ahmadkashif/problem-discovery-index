# The Photographer Policing Their Own Images

**Industry:** [[commercial-photographers|Commercial Photographers]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A self-employed commercial photographer spends evenings reverse-searching their own portfolio, working out which uses are licensed, and choosing between giving half the recovery to an enforcement service or chasing a stranger alone. Registering late quietly forfeits most of the remedy.
**Tags:** #contrastive-learning #k-nearest-neighbors #large-language-models #compliance #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A commercial photographer licenses a set of interiors to a hotel for its website and brochure. Eighteen months later the same images turn up on a booking aggregator, a furniture maker's catalogue, a contractor's Instagram, and a travel blog that took them from the hotel's site. Some of these may be legitimate (the hotel's licence may cover its booking partners), some are clearly not, and some fall in the grey area of parties who were on the project. Working out which is which means finding the original estimate, licence and correspondence. Then the photographer has to decide whether to email, send a formal demand, file with the Copyright Claims Board or hand the case to a service such as Pixsy, which takes 50% of whatever it recovers.

Registration decides the remedy. The Board pays up to $15,000 per work registered before the infringement or within three months of publication, and half that for unregistered works. Group registration of published photographs takes up to 750 images per application, but only if the photographer has kept up with it. Most haven't.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
It is unpaid evening work with an uncertain return, and it is personal: someone is profiting from work the photographer was paid little or nothing to license. Sending a demand to the wrong party, such as a client's partner who was in fact licensed, can damage a relationship worth far more than the claim, and enforcement services that send aggressive letters are routinely called trolls, a label that sticks to the photographer too. The choice between giving up half the recovery and doing it alone is a choice between money and time the photographer doesn't have.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A licence ledger that knows, image by image, who was granted what, where and until when, built from the photographer's own estimates, invoices and contracts. Monitoring that checks each match against that ledger before anything is flagged, so licensed and probably-licensed uses are filtered out. A registration assistant that batches new work into group registrations inside the three-month window. And a drafted, proportionate response for each true infringement — a friendly licence offer, a formal demand or a Board claim — with the evidence assembled.

## Impact If Solved
Hours of evening searching become a weekly review of a few true cases. Timely registration preserves the full remedy, and the photographer keeps the recoveries an agent would have halved, plausibly a few thousand dollars a year for an active commercial photographer (estimate), without hitting clients who had every right to the image.
