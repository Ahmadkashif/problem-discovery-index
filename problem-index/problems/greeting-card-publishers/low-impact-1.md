# Art Rights and Artist Royalties on Thousands of Small Agreements

**Industry:** [[greeting-card-publishers|Greeting Card Publishers]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Licensing platforms are built for a brand owner managing a few dozen licensees; a card publisher is the licensee of thousands of small artworks, each with its own fee, term, territory and list of products it may appear on.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #compliance #automation #data-integration

## The Problem
A card publisher acquires art two ways. It buys designs outright for a flat fee, which the Greeting Card Association puts at roughly $275–$500, with ownership usually passing to the publisher. Or it licenses them for a set term, use and territory, typically paying an advance of $150–$300 against a 4–6% royalty while the artist keeps ownership. Large character properties come on separate licences with minimum guarantees and approval rights. A mid-size publisher accumulates thousands of these agreements over the years, written on several template versions and amended by email.

Every downstream decision depends on them. Can this floral design move from a card to gift wrap and a gift bag? Can it run in the UK? Does the licence expire before the Christmas line ships? Is the artist owed royalty on scanned sales, on shipments, or on net shipments after seasonal returns? Licensing managers answer these questions by opening the contract, and royalty statements are often built in spreadsheets each quarter.

## What Already Exists
Brand licensing platforms such as Flowhaven (built on Salesforce) and RoyaltyZone manage agreements, design approvals and royalty reporting. Rights management systems such as Rightsline serve film and TV distributors. Contract lifecycle tools (Ironclad, DocuSign CLM) store and search contracts. Publishers' ERP and PLM systems hold SKUs and sales.

## The Customisation Gap
The licensing platforms assume the user is the licensor, tracking a modest number of high-value deals and validating the licensees' reports. A card publisher is on the other side of thousands of low-value agreements, and its questions are per design and per format. It needs: rights extraction from old agreements into a design-level grid (formats, territories, term, exclusivity, sublicensing to gift wrap or digital partners); royalty bases that reflect scan-based trading, where revenue is recognised at the register and shrink and seasonal take-backs reduce the base; flat-fee designs flagged as owned so they are never mistakenly reported; expiring licences surfaced against the line calendar before a design is planned into a season it cannot ship in; and artist statements generated per design from pocket sales. None of this is exotic, but none of it ships out of the box.

## Impact If Solved
Licensing staff stop reading contracts to answer format questions, artists receive accurate statements on time (which matters for keeping the best freelancers submitting), and the publisher stops either leaving owned art unused on new products because nobody was sure of the rights, or using art it no longer has the right to use.
