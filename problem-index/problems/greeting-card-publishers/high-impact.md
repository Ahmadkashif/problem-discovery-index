# The Editor's Eye for a Pocket Winner

**Industry:** [[greeting-card-publishers|Greeting Card Publishers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Experienced card editors can look at twenty candidate designs for "Birthday — Sister" and pick the two that will outsell the card already in the pocket, and the publisher records neither their picks, their reasons, nor whether they were right.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #transformers #contrastive-learning #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
Every pocket on a card fixture is a slot defined by occasion, recipient and tone: Birthday — Wife — Sentimental, Sympathy — Loss of Pet, Anniversary — Husband — Humor. Each season a publisher's line planners decide which pockets to refresh, the creative team produces candidates for each one, and a review meeting of editors, art directors and product managers chooses which go to print. A large publisher runs this cycle across thousands of pockets a year; Hallmark's creative staff of about 900 produces more than 19,000 new or redesigned cards and related products annually. Most of those decisions rest on the judgement of a handful of experienced people.

What those people know is hard to write down. A senior editor can read a caption and know it is "too clever" for the buyer of that pocket, who is usually a woman choosing for someone she has known for decades. An art director can see that a design is on-trend in a way that will look dated by the time it ships. A line planner knows that a gentle humour card works in a Midwestern grocery and dies in a Manhattan drugstore, that a foil accent lifts a sympathy card but cheapens a teen birthday card, and that a caption naming the recipient ("For a Wonderful Granddaughter") sells better on the front than inside. They describe it as "a feel for the rack". It is pattern recognition built over years of watching designs succeed and fail.

The outcome of every one of these choices is measured precisely. Under scan-based trading the publisher sees units sold by pocket, week and store, often within days. But the decision itself is not stored in any form that can be joined to that outcome. The candidates that were rejected, the reasons a winner was chosen, and the editor's own confidence go unrecorded, so nobody can tell whether the review meeting adds value over picking at random from the creative team's shortlist, which editors are reliably right about which occasions, or what a retiring editor knew that her successor does not.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection problem is that the expert's judgement has to be captured at the review table, where the work happens quickly and socially. Editors pass over dozens of candidates in minutes and rarely articulate why; a form asking for reasons after the meeting gets rationalisations or nothing. Rejected designs are often never finished or filed, so the counterfactual (what would have sold) is missing for exactly the designs the experts turned down.

The labelling problem is that sell-through is a noisy label for design quality. A card's weekly sales depend on its position on the fixture (eye level versus bottom row), its neighbours, the store's shopper mix, shrink, out-of-stocks the merchandiser did not fix, and the season. Two experts disagree about which of two cards is better, and the same expert ranks the same pair differently a month later, so any model must be measured against the experts' own test-retest agreement rather than an imagined perfect label. Pocket-level history is also selected: only chosen designs generate sales.

The deployment problem is speed and culture. A review meeting moves faster than any system that needs a design fully finished, scanned and tagged before it can score it, so the model has to work on sketches and draft captions. Creative staff are wary of a tool that ranks their work, and an editor who has been right for twenty years will ignore a score that cannot explain itself in her own terms. And the useful answer is store-cluster specific, because a single national "will it sell" score hides the regional and demographic differences that experienced planners exploit.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start with a selection ledger. Every review meeting records the pocket brief, all candidates (including sketches that were rejected), each reviewer's quick pick and confidence, and a one-line reason chosen from a short vocabulary the editors define themselves ("too wordy", "wrong age", "trend fading"). A tablet at the table makes this a tap per design, not a form.

On top of the ledger, train a model that predicts pocket-level sell-through relative to the incumbent card, by store cluster, from the design image, the caption text, the finish and the price point, using image and text embeddings tuned on the publisher's own history. Use the editors' recorded picks as a second target, so the model learns what experienced reviewers see before the sales data arrives. In the meeting it shows, for each candidate, a predicted index against the incumbent, the most similar past designs and how they sold, and where the model disagrees with the room. After launch, every chosen design is graded against its prediction and its reviewers' confidence, so the publisher learns which judgements carry information.

## Impact If Solved
A publisher that can say which candidates, reviewers and design attributes predict sell-through can refresh pockets with fewer misses, which matters directly under scan-based trading where an unsold card is the publisher's inventory and a weak card wastes a pocket for a full cycle. Improving the hit rate of new designs by even a few percentage points across thousands of pockets moves department sales per linear foot, the number retailers use at contract renewal. Keeping a senior editor's documented eye after she retires turns the industry's most valuable and least transferable skill into something the firm owns.
