# The Front Desk Phone Queue

**Industry:** [[physician-practices|Physician Practices]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A receptionist paid ~$18 an hour answers the phone, checks patients in, verifies insurance and reschedules the day around cancellations, all at once, while the hold queue grows and every unanswered call is a patient lost.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #gradient-boosting #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
A four-physician practice's front desk takes a few hundred calls a day (estimate): appointment requests, reschedules, "is my referral in?", refill questions, billing questions, test results, and the patient calling to say she is running late. The same two or three people check in every arriving patient, scan insurance cards, collect copays, confirm demographics and answer questions at the window. Between 8 and 10 a.m. and after lunch, the phone queue overflows. Calls go to voicemail, which someone has to work through later, and patients who cannot get through book elsewhere.

BLS counts 174,460 receptionists and information clerks in physicians' offices, at a mean wage of $18.20 an hour ($37,860 a year), alongside 241,900 medical secretaries (OEWS, May 2023). They are among the lowest-paid people in the building and the first voice every patient hears.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The job is split attention all day. A receptionist is mid-sentence with a patient at the window when three lines light up, and whichever they choose, someone is annoyed. They absorb patients' frustration about things they do not control: authorization delays, billing errors, a physician running an hour late. The scheduling rules live in their heads: which physician sees which visit types, which slots are held for new patients or procedures, which payers need a referral on file. A mistake becomes a no-show, a denied claim or an angry physician. Turnover is high (estimate), and every departure takes those rules with it.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A voice and text agent answers the routine half of the calls: booking, rescheduling and cancelling against the practice's real template rules, answering referral-status and location questions, taking refill requests into a structured queue, and confirming insurance before the visit. It hands the call to a person, with a summary on screen, when the patient is upset, clinically worried or the request falls outside the rules. The receptionist works the window and the handoffs instead of the hold queue, and the agent learns the scheduling rules that were in the receptionist's head.

## Impact If Solved
If an agent resolved ~50% of inbound calls without a person (estimate), each receptionist would get back ~3 hours a day of phone time. Practices would stop losing the ~10%–20% of new-patient calls that today end in voicemail or abandonment (estimate). The receptionist's job becomes the part that needs a human: the patient at the window and the call that is not routine.
