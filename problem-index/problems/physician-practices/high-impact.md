# The Physician's Sense That This Patient Is Sicker Than They Look

**Industry:** [[physician-practices|Physician Practices]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced primary care physician acts on a feeling that a routine-looking patient is in trouble, and the practice never records that feeling, never learns which ones were right, and loses it when the physician leaves.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #transformers #survival-analysis #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A family physician sees ~20–25 patients a day (estimate), most of them for problems that will resolve on their own: a cough, back pain, fatigue, a headache, a stomach that has been off for two weeks. Somewhere in that list is the patient whose fatigue is leukaemia, whose headache is a bleed, whose indigestion is cardiac. An experienced physician often senses it before any test does. The patient describes the pain a little differently, looks a shade greyer than last time, or has come back twice in a month. The physician cannot say why, but does something different: orders the CT, books a recheck in a week instead of "come back if it gets worse," calls the patient at the weekend, or gives exact instructions about when to go to the emergency department.

General practice researchers call this the "sense of alarm", and clinicians call it gut feeling. It is tacit knowledge in the craftsman's sense: pattern recognition built across thousands of visits that the expert cannot fully specify. It is also where outpatient care fails most expensively. About 5.08% of US adults, roughly 12 million a year, experience an outpatient diagnostic error, and about half of those errors could cause harm (Singh, Meyer and Thomas, BMJ Quality & Safety, 2014). The studies behind that estimate found the errors with electronic triggers: an unplanned hospitalisation or an unscheduled return visit within 14 days of a primary care visit, confirmed by chart review.

The practice records almost none of this. The note says "fatigue, labs ordered, return in one week." It does not say the physician was worried, or why. The outcome is recorded somewhere else: an emergency admission at the hospital across town, a cancer diagnosis by a specialist months later. Usually nobody tells the practice. When the physician retires or moves groups, which happens more often now that 82% of physicians are employees of hospitals or corporate entities (PAI–Avalere, January 2026), the panel is handed to someone who never learned which of those patients worried the last doctor.

The hospital's version of the same judgement, the bedside nurse's read of deterioration, is [[problems/general-hospitals/high-impact|The Nurse's Read of a Patient Going Downhill]] in [[general-hospitals|General Hospitals]]. That one is read hour by hour from flowsheet behaviour on a monitored ward, while this one is formed once in a fifteen-minute visit and graded by outcomes weeks later in another organisation's records.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection challenge is that the expert's alarm is never written down as a judgement. It surfaces only in behaviour: the test ordered, the shortened follow-up interval, the extra minutes spent, the safety-netting sentence said aloud but rarely charted. Ambient scribes now capture the visit conversation itself, which is the first record of the hedged language ("I don't love this", "let's not wait on it") that carries the signal. But ambient notes are templated, and the summary often strips the hedge. Capturing the expert means keeping the transcript features and the ordering behaviour, not just the polished note. The outcomes needed to grade the alarm sit outside the practice, in hospital records, claims and registries, and must be joined back to the visit.

The labelling challenge is twofold. First, the physician's action prevents the outcome that would have labelled it. The worried physician who orders the scan catches the tumour early, and the record looks like an ordinary work-up, not a save. Escalated patients who did well look like false alarms. Second, experts disagree with themselves. The same physician is more cautious after a recent miss, less cautious late on a Friday with a full waiting room, and different with patients they know well. The labels must be outcome-based (unplanned emergency visit or admission within days, delayed diagnosis of a cancer or a vascular event) rather than expert ratings, and analysed against similar patients who were not escalated.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. A visit lasts fifteen minutes, and the physician forms the impression in the first two. A tool that flags risk after the note is signed is useless, and one that flags 20% of patients will be ignored by the second week. It must arrive during the visit or before it, as a quiet signal with a reason. It must be quieter than the physician's own instinct on the easy cases and earlier on the hard ones. It must not teach newer clinicians to stop forming their own impression.

## What a Solution Looks Like
First, a ledger. Each visit gets a structured record of what the physician did that departed from routine (an urgent order, a short recheck, an after-hours call, a red-flag phrase in the transcript), drafted automatically from orders, scheduling and the ambient transcript, and confirmed by the physician in one tap if they want to add "I was worried." Each record is graded later against outcome triggers drawn from claims and health information exchange feeds: emergency visits and admissions within 14 days (the window Singh's triggers used), new serious diagnoses within a year, and repeated return visits.

Second, a model trained on that ledger. Learn from experienced physicians' escalation patterns, joined to outcomes, which combinations of history, presentation, prior utilisation and transcript language precede a serious event. Surface the signal to every clinician on the panel before the visit ("this patient has three visits for the same complaint in five weeks and a rising haemoglobin trend"), and to the physician inheriting a departing colleague's patients. The goal is not to replace the read but to give every clinician the experienced physician's pattern library, with its track record.

## Impact If Solved
If a group cut its harmful diagnostic delays by even a fifth, it would affect ~1% of its adult panel each year (derived from Singh's 5% rate, half harmful). For a 30-physician primary care group with ~60,000 adult patients (estimate), that is several hundred patients a year diagnosed sooner. It would also mean fewer emergency admissions that cost a value-based contract ~$10,000–$15,000 each (estimate) and fewer diagnosis-related malpractice claims. The practice would also keep its best clinicians' judgement when they leave, instead of losing it with every departure.
