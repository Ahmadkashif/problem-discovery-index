# The Result That Doesn't Fit the Patient

**Industry:** [[clinical-laboratories|Clinical Laboratories]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every held result is judged real or spurious by a technologist in seconds, and that read of IV contamination, mislabelled tubes and damaged specimens decides whether a patient is treated for a number that was never theirs, yet it is recorded only as "released" or "recollect".
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #feature-engineering #transfer-learning #evaluation-metrics #conditional-probability-and-bayes-theorem #worker-facing #automation

## The Problem
A large core lab runs tens of thousands of chemistry and haematology results a day. Middleware releases most of them automatically against rules the lab wrote: within reference range, passes a delta check against the patient's previous result, no instrument flag. The rest land in a held queue in front of a medical laboratory scientist, who has to decide, result by result, whether to release, rerun, add a comment, call a critical value or ask for a new specimen.

The experienced technologist reads patterns the rules cannot express. A glucose of 900 mg/dL with chloride up and calcium, potassium and protein all diluted means the specimen was drawn above a dextrose drip, not that the patient is in crisis. A potassium of 7.5 with a calcium near zero means EDTA from a purple-top tube got into the chemistry sample. A high potassium with a low glucose on an outreach specimen means it sat unspun in a courier van all afternoon. A haemoglobin that has jumped three grams overnight in a patient with no transfusion may be someone else's blood in the tube. Senior staff "just know" these signatures from years of seeing them and from the phone calls that followed when they were wrong. A newer technologist on a short-staffed night shift releases the dextrose result, or phones it as a critical value to a nurse who gives insulin.

That judgement is never written down as a reason. The laboratory information system records the action — released, repeated, cancelled with "specimen contaminated" free text, recollection requested — and almost never links the held result to what the recollected specimen showed. The lab therefore cannot say how often its held results are spurious, which technologists catch what, which wards and draw practices produce contamination, or whether its delta-check thresholds are catching the right things. Pre-analytical problems are the largest source of lab error (~60–70% in the literature, estimate), and they are exactly the problems the instrument cannot see. [[testing-laboratories|Testing Laboratories]] have a similar release moment for water, food and product samples. Here the sample is a patient's blood and a wrong release is acted on at the bedside within the hour.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's read happens in seconds at the bench, and the evidence that would grade it arrives later and elsewhere. To learn the read, a lab has to capture each held result with its full context — the panel, prior results, draw time and location, collector, tube types, order of tests, IV orders on the medication record — at the moment of decision, and then join it to the recollected specimen, the corrected report or the clinical event that settled what was true. In most labs those records sit in the LIS, the middleware, the EHR and a free-text comment field, and nothing joins them.

The labelling problem is that experts disagree with themselves and the truth is often never observed. Contamination is a matter of degree: a 5% dextrose admixture changes results less than a 20% one, and the same technologist releases a borderline case on a quiet afternoon and rejects it at 3 a.m. Many spurious results are never confirmed because no one recollects. The published work shows the consequence. An ensemble pipeline from Washington University, ARUP and Yale detected IV-fluid contamination in basic metabolic panels with 0.858 sensitivity and 0.993 specificity internally. At the second hospital sensitivity held but the Matthews correlation coefficient fell from 0.747 to 0.387 as false flags rose (Clin Chem 2025). The authors trained partly on simulated contamination because real labels were too scarce, and different patients, fluids and draw practices moved the base rate.

The deployment problem is speed and accountability. The model must score a held result before the technologist has finished reading it, inside middleware the lab may have to revalidate, and it must lower false flags rather than add to the queue. A tool that holds more results than the current rules, or that the lab director cannot explain to a CAP inspector, will be switched off. Every lab's contamination signature is local, so the model has to adapt to each site without a large labelled set from it.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A held-result ledger first, a model second. Every held result is captured with its context and the technologist's action, and a one-click reason ("dextrose contamination", "EDTA", "haemolysed", "WBIT suspected", "real") replaces free text. A background job joins each held result to whatever settled it: the recollection, a corrected report, a transfusion record, a repeat on the next draw. That ledger alone tells the lab director which wards contaminate most, how often each rule fires on real results, and where technologists disagree.

On top of the ledger, train a model of the senior technologist's read. It gives multi-label probabilities for IV contamination by fluid type, EDTA, delayed separation, haemolysis interference and wrong blood in tube, estimates severity, and shows the analytes that drove the score. Start from a general model built on pooled patterns and simulated admixtures, then calibrate it to each site with the site's own ledger. Deploy it first as a ranked queue and a suggested comment, never as an auto-release. Graduate to auto-release only for categories where its precision beats the lab's best technologists on the ledger.

## Impact If Solved
A lab that can score held results reliably releases genuine results faster and stops phoning spurious critical values, so fewer patients are treated for a dextrose drip or EDTA. It recollects the right specimens the first time, and newer technologists work with the judgement of the most experienced one on every shift. On a core lab holding ~5% of 20,000 daily results (estimate), cutting review time from ~60 to ~25 seconds per held result returns about 10 technologist-hours a day. That matters in a workforce where vacancies remain above pre-pandemic levels. The ward-level contamination report is the first measurable view hospitals have had of a patient-safety problem that today goes uncounted.
