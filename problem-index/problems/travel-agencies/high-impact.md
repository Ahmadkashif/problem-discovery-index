# The Match Nobody Records

**Industry:** [[travel-agencies|Travel Agencies]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced advisor hears twenty minutes of a client talking and knows which resort, ship, cabin and ground operator will work, yet that judgement and its outcome are never recorded together, so the agency can neither measure it nor keep it when the advisor leaves.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #large-language-models #transformers #gradient-boosting #word-embeddings #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A couple calls a luxury advisor about a tenth-anniversary trip. They want "somewhere quiet but not boring", they hated a big all-inclusive in Cancún, he gets seasick, she wants a spa that isn't an afterthought. Within the call the advisor has ruled out three islands, picked two properties and a villa category, decided which of the two general managers will actually deliver the consortium amenity, and knows which transfer company to avoid at that airport after last spring. None of that reasoning is in the CRM. What is recorded is the booking: property, dates, rate, commission.

This is tacit knowledge in the strict sense. Senior advisors describe it as "knowing the client" and "knowing the product", built from hundreds of trips, site inspections, fam trips and post-trip phone calls. They read signals a questionnaire misses: the client who says "budget isn't an issue" but flinches at the deposit, the family whose teenager will decide whether the trip was a success, the "adventurous" traveller who means a guided walk, not a trek. They also hold a private ledger of supplier reliability — which hotels honour upgrades for agency bookings, which river-cruise line handles a medical disembarkation well, which destination management company answers at 2 a.m.

The outcome of every one of these judgements exists somewhere. The client rebooked or didn't; complained or sent a thank-you; the upgrade was honoured or the advisor spent a day chasing it; the client moved to another advisor or started booking direct. But the outcome is scattered across texts, emails, supplier portals and memory, and it is never joined to the recommendation that produced it. When an advisor retires or moves to another host agency — and independent contractors usually take their client books with them — the agency or host keeps the booking history and loses the judgement that made it profitable. New advisors, who enter mostly as part-time independent contractors, have no way to borrow it.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's reasoning has to be captured while the expert performs the task: on a discovery call, in a quick text exchange, during the hour spent narrowing options. Advisors are paid on commission, often months later, and every minute spent writing notes is unpaid. CRMs and itinerary tools are built to store bookings and produce proposals, not to record why option B was chosen over option A, and an advisor writing a rationale after the fact rationalises rather than records.

The labelling challenge is that trip outcomes are noisy and slow. A wonderful match can be ruined by a hurricane or a cancelled flight; a mediocre one can be rescued by good weather and a charming waiter. Satisfaction is partly a function of expectations the advisor set. Experts disagree with themselves — the same advisor will steer a similar client differently after a bad experience with a property — and different advisors in the same consortium hold contradictory views of the same supplier. Labels have to be built from several weak signals (repeat booking, complaint, amenity confirmation, post-trip rating) and their reliability measured, not assumed.

The deployment challenge is speed and ownership. Any assistance must surface during the call or while the proposal is being built, faster than the advisor's own recall, or it will be ignored. And the data is contested: independent advisors see their client knowledge as their business, and hosts that try to centralise it risk losing the advisors they want to help.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A trip ledger first and a model second. Every proposal is captured with the options offered, the option chosen and a short structured rationale — drafted automatically from the call transcript, messages and the advisor's notes, and confirmed with one tap rather than written. After travel, the ledger collects outcome signals without effort from the advisor: supplier confirmation of amenities, a two-question post-trip check-in, complaint and refund records, and whether the client books again within a year.

On top of the ledger, learn the expert's fit judgement. Pair client descriptions and stated preferences with the options senior advisors offered and the outcomes that followed, and train a ranking model that proposes a shortlist the way a senior advisor would, with the reason attached ("similar clients who disliked large resorts rated this property highly; the spa is the deciding factor"). Separately, build a supplier reliability record from outcome signals across many advisors, so a new advisor knows which property honours agency amenities before the client arrives. The aim is not to replace the advisor's read but to make an experienced advisor's pattern recognition — with its track record — available to the junior and to the successor.

## Impact If Solved
A host or consortium that can show which advisor recommendations and which suppliers produce repeat clients can train new advisors in months rather than years, and the cost of the early years shows up in attrition (~half of new hosted advisors leave within 12-18 months, estimate). For a luxury agency, raising the share of clients who rebook by even a few points on trips with ~$1,500-5,000 in commission each (estimate) is worth more than any efficiency gain, and keeping a retiring advisor's documented judgement turns the industry's most expensive recurring loss — the book walking out the door — into something the agency keeps.
