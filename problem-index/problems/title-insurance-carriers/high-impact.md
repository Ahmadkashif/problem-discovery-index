# The Claims Counsel's First Read of a Title Claim

**Industry:** [[title-insurance-carriers|Title Insurance Carriers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced title claims counsel reads a new claim notice and knows within minutes whether it will close with a release from a servicer, turn into a quiet-title suit or end as a full loss of title, and that read sets the reserve, the legal spend and the outcome without ever being written down or graded.

## The Problem
A title claim arrives as a letter, an email from a lender's servicing department or a complaint served on the insured. A homeowner says a neighbour's fence sits on land the policy described. A lender finds, after foreclosure, a prior mortgage nobody released. A buyer learns the seller was an impostor and the real owner wants the land back. The claims counsel assigned to it — a lawyer, usually licensed in the state, carrying a caseload of perhaps 100–200 open files (estimate) — has to answer several questions fast. Is the loss covered, excluded, or within an exception the commitment took? Can the title be cured, by getting a release, recording a corrective deed or buying out an adverse claim? Must the insured be defended in litigation, at the insurer's expense? Or is this a loss to be paid? Unfair claims settlement practices acts put a clock on the acknowledgement and the coverage position (roughly 10–45 days depending on state and step; estimate), and the first case reserve is set from the same read.

Experienced counsel make that read from patterns they cannot fully state. They know which servicers will issue a release for a payoff that was made but never recorded, and which will fight. They know which county's judges grant quiet-title relief on a missing heir in months, and that a forged deed on vacant land owned by an out-of-state owner tends to become a full loss of title. They can often tell an agent's examination error from a defect no search would have found, and they sense when an insured's lawyer is building a bad-faith case. The read decides the money. Fraud and forgery claims averaged over $143,000 in Milliman's study for ALTA of over 127,000 claims (policies issued 2013–2022), against about $26,000 for other claims, and made up 21% of claim dollars. Nearly 30% of losses and claims expense came from defects a public-records search could not reveal. Industry losses and loss adjustment expense came to $807.7M in 2025 (NAIC).

The consequence reaches the balance sheet. First American reports that 65–75% of claim amounts become known in the first six years of a policy's life, and at the end of 2025 held $1,095.9M of IBNR against $54.6M of known-claim reserves. A 50 basis point change in loss rates on the last six policy years moves that IBNR by $162.7M. The early reads of claims counsel feed the case reserves, the case reserves feed the development triangles, and the triangles set the loss provision rate (3.0% of premiums and escrow fees in 2025). When an experienced counsel retires, their sense of which claims escalate goes with them. It survives only in claim notes nobody mines.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge comes first. The expert's read happens at intake and is recorded, if at all, as a reserve figure and a few lines in a claim note. The reasoning stays out of the file: why this servicer, why this county, why this looked like fraud. Capturing it means recording counsel's predicted path, severity band and confidence at the moment they form them. Historical claims then have to be reconstructed from notes, coverage letters, panel-counsel invoices and payment records spread across claim systems that have changed several times. Outcomes take roughly one to five years to arrive (estimate), and the underwriting file (the commitment, the examiner's notes, the approval email) often sits with the agent, not the carrier.

The labelling challenge is that counsel disagree, with each other and with themselves. Two experienced counsel will choose different resolution paths for the same file, and the same counsel reads a forged-deed claim differently after a recent large loss. Cure options depend on state law and local courts, so a pattern learned in Texas may be wrong in New York. Each counsel sees few claims of a given defect type in a given state in a year, so personal feedback is sparse. Labels need repeated annotation on a sample to measure agreement, and outcomes need careful definition, because a cured claim with heavy legal spend can cost more than a prompt payment.

The deployment challenge is speed and liability. Counsel form their read in minutes, so a model that needs a day of document gathering will be ignored. Coverage positions also carry bad-faith exposure. No carrier will let a model deny a claim, and every suggestion must be explainable to a regulator, an insured's lawyer and, eventually, a jury. The system has to sit inside intake, pull the policy and the underwriting file automatically, and offer a second opinion with precedents rather than a verdict.

## What a Solution Looks Like
At intake, a model reads the notice, the policy and endorsements, the commitment and any underwriter approval, the agent's file where available, and the recorded documents for the parcel. It predicts the likely resolution path (cure, defend, negotiate, pay, decline as excluded), a severity band with an interval, expected loss adjustment expense and time to close. It also retrieves the ten most similar closed claims with what was done and what it cost. Counsel records their own read in one click: path, severity and confidence. Both predictions are scored when the claim closes. Root-cause tags (examination error, undiscoverable fraud, survey matter, agent defalcation) flow back to underwriting and agency management. Over time the carrier learns which counsel are calibrated on which claim types. New counsel inherit a searchable precedent base instead of a mentor's anecdotes, and the actuaries get earlier, better-labelled case reserves.

## Impact If Solved
If earlier and better-calibrated triage cut loss adjustment expense on curable claims by even 10% (estimate), the industry would save tens of millions of dollars a year against $807.7M of losses and LAE. The larger prize is reserve confidence: at First American's scale, every 50 basis points of loss-rate uncertainty on six policy years is $162.7M of IBNR, and better-labelled case reserves narrow that range years sooner.
