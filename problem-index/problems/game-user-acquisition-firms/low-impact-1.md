# Creative That Shows a Game That Does Not Exist

**Industry:** [[game-user-acquisition-firms|Game User Acquisition Firms]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Ads depicting mechanics that are not in the game acquire installs cheaply, the practice has drawn platform policy and consumer protection attention, and almost nobody has measured whether the installs are worth anything after the churn.
**Tags:** #cnns #transformers #contrastive-learning #causal-inference #survival-analysis #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #compliance

## The Problem
Mobile game advertising has a well-documented practice of depicting gameplay the product does not contain — pull-the-pin puzzles advertising a base builder, a decorating mechanic advertising a match-three, a combat scene advertising an idle game. It persists because it works at the top of the funnel: the ads are cheap to produce, they generate high install rates, and install volume is what a campaign is measured on first.

Platform policies prohibit misrepresenting gameplay, enforcement is uneven, and consumer protection bodies in several jurisdictions have taken an interest. The industry's internal position is largely that it is standard practice, with some operators declining to do it and others building the advertised mechanic into the game afterwards specifically to make the ad accurate — which is an unusual product development process and a real one.

The commercial question is unanswered. A misleading ad acquires a player expecting a different game, who churns quickly and at high rates, and whether the cheap install survives that churn into positive value is an empirical question. It is measurable and is rarely measured, partly because campaign evaluation stops at cost per install and partly because the answer might require abandoning a working tactic.

## What Already Exists
Platform advertising policies prohibit gameplay misrepresentation and are enforced through review and complaint. Creative production tooling generates variants at volume. Creative analytics vendors tag assets and report performance. Some operators run creative testing with retention-based metrics rather than install-based ones, which is the correct practice and is not the norm. Industry bodies have discussed self-regulation without much effect.

## The Customisation Gap
Claim accuracy is checkable and nobody checks it. Comparing what an advertisement depicts against what the game actually contains — mechanics, art style, progression, interface — is a multimodal matching problem between the creative and the game's own build, and a firm running a portfolio could measure its own creative's accuracy systematically rather than relying on an individual's judgement.

The measurement gap is the more consequential one. Evaluating creative on install cost hides the entire effect; evaluating on cohort value at day 30 and day 90, with early churn attributed to the creative that acquired the player, gives the honest number. That requires linking creative identity through to cohort outcomes, which the aggregated attribution environment makes harder and not impossible at cohort level.

The per-operator customisation is genre and audience specific. A stylised depiction that reads as an exaggeration in one genre reads as a false claim in another, and the line between attractive presentation and misrepresentation is a judgement that a portfolio operator can calibrate from its own retention data rather than from an intuition.

And the policy risk should be quantified. Which creatives are most likely to trigger enforcement, and what a platform rejection or a regulatory action would cost, is estimable from the operator's own history and the public record, and turning it into a number is what would make the internal argument concrete rather than moral.

## Impact If Solved
This practice is the industry's most visible reputational problem and its commercial justification has never been tested properly. Automated claim-accuracy measurement plus creative evaluation on cohort value rather than install cost would answer whether the tactic actually pays — and if the honest answer is that it does not once churn is counted, that finding removes a practice that regulators are increasingly likely to remove for the industry on worse terms.
