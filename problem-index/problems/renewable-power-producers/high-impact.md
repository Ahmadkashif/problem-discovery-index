# The Queue Position Nobody Can Price

**Industry:** [[renewable-power-producers|Renewable Power Producers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A developer pays escalating deposits to keep each interconnection request alive for five years, and the senior interconnection lead's read of which positions will survive their cluster studies, the call that decides where the money goes, is never written down or scored.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #survival-analysis #gradient-boosting #bayesian-inference #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #data-integration #revenue-impact

## The Problem
The interconnection director at a mid-size solar and storage developer carries 40–80 queue positions across MISO, PJM, ERCOT, SPP and CAISO (estimate). Each sits in a cluster run by a grid operator or utility, and each reaches a decision point several times before it is built: whether to enter the cluster window at all, whether to post the commercial readiness deposit after the first study results, whether to post 5% and then 10% of the assigned network upgrade cost, and whether to sign the facilities study and the interconnection agreement. At each point the options are to continue, downsize, swap solar for storage, or withdraw. Under FERC Order No. 2023 the money at risk climbs with every step. A 200 MW solar project assigned $60M of network upgrades must put up ~$3M at restudy and ~$6M at the facilities study (derived from the 5% and 10% rules), and withdrawal penalties are tied to those deposits.

Experienced interconnection people make these calls on knowledge they cannot fully specify. They know that a particular 345 kV substation is "full" and the next cluster will be handed a line upgrade. They know which transmission owners' studies slip two cycles, that the projects queued ahead of theirs at the same point are speculative and will drop out, which reallocates their upgrade costs onto whoever stays, and that an affected-system study from the neighbouring utility tends to add cost late. That is pattern recognition built over dozens of clusters, the same kind of tacit judgement a master electrician shows at a panel.

The outcomes are recorded in public. LBNL's Queued Up 2026 edition found that more than 70% of requests submitted from 2000 to 2020 were withdrawn, only 13% of the capacity reached operation, 41% of capacity that signed an interconnection agreement from 2000 to 2022 still withdrew, and the median 2025 project took 61 months from request to operation. What no dataset records is what the developer expected at each decision: the survival odds, upgrade cost and online date in the director's head when she paid the next deposit. That is where the money is lost.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is capturing the expert's reasoning at the moment of decision. Cluster request windows last 45 days and initial studies 150, and decisions crowd into those windows. Queue trackers record status changes; the reasoning, if written at all, is a line in an investment committee deck. A view reconstructed after the result is in is rationalised.

The labelling challenge is that the outcome is slow and ambiguous. About five years pass between request and operation, so a model trained on finished outcomes learns from projects filed under the old serial process. A withdrawal can be a good call that avoided a bad upgrade assignment or a lost position that would have paid off, so "withdrawn" is not a label for "wrong". Experts disagree with themselves too: the same director rates the same substation differently after a painful restudy. The positions a developer enters are not a random sample of the ones it screened, and the public data have holes, with valid in-service dates for only 73% of operational projects and withdrawal years for 63% of withdrawn ones.

The deployment challenge is speed and access. A screen must rank hundreds of candidate points of interconnection inside a cluster window, faster than the director can, or it will be ignored. The power-flow cases that decide upgrade costs are critical energy infrastructure information released under non-disclosure, so a developer cannot pool them across firms, and the study results themselves arrive as PDF reports with tables that differ by grid operator.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start outside the firm with the two-week MVP: a withdrawal-odds and months-to-operation model trained on the Queued Up data file, current ISO queue files pulled with the open-source gridstatus library, and EIA-860 operating dates. A competing-risks survival model treats operation and withdrawal as rival endings, with features such as grid operator, transmission owner, technology, size, interconnection voltage, megawatts queued at the same substation, queue vintage, study phase and prior withdrawal rates at that point. Its output is a probability of reaching operation and a distribution of months to get there for every active request, recomputed when the queue files update.

Then add the inside record. At every decision point, a position ledger captures the director's own estimate of survival, upgrade cost per kilowatt and online date, plus a one-line reason, drafted from the study report and the committee memo and confirmed in a minute. As outcomes arrive, compare her estimates with the model's, learn where her read adds information the public covariates miss, and flag the positions where she and the model disagree before each deposit is due.

## Impact If Solved
A developer posting ~$50M a year of study and readiness deposits and spending ~$20M a year developing positions that later withdraw (estimate) saves ~$5M a year if it cuts late-stage withdrawals by a quarter, and moves that money to positions that get built. Across the industry, turning 1% of the 2,061 GW backlog from withdrawn into built is ~20 GW of capacity, and the firm keeps its interconnection lead's judgement when she leaves.
