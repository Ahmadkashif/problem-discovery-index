# The Savings Assumptions Nobody Grades

**Industry:** [[logistics-consultants|Logistics Consultants]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every network and facility recommendation rests on a handful of assumptions a senior consultant sets by feel — how fast a new building ramps, what unbid lanes will cost, how much of the saving the client will really capture — and none is recorded as a judgement, graded against what the client later realised, or kept when the consultant leaves.

## The Problem
Once a network study's baseline is accepted, the work that decides the recommendation begins: scenarios. Should a consumer goods shipper close its single national DC and open two regional ones? The optimiser answers that question only after a senior consultant — usually someone with fifteen or more years in the work — has set the inputs no dataset supplies. What labour productivity will a new building reach in its first year, against the mature building it replaces? What will carriers charge on outbound lanes from a city the shipper has never shipped from, where no bid history exists? What wage and turnover should be assumed in each candidate labour market? What will the transition cost in dual running, inventory build and customer disruption? And, decisively, how much of the modelled saving will this client actually capture, given that its sales team will refuse to move two large customers and its transport team has never managed a multi-DC network?

Experienced consultants answer these from pattern recognition across dozens of past engagements. They "know" that a new building in a tight labour market will take longer to reach standard than the vendor's ramp curve says, that a projected saving above a certain share of freight spend usually means an error somewhere rather than an opportunity, that a client whose executive sponsor is in finance captures more of the plan than one whose sponsor is in sales. This is tacit knowledge in exactly the sense the term is used for a craftsman: they cannot write the specification, and a junior cannot infer it from the data.

None of these judgements is recorded as a judgement. An assumption is a number in a cell, perhaps a footnote on a slide; the reasoning was a conversation with the partner. The projected saving goes into the readout deck. Twelve to thirty-six months later the outcome arrives in the client's freight invoices, labour reports and inventory balances — and nobody compares it with the projection, because the consultant has rolled off and the client implemented part of the plan in a freight market that has since moved. When the senior consultant leaves, which in a firm of twelve people means a large share of the firm's judgement, the firm keeps the decks and loses the reasoning.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's reasoning has to be captured at the moment the assumption is set, by the expert, inside a scenario deadline. Network design platforms store the value of a parameter, not who chose it, from what precedent, or how confident they were. Asking a senior consultant to document every assumption is a tax on the scarcest person in the firm, with no visible return to them. The other half of the record — what actually happened — sits with the client, and fixed-fee engagement letters rarely grant access to post-implementation data.

The labelling problem is that the realised outcome is a noisy label for the quality of an assumption. Whether a projected saving appeared depends on volumes, channel mix, the freight market and partial implementation; a sound productivity assumption can look wrong because the client delayed the go-live into peak season, and a careless one can look right because rates fell. Honest grading needs the projection restated at the volumes and market rates that actually occurred — a counterfactual, not a comparison. Outcomes are also selected: only the recommended scenario is implemented, so the alternatives are never observed. And experts disagree with themselves: the same consultant sets different ramp assumptions for two near-identical buildings a year apart, so any model of their judgement needs repeated assessment on a sample to measure that consistency.

The deployment problem is timing and authority. Help has to arrive while the scenario is being built, faster than the consultant's own recall of "the last three times we opened a building like this", or it will be ignored. And the partner signs the recommendation; they will accept a system that shows precedent and a track record, not one that overrides them.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An assumption register first, and models second. Every scenario assumption is captured as a structured record — the parameter, its value, its author, the precedent or source cited and a confidence range — drafted automatically from the model file and the consultant's notes and confirmed in seconds. Where the engagement letter allows a follow-up, the realised values are captured at 12 and 24 months: achieved productivity, actual lane rates, transition cost, and the share of the plan implemented, with the projection restated at realised volumes and market rates so the comparison isolates the assumption.

On top of the register, learn the senior consultants' recurring calls. Models trained on past engagements predict the value a senior consultant would set for each assumption given the engagement's features, and — where outcomes exist — how far that kind of assumption has historically missed. They surface both to the consultant setting the scenario, with the precedent attached, and to the partner reviewing it. The goal is not to replace the read but to make an experienced consultant's judgement, with its track record, available to the next engagement and to their successor.

## Impact If Solved
A firm that can show clients a record of projected against realised savings, restated honestly, has the strongest sales argument in an industry where every competitor claims savings and none can prove them, and it can see which of its assumptions are systematically optimistic before a client discovers it in a P&L. Keeping the senior consultant's calibrated judgement through a departure turns the industry's most expensive recurring loss into something the firm retains.
