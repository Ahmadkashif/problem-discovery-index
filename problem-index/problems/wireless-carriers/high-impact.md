# The Degraded Sector Only a Senior RF Engineer Can Read

**Industry:** [[wireless-carriers|Wireless Carriers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced RF engineer can look at a sector's counters and know within minutes whether the fault is tilt, interference, a neighbour list or a dying radio, but the carrier records only the parameter change and never the diagnosis, so the judgement can't be measured, taught or kept.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #graph-neural-networks #gradient-boosting #causal-inference #time-series-forecasting #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A regional optimisation team at a national carrier watches thousands of sectors. Each morning a worst-offender report ranks them by dropped calls, failed connections, throughput and interference, and an RF engineer works down the list. For each sector the engineer pulls up the counters by hour, the signal strength and quality distributions (RSRP, SINR), the PRB utilisation, the neighbour relations and handover failures, recent parameter and hardware changes on that site and its neighbours, and sometimes a drive test or crowdsourced measurements. Then they decide what is wrong and what to change.

The senior engineer's read is fast and mostly unspoken. A rise in the uplink noise floor that tracks the local time of day suggests an external interferer, perhaps a faulty booster in a nearby building. The same rise that tracks the sector's own traffic points to passive intermodulation in a corroded connector. Handover failures to one neighbour after a tilt change on that neighbour mean the change, not the sector, is the culprit. Throughput collapsing at 8 p.m. while signal quality holds is congestion, not coverage, and the fix is capacity, not tilt. A junior engineer sees the same charts and changes the wrong parameter, or opens a ticket for a climb that wasn't needed. Job ads for these roles ask for five to seven years of experience. That number is a rough measure of how long the pattern library takes to build.

The carrier logs the change: a new tilt, a power setting, a neighbour added, a radio swapped. It does not log the diagnosis that produced the change, the alternatives the engineer ruled out, or whether the sector actually recovered once traffic and the neighbours' changes are accounted for. When the engineer retires, moves to another market, or the work moves to an equipment vendor's managed-services team, the carrier keeps the change history and loses the judgement behind it. 5G makes this worse. Massive MIMO radios add beam and power settings by the dozen, and the number of sectors per engineer keeps rising as small cells multiply.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's reasoning exists only in the moment of diagnosis. Change requests carry a free-text reason field that engineers fill with "optimisation" or a ticket number. Asking a busy engineer to write a diagnosis for every sector is a tax with no visible return, and a diagnosis written after the outcome is known is rationalised. The record has to be captured as the engineer works: which charts they opened, which neighbour they checked, which hypothesis they tested. Then it has to be confirmed in a click.

The labelling problem has two layers. First, experts disagree with each other and with themselves. Two senior engineers will call the same uplink pattern interference and PIM, and the same engineer calls it differently after a recent PIM hunt. Labels need repeated annotation on a sample to measure that ceiling. Second, the outcome label is confounded. A sector's KPIs move with traffic, weather, events, school terms and every change made to its neighbours that week. "The sector recovered after the change" is a noisy label unless it is measured against comparable untouched sectors and neighbour changes are controlled for. Some fixes, such as a climb to replace a jumper, take weeks to happen, so the label arrives late.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. A suggestion has to arrive while the engineer is working the worst-offender list, inside the vendor tools they already use, and be faster than their own glance at the charts. Otherwise it is ignored. It also has to explain itself in the engineer's terms, because a wrong recommendation that triggers an unneeded tower climb costs money and credibility. The national carriers' network data often sits in vendor-managed platforms, so even getting the counters and the change history into one place is a negotiation.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A diagnosis ledger first and a model second. Every sector investigation produces a short structured record: the symptoms seen, the suspected cause from a fixed list (interference, PIM, coverage, capacity, neighbour or mobility, hardware, backhaul, configuration), the action taken, and the expected effect. The record is drafted automatically from the charts the engineer opened and the change they submitted, and confirmed in seconds. Each record is graded two to four weeks later against matched control sectors, with neighbour changes and traffic shifts accounted for.

On top of the ledger, learn the senior engineers' reads. A model over the sector and its neighbour graph (counters, timing patterns, change histories) predicts the likely cause and the action most likely to work, shows the closest past cases with their outcomes, and flags when a proposed change is the kind that has failed before on similar sectors. Juniors get the senior's pattern library with its track record. Managers get a measure of which playbooks and which teams fix sectors, including the vendor's managed-services team.

## Impact If Solved
If engineers resolve degraded sectors faster and with fewer wrong changes and unneeded climbs, each engineer covers more sectors and fewer customers sit for weeks on a sector that drops calls. With postpaid churn near 0.9% a month, cutting network-driven churn even slightly is worth tens of millions a year to a national carrier (estimate). A carrier would also keep its best engineers' judgement when they retire or the work is outsourced, and could finally grade the optimisation work it pays vendors for.
