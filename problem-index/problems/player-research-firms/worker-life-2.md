# The Moderator Running Eight Sessions a Day

**Industry:** [[player-research-firms|Player Research Firms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Session moderation is emotionally demanding concentration work performed back to back, and the person doing it is also the one who writes it up afterwards.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #gradient-boosting #bert #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
Moderating a research session is sustained, careful work. The moderator must keep a participant talking aloud without leading them, observe and note what happens without interrupting, manage the technical setup, keep to a protocol, handle a participant who is frustrated or embarrassed at struggling, and remain neutral while watching someone fail at something repeatedly.

Sessions are scheduled back to back because participant availability and lab time are expensive. Six to eight sessions a day during a study period is normal, each ninety minutes, with short turnarounds for reset and notes.

The concentration decays and everyone knows it. The eighth session of the day is not observed as carefully as the first, and the notes from it are thinner — which introduces a systematic bias into the study that is entirely a function of scheduling.

Around the sessions sits the operational load: recruitment coordination, no-shows and replacements, technical setup and its failures, consent and incentive administration, and then the analysis and write-up, which lands on the same person after the session days are over.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is emotional labour with a research protocol attached. Watching people struggle, keeping them comfortable while not helping them, and managing their embarrassment is genuinely tiring, and doing it eight times a day for a fortnight is a specific kind of depletion that the field discusses informally and schedules as though it does not exist.

The role is also frequently junior and treated as an entry point, which means the person doing the most demanding in-person work has the least influence over how it is scheduled.

And the quality cost is borne silently. A moderator knows that their later sessions were weaker and has no mechanism to flag it, because the alternative is fewer sessions and the study was scoped on the number.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Take the note-taking off the moderator. Automatic transcription with speaker separation, automatic event coding against game state, and automatic capture of struggle indicators mean the moderator can attend to the participant rather than to the clipboard — which improves the session and removes the largest source of between-session quality variance.

Handle the operations. Scheduling, reminders, no-show prediction and replacement, consent and incentive administration are mechanical and consume the gaps between sessions that should be recovery.

Measure and schedule for fatigue. Session quality indicators — note density, prompt frequency, protocol adherence, session length drift — are measurable, and a firm that could show quality declining after the fifth session of a day would have the evidence to scope studies differently. That argument currently has no numbers behind it.

Support the write-up. A drafted session summary generated from the transcript, the event coding and the moderator's own marks, ready for editing, removes the work that currently lands on the same exhausted person after the session days end.

## Impact If Solved
Moderation quality determines study quality and degrades predictably across a day in a way nobody measures. Automating note-taking and operations returns the moderator's attention to the participant, evidence about fatigue makes study scoping arguable, and drafted summaries remove the post-session load — all of which improve the findings as well as the working life of the people producing them.
