# Diligence Analyst in Seller Central

**Industry:** [[ecommerce-aggregators|Ecommerce Aggregators]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A diligence analyst evaluates an acquisition target through screenshots and exports from a marketplace reporting interface built for sellers, under a deadline set by a competitive auction.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #hypothesis-testing #confidence-intervals #time-series-forecasting #descriptive-statistics #evaluation-metrics #data-integration #worker-facing

## The Problem
An acquisition target is presented. The analyst has days, sometimes a couple of weeks, to determine whether the business is what it appears to be and what it is worth.

The evidence is a data room containing exports and screenshots from Seller Central. That interface reports a seller's own performance and was never designed for third-party analysis, so the analyst reconstructs a business from reports that do not reconcile, in formats that vary by seller, sometimes at different date ranges.

Then the real questions begin, and the data room does not answer them. How much of the trailing revenue came from a period of unusual demand. How defensible is the ranking. What has happened to competition in the category over the same period. Are the reviews organic. Is the supplier relationship transferable. Is the margin real once fees, returns, storage and advertising are properly allocated.

Answering these requires external data and analysis that takes longer than the timeline allows, so the analyst produces a model built on trailing performance with adjustments they know are inadequate.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The analyst's recommendation commits substantial capital on evidence they know is incomplete, under a deadline, in a competitive process where taking longer means losing the deal. That is a professionally uncomfortable position held repeatedly.

The manual reconstruction is the bulk of the time. Normalising exports, allocating fees correctly, reconciling reports that disagree — the mechanical work crowds out the judgement that is the analyst's actual contribution.

The feedback loop is absent or brutal. Acquisitions that go badly become visible eighteen months later, and by then the analyst has assessed dozens more on the same method. When the sector corrected, the analysis that was performed under those conditions was judged retrospectively as though better analysis had been possible.

And the accumulated knowledge is unstructured. An analyst who has assessed two hundred businesses has real pattern recognition and no artefact expressing it, so the two-hundred-and-first assessment starts from a template.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Automated normalisation of marketplace exports into a standard financial and operational model, with fees, returns, storage and advertising allocated correctly. This is mechanical, it is the bulk of the analyst's time, and it is the same work at every deal.

External enrichment as standard. Category competitive dynamics, competitor listing growth, price trends and review velocity comparisons are observable from public marketplace data and are exactly the context the data room omits.

Anomaly flagging against a portfolio baseline. Review velocity inconsistent with sales, a demand spike that looks like a one-off, a margin that depends on an unusual fee treatment — each is detectable by comparison against the hundreds of businesses the firm has already assessed.

Prediction anchored on realised outcomes. The firm knows what happened to every brand it bought, and a model trained on that is worth more than any multiple heuristic.

Structured capture of the analyst's own judgement, so that the qualitative assessments that turned out to matter become part of the next model rather than staying in a memo.

## Impact If Solved
Acquisition decisions in this sector were made at pace on reconstructed data with the decisive questions unanswered, and the resulting write-downs were substantial. Automating the reconstruction returns the analyst's time to judgement, and anchoring the projection on the firm's own realised outcomes is the only underwriting improvement that addresses what actually went wrong.
