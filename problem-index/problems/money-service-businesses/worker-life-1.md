# The Clerk Who Has to Question a Scam Victim

**Industry:** [[money-service-businesses|Money Service Businesses]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The clerk at an agent counter is the last person who can stop a grandmother from wiring her savings to a scammer, and is expected to do it in a second language, with a queue behind her, guided by a training video she clicked through once a year.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #compliance #automation

## The Problem
A woman in her seventies comes to the counter of a corner store that sells money transfers. She wants to send $2,800 to a "lawyer" in another country who is helping her grandson out of jail. Or a young man wants to send a deposit for an apartment he found online, or a man is sending his third transfer this week to a woman he met on a dating app. The clerk — often the owner's relative, paid hourly, working the register and the lottery machine at the same time — is supposed to notice, ask the right questions, and refuse or escalate if the answers sound coached.

States require it. California's 2011 law obliges licensed transmitters to train their agents every year on recognising and responding to elder financial abuse, and to train new agents within a month of appointment; Maryland has a similar statute. The transmitter's terminal may show a generic warning screen. In practice the clerk relies on whatever instinct they have built: the customer who is on the phone while sending and repeats what the voice says, the receiver name that does not match the stated relationship, the customer who has never sent abroad before and now sends to a country they have no tie to.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
It is an awful position. Ask too little and the clerk watches a regular customer lose her savings; ask too much and the customer is insulted, leaves, and goes to the competitor's brand three doors down — and the store owner, paid on commission, is unhappy. The clerk carries the moral weight without authority, without language support when the customer speaks Tagalog and the clerk speaks Spanish, and without feedback: they almost never learn whether the transfer they hesitated over was a scam.

It also carries personal risk. Agents who process fraud repeatedly are suspended or terminated by the transmitter, and the clerk's job goes with the store's agent contract. Clerks at stores that a scam crew targets can be pressured or threatened.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A counter assistant that scores each send in real time against the sender's history, the corridor, the receiver's history across the network and known scam typologies, and, when risk is elevated, gives the clerk two or three specific questions to ask in the customer's language — rather than a generic warning screen — along with a scripted, face-saving way to pause the transfer and call the transmitter's fraud line. Afterwards the clerk is told when a transfer they paused turned out to be fraud, and the store sees how many customers its staff protected.

## Impact If Solved
Clerks get a tool instead of a burden: fewer moments where they must choose between a customer's savings and the store's commission without guidance, a few seconds per flagged transaction rather than an awkward interrogation, and evidence that their judgement mattered. Transmitters turn hundreds of thousands of counter staff from a liability into the first line of scam defence.
