# Telling a Complicit Agent From a Busy One

**Industry:** [[money-service-businesses|Money Service Businesses]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A money transmitter is legally responsible for tens of thousands of independent agent counters, and the judgement that separates a busy store from a captured or complicit one lives in a few senior investigators' heads, is never graded against what happened, and arrives years too late in the cases that matter.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #graph-neural-networks #survival-analysis #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #compliance #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A money transmitter does not own most of the places where its customers hand over cash. It contracts with agents: a grocery in Houston, a travel agency in Queens, a phone shop in Minneapolis, each an independent business that runs the transmitter's terminal, collects the sender's cash and ID, and sweeps the funds to the transmitter by ACH. Many of these counters carry two or three competing brands. Under the Bank Secrecy Act and state licensing law the transmitter is responsible for every one of them: it must risk-rate, train, monitor and, when necessary, suspend or terminate the agent.

Agent-oversight teams do this from three kinds of evidence. There are transaction logs: the agent's volume, average ticket, corridors, time of day, how often senders split amounts just under thresholds, how often the same receiver collects from many senders, how often transfers are refunded, how often consumers later report fraud. There are field visits, where an auditor checks the clerk's ID handling and the displayed notices and comes back with notes. And there are complaints, law-enforcement requests and fraud reports. Out of that mass a senior investigator picks the handful of agents to escalate.

What the experienced investigator knows is not in the written procedure. That a sudden shift from Mexico to West African corridors at a store with no West African clientele means the clerk is processing for a fraud ring. That a run of transfers at round amounts, sent to one city by senders with sequential ID numbers, is a structuring crew using one compliant-looking store. That an owner who calls the help desk asking how the threshold works is more worrying than one who never calls. That a "good" agent with perfect field-visit scores and no alerts can be the problem precisely because the alerts are being gamed. Investigators describe this as a feel for a store, developed across hundreds of cases.

The cost of reading it late is the defining story of the industry. Western Union's 2017 resolution with the Justice Department and the FTC carried a $586M forfeiture; 26 of its agents in the US and Canada had been convicted for conspiring with fraudsters, and prosecutors found that the company's own suspension guidelines would have led to corrective action against more than 2,000 agents worldwide between 2004 and 2013 had they been followed. The judgement existed in the organisation. It was not applied consistently, and nobody could show which signals actually predicted a bad agent.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's reasoning has to be captured while they work a case, and agent investigations run for weeks across tools that do not talk to each other: the monitoring system, a case management tool, field-visit forms, a complaint log, sweep returns from treasury, and email with law enforcement. The investigator's actual read — "the corridor mix is wrong for this neighbourhood" — rarely appears anywhere except a closing memo, if one is written. Field-visit notes are free text written by contractors with varying diligence.

The labelling problem is severe. The true label — this agent was complicit — arrives rarely and late, often years after the activity, and sometimes never because the agent was quietly terminated. Terminations themselves are a noisy label: some reflect commercial disputes or a missed sweep rather than misconduct, and some complicit agents are never caught, so "not terminated" does not mean "clean". Experts also disagree with themselves: the same investigator rates the same pattern differently after a recent enforcement action or a quiet quarter. And the label set is confidential. SAR confidentiality under the BSA bars disclosure of whether a report was filed, so the most informative labels cannot be pooled across transmitters or shown to the agent — and no transmitter sees an agent's activity for its competitors' brands at the same counter.

The deployment problem is that agent risk scores compete with commerce. Agent-network sales teams are paid on locations and volume, and a high-volume agent in a strong corridor is exactly the kind a model may flag. A score is only acted on if it comes with reasons an investigator recognises as the ones they would have used, fast enough to stop the next week's transfers rather than explain last year's.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An agent file first and a model second. Every agent gets one longitudinal record joining transaction features, field-visit findings, complaints, fraud reports, sweep returns, training completion, ownership changes and every investigator action and conclusion, with the reason recorded in the investigator's own words and structured at closing in seconds rather than written from scratch. Outcomes — termination for cause, law-enforcement action, consumer-fraud concentration, regulator findings — are attached when they arrive.

On top of the file, learn the investigators' read. Train time-to-adverse-outcome models on agent histories, using investigator escalations as an early, noisy label and confirmed outcomes as the late, clean one. Model the network around each agent — shared senders, shared receivers, shared phone numbers and devices across locations — so a crew moving between stores is visible as one pattern. Present each flag with the two or three features and the comparable past cases that drove it, so a senior investigator can agree or disagree quickly, and record that disagreement as a label.

## Impact If Solved
A transmitter that can rank its agent network by evidence rather than by volume and recent complaints concentrates field visits and investigator time where risk actually sits, and can show regulators, sponsor banks and monitors a consistent, documented basis for every suspension. Catching a captured agent months earlier rather than years later is the difference between a contained fraud loss and the kind of resolution that reshapes a company; retaining senior investigators' reads as a graded, reusable record also protects the programme when those investigators leave.
