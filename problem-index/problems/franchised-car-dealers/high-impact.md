# The Order Board Nobody Can Explain

**Industry:** [[franchised-car-dealers|Franchised Car Dealers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A new-car manager decides every order cycle which trims, colours and packages to take from the OEM's allocation, and the store's turn, floorplan bill and stair-step bonus ride on a feel for the local market that is never written down and leaves with the manager.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #causal-inference #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
A franchised store does not choose its new-vehicle inventory freely. The OEM allocates units by formula, usually weighting recent retail sales and days supply, and the store then places orders against that allocation in the OEM's order portal, accepts or declines units the OEM has built for it, and swaps vehicles with other dealers through dealer trades. An average store sells about 950 new vehicles a year (16.2 million units across 16,990 dealers, NADA Data 2025), so the new-car manager or general sales manager makes hundreds of these configuration decisions a year, often within an order window of a few days (estimate, it varies by OEM).

Each decision is a bet on the local market. An experienced manager "just knows" that the mid trim with the towing package and all-wheel drive turns in two weeks in this county while the top trim in red sits for ninety days; that white and grey sell in August but not every colour sells in December; that a fleet-heavy neighbour store will take the base trucks in a trade; and that a few extra units are worth taking this month because two more retail sales clear the stair-step and pay a bonus on every unit sold. Juniors learn this over years and by losing money on aged units. When the manager moves to another store, the allocation history stays in the portal and the judgement that drove it goes with them.

The cost of a wrong mix is direct. A unit on the lot carries floorplan interest from delivery, roughly $8–10 a day at today's ~$50,000 transaction prices (estimate), plus OEM floorplan assistance that typically covers only a set number of days (estimate). An aged unit ends up discounted or wholesaled, it drags the store's days supply up, and under turn-and-earn a slow turn shrinks next month's allocation of the vehicles that do sell. A right mix compounds the other way: faster turn, more allocation of the hot units, stair-step targets hit, and less money spent on incentives to move the wrong cars.

## Why It's Unsolved
**Collecting the expert's decision.** The decision happens in an OEM portal that the dealer does not control and that records only the order, not the reason. The manager rarely writes down why a unit was declined or why the mix shifted. Reconstructing it means pairing order-bank snapshots, allocation notices and dealer-trade logs with DMS sales history, and then asking the manager to annotate decisions during a busy order window. Any capture has to sit inside that window and take seconds, or it will not be used.

**Labelling the outcome.** Days-to-sale and front gross are recorded for every unit sold, but they are poor labels on their own. The outcome is censored for units still on the lot. It is confounded by price and incentive changes made after the order. And it is selected: configurations the manager never ordered have no outcome at all, so the data cannot directly show whether declining the red top trim was right. Experts also disagree with themselves. The same manager orders more conservatively after a month of aged units, and two managers in the same group read the same market differently. Agreement with the manager is therefore a weak target, and the honest target is a counterfactual one.

**Deploying it.** A recommendation must arrive inside the order window, must be faster than the manager's own scan of the allocation sheet, and must explain itself in the manager's terms (comparable units, recent turn, open stair-step gap). It also has to respect constraints the model does not see, such as OEM minimums, programme rules that tie allocation to retail effort, and a group's buying policy. Without an OEM API, pulling the allocation into a tool and pushing orders back often means screen work or file exports.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An order desk that sits beside the OEM portal. It ingests the allocation and order-bank offer, the store's DMS sales and inventory history, dealer-trade logs, licensed local registration data and the month's programme rules, and returns for each offered unit an expected days-to-sale and gross range, a take, decline or trade recommendation, and the reason ("last 12 of this configuration sold in a median 11 days; you are 3 units short of the stair-step tier"). The manager's accept, decline or override is captured with one click and a reason code, which builds the decision record the store never had.

Underneath sit survival models of time-to-sale by configuration, trained on the store's and the group's history with censoring handled properly, and a counterfactual layer that uses other stores' outcomes on configurations this store declined to estimate what it missed. Over time the desk can compare each manager's calls with the model's and with what happened, which turns a feel for the market into a measurable track record, and hands a new manager the previous one's reads with their hit rate attached.

## Impact If Solved
For a store selling ~950 new units a year, cutting average days-to-turn by ten days saves ~$80,000–95,000 a year in floorplan interest alone (estimate, at ~$8–10 a unit-day), before the larger effects: fewer aged units discounted or wholesaled, more allocation of the vehicles that sell, and stair-step bonuses earned rather than missed. At group scale the same record makes it possible to tell which managers' order judgement actually earns money, and to keep that judgement when they leave.
