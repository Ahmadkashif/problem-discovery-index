# The Microbiologist Reading Plates All Day

**Industry:** [[testing-laboratories|Testing Laboratories]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A food or water microbiologist counts and judges colonies on hundreds of agar plates a shift, on an incubation clock that ignores weekends, and gets back their eyes, their Saturdays and the time to work on the hard cases.
**Tags:** #cnns #object-detection #semantic-segmentation #transfer-learning #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation

## The Problem
Food, water and environmental microbiology labs run on incubation times. Plates for aerobic counts, coliforms, E. coli, yeast and mould, and presumptive pathogens go into incubators and must be read at a defined time — 24, 48 or 72 hours, or several days for moulds — whether that falls on a Tuesday or a Sunday. A microbiologist reading a busy shift may look at hundreds of plates, counting colonies in the countable range, judging which colonies are typical of the target organism by colour, size and morphology, picking suspects for confirmation, and recording counts by hand or by keying them into the LIMS.

It is close, repetitive, visually tiring work. Plates with spreaders, crowded colonies or atypical morphologies need judgement; plates in the countable range need careful counting; every count must be transcribed. A miscount or a missed typical colony is invisible until a proficiency test or a client's own result disagrees.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The person doing this trained as a scientist and spends much of the day as a counting instrument. The incubation clock decides their weekend rota: someone has to be in the lab on Saturday and Sunday to read plates at the right hour. Eye strain and repetitive strain are common complaints, and the judgement calls — is this colony typical, is this plate countable — are made fastest by the most experienced readers, who are the ones most needed elsewhere. Junior microbiologists learn colony morphology slowly, by having a senior colleague look over their shoulder.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An imaging station at the bench, or imaging built into the incubator, photographs each plate at the read time. Software counts colonies, separates touching colonies, flags spreaders and plates outside the countable range, classifies colonies as typical or atypical for the medium, and proposes which to pick for confirmation. The microbiologist reviews and accepts or corrects the read on screen, and the count flows to the LIMS with the image attached as evidence. Weekend reads can be done from images captured on schedule, with the reviewer confirming remotely or first thing on Monday where the method allows.

## Impact If Solved
Automated counting with human confirmation could cut plate-reading time by half or more (estimate), remove transcription errors, attach an image to every reported count for assessors and clients, and turn weekend shifts from a counting obligation into a short review — while giving junior staff a library of the senior reader's calls to learn from.
