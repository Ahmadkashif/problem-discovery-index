# The Field Technician on the Early Pour

**Industry:** [[testing-laboratories|Testing Laboratories]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A construction materials field technician starts before dawn, tests concrete and soil on a hot or frozen site with a superintendent waiting, writes everything on paper, and drives home to type it up — the technician gets back evenings and the confidence that a rejected load won't come back on them.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #linear-regression #feature-engineering #confidence-intervals #worker-facing #automation #quick-win

## The Problem
The day starts with a dispatch text the night before: site, tests, specification. Big pours start early, so the technician is on site before sunrise with a slump cone, air meter, thermometer, cylinder molds and a nuclear density gauge. For each truck or interval they check the delivery ticket against the mix design, run slump, air content and temperature, cast cylinders, and record it all on a paper field sheet while the pump crew waits. Between pours they move to earthwork, running density and moisture tests on compacted fill and calculating percent compaction against a laboratory proctor value. The cylinders must be left to cure on site and collected within the standard's window, then carried back to the lab.

The paperwork follows them home. Field sheets are transcribed into the lab's system or a report template at the end of a long day, often by the technician, and any arithmetic error — a wrong proctor value, a misread gauge — becomes a report the engineer of record relies on. One job ad in Ohio offers $18–28 an hour, overtime when production runs long, and outdoor, physical work through winter and summer.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The technician carries a responsibility out of proportion to their pay and seniority. When a load is out of specification, they are the person who tells a superintendent, in front of the crew, that the truck should be rejected — and they are the person blamed if a low cylinder break shows up 28 days later with no record of what they saw. Pressure to pass marginal loads is real and personal. The early starts, the driving between sites and the evening paperwork make it a job people leave after a few years, which in turn keeps field crews short and the remaining technicians overloaded.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A rugged phone or tablet app that takes the dispatch, pre-loads the mix design and specification limits, reads the batch ticket from a photo, does the calculations, flags an out-of-spec reading on the spot with the standard's limit shown, captures photos and GPS stamps as evidence, labels cylinders with a scannable ID at casting, and sends a draft field report to the lab before the technician leaves the site. Behind it, a model trained on the firm's history estimates the likely 28-day strength of each placement from the early data and mix, so a borderline pour is flagged for an early break rather than discovered a month later.

## Impact If Solved
Removing end-of-day transcription gives each technician back an hour or more per field day (estimate), eliminates the arithmetic errors that turn into disputed reports, and puts the technician's on-site call on the record with evidence — which is what lets a 24-year-old technician reject a truck without fearing for their job.
