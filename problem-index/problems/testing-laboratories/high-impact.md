# The Senior Reviewer's Call Before a Result Is Signed

**Industry:** [[testing-laboratories|Testing Laboratories]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every analytical result passes through a senior reviewer who decides, by a judgement nobody has written down, whether the integration, the QC and the chromatography can be trusted — and that judgement sets the lab's turnaround, its defensibility and its exposure when the reviewer leaves.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #transfer-learning #compliance #worker-facing

## The Problem
An environmental or food chemistry lab finishes a batch overnight: twenty samples, a method blank, a laboratory control sample, a matrix spike and duplicate, calibration checks, run on a GC-MS or LC-MS/MS. The chromatography data system integrates every peak automatically. Before anything is reported, an analyst checks the batch and a second, more experienced person reviews it. They look at peak shapes, baselines, retention-time shifts, internal standard responses, ion ratios, QC recoveries and the history of the client's site, and for each result decide: accept the automatic integration, reintegrate manually, qualify the result, dilute and rerun, or reject the batch.

Experienced reviewers do this fast and they do it by feel. They know that a shoulder on this analyte in this matrix is a co-eluting interference, not the target. That a slowly rising internal standard response means the source needs cleaning before tomorrow's calibration fails. That a perfect LCS next to a strange spike recovery on one client's samples is matrix, not method. That a particular analyst's manual integrations always clip the tail. None of this is in the SOP, which states acceptance limits but not how to read a chromatogram. It is learned over years of looking at thousands of them, and in most labs it lives in two or three people.

The consequences reach every part of the business. Review is the step that turns a finished batch into a reportable result, so the reviewer queue sets turnaround more than instrument capacity does. Manual integration is where data-integrity findings come from: practitioners recommend that a senior reviewer examine every manual integration, and regulators and assessors treat unexplained reintegration as a red flag. When the senior reviewer is on holiday the queue grows; when they retire, the lab loses its ability to tell a good result from a plausible one. The same pattern holds outside chemistry — an EMC engineer reading a failing emissions scan, a microbiologist reading a plate, a metallographer reading a microstructure — but chromatographic review is where the volume and the risk concentrate.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the reviewer's reasoning is never captured at the moment of decision. The chromatography data system records that a peak was manually integrated, by whom and when, and good practice adds a short justification; it does not record what the reviewer saw. Accepted results leave no trace of the judgement at all, and a rejected batch is often rerun without the original decision being labelled. Building a training set means sitting beside reviewers, or giving them a one-click way to tag the reason for each action, during the busiest part of their day.

The labelling problem is that experts disagree with themselves. Two senior reviewers will treat the same borderline peak differently, and the same reviewer will be stricter after an assessment finding than before. The outcome label is weak and slow: a wrongly accepted result is rarely exposed, except through a later proficiency test failure, a client complaint, a field duplicate that disagrees, or an amended report. Methods, instruments, columns and matrices differ, so a model trained on one method family may not transfer to another.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. A triage tool must be faster than the reviewer's glance, or it will be ignored, and it must never change raw data or an integration on its own — that would itself be a data-integrity violation. Under ISO/IEC 17025, and under 21 CFR Part 11 where pharmaceutical work is involved, any tool that influences release must be validated, and assessors will ask how it was trained. Labs are conservative buyers precisely because the cost of one indefensible certificate is higher than the cost of a slow one.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start with a review record, then a model. For every batch, capture the reviewer's actions — accept, reintegrate, qualify, rerun, reject — with a one-click reason drawn from a short vocabulary that mirrors how reviewers already talk ("co-elution", "baseline drift", "IS suppression", "carryover", "matrix"). Join each decision to the raw traces, the automatic integration, QC results, the instrument's recent history and the eventual outcome: amendments, proficiency results, complaints and field duplicate agreement.

On top of that record, learn the reviewer's read. Models score each peak and each sample for how likely a senior reviewer would intervene and why, rank the batch so the reviewer sees the few suspect results first, and propose clean batches for review by exception, which the reviewer still signs. The same data drives an oversight view: manual integration rates by analyst, method and instrument, and drift that shows up across batches before any single batch fails. Juniors get the senior reviewer's pattern library with examples, which shortens the years it takes to become one.

## Impact If Solved
If review by exception clears even half of clean batches without a full manual pass, a lab where review is the binding step could cut one to two days from routine turnaround (estimate) — the figure environmental and food clients actually choose labs on — and reallocate senior time to the hard cases. A recorded, explainable review trail turns manual integration from the lab's largest data-integrity exposure into its best-documented decision, and keeps the senior reviewer's judgement in the building after they leave.
