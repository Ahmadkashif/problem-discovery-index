# The Distribution Call Only the Senior Trust Officer Can Make

**Industry:** [[trust-companies|Trust Companies]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Trust officers approve or decline discretionary distributions on instruments written decades earlier, and the read that keeps the trustee out of court — which request is ordinary support and which is a beneficiary in trouble — lives in a few senior officers' heads and is never graded against what happened next.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #large-language-models #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #compliance #worker-facing

## The Problem
A beneficiary emails her trust officer: she wants $85,000 for a down payment on a house. The officer pulls the instrument. The distribution standard is "health, education, maintenance and support in her accustomed manner of living," with a clause telling the trustee to consider her other resources and a remainder that passes to her two children. The officer checks what she has been paid in the last five years, what she earns, whether her siblings' shares have been treated the same way, what the tax cost of selling the appreciated stock in the trust would be, and whether a loan from the trust would serve better than a distribution. Then they write a discretionary memorandum and present it to the distribution committee, which approves, trims or declines it. Charles Schwab's posting for a senior trust officer describes exactly this — presenting "discretionary memorandums for review and approval of the Discretionary Distributions Committee" — for an "assigned book of trust accounts." A trust company with a few thousand discretionary trusts handles ~10,000 or more such requests a year (estimate).

The written part is the easy part. What experienced officers know best is not in the memo. That a beneficiary's requests have moved from annual tuition to monthly "emergencies," which is what addiction or a predatory new partner looks like from the trustee's chair. That a widow's sudden request to "help" a new adviser is the first sign of financial exploitation. That approving one sibling's house will produce a written objection from the other within a month. That this family's "education" requests are really support and should be judged under the support standard. That saying yes to a modest request now prevents a petition to the probate court next year. Senior officers describe this as knowing the family; it is pattern recognition built over hundreds of requests and dozens of families, and it is the trust company's real product.

The outcome of each judgement is recorded, but late and in other systems. Distributions sit in the trust accounting ledger; memos sit as PDFs; declines often sit in a phone call nobody logged. Complaints, petitions to remove the trustee, surcharge claims, beneficiaries who exhaust a trust early, and next-generation clients who move the assets when the trust terminates all happen years later, and none of it is joined back to the decision. When a senior officer retires or a trust book is sold, the company keeps the ledger and loses the read.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's reasoning has to be captured while the officer is doing the work, and the officer is drafting a memo against a deadline with a beneficiary waiting. The memo records the instrument language and the recommendation, not the cues that drove it. The cues that matter — the tone of the email, the history of the relationship, the remainder beneficiary's last phone call — are scattered across email, CRM notes and memory. Declined and withdrawn requests, the most informative cases, are the least documented.

The labelling problem is that the honest outcome is rare and slow. Litigation and surcharge affect a small fraction of accounts (estimate) and resolve over years; complaints are noisy; a beneficiary who spends a trust down quickly may have been well served. Experts disagree with themselves: the same officer judges the same request differently after a recent lawsuit, and committees vary with their membership. The requests that reach a memo are already selected, because officers screen out many informally.

The deployment problem is partly speed and partly law. Any assistance must be faster than the officer's own recall — the beneficiary expects an answer within days — or it is ignored. And a trustee must exercise its discretion itself: a system that decides, or that an examiner reads as deciding, is a fiduciary breach. The tool has to inform the officer and the committee, record why they decided, and never decide. Beneficiary files are also customer information under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act and personal family records, so the data cannot be pooled freely across trust companies, which keeps every firm's labelled history small.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A decision ledger first and a model second. Every request becomes a structured record drafted automatically from the email or call note and the officer's memo: request type and amount, the governing standard abstracted from the instrument, the beneficiary's other resources, prior distributions, the remainder beneficiaries' positions, tax effects, the committee's decision and the stated reason. Declines and informal screens are captured in one click. Each record is later joined to what followed: further requests, complaints, objections, petitions, early exhaustion of the trust, and whether the family kept its assets with the company at termination.

On top of the ledger, the system learns the senior officers' reads. It retrieves the most similar past requests under similar instrument language and shows how they were decided and what happened; it flags patterns senior officers recognise — escalating request cadence, exploitation indicators, sibling-equalisation risk — with the evidence attached; and it checks consistency across officers and committee sittings. The officer still decides and the committee still approves; the system makes the reasoning visible, repeatable and reviewable, and lets a new officer inherit a family's history in minutes rather than months.

## Impact If Solved
Cutting memo preparation from ~1–3 hours to ~30 minutes (estimate) across ~10,000 requests a year returns ~5,000–25,000 officer hours at a mid-sized trust company. The larger value is defensive and commercial: consistent, documented discretion is the trustee's best answer to a surcharge claim or an examiner, and catching an exploited or struggling beneficiary early protects both the family and the fee base that depends on the next generation staying.
