# The Claims Coordinator Who Has Never Seen the Worksite

**Industry:** [[professional-employer-organizations|Professional Employer Organizations]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A PEO's workers' comp coordinator is the legal employer's voice on hundreds of injuries at small companies they have never visited, chasing reports, light duty and paperwork between an injured worker, a busy owner and a carrier adjuster.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #bert #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #compliance

## The Problem
When a roofer falls at a 14-person client, or a dental assistant develops a wrist injury, the PEO is the employer for workers' compensation. Its coverage is either a master policy covering every client's workers or a multiple coordinated policy per client. The PEO's claims coordinator must get a first report of injury from the client, ideally within a day (estimate), submit it to the carrier or TPA, and reach the injured worker. Then the long middle: getting medical updates, working out whether the client can offer modified duty, sending the restricted-duty offer letter, tracking when the worker is released, and answering the carrier adjuster's questions about wages and job duties.

The coordinator works through intermediaries at every step. The client owner did not see the accident, is short-handed and wants the worker back at full duty or not at all. The injured worker is frightened about money and calls the coordinator because the PEO's name is on the paystub. The adjuster works for the carrier and holds the medical file. A coordinator may carry well over a hundred open claims across dozens of clients (estimate), each in a different state's comp system with its own forms and deadlines. Behind it all sits the PEO's own money: large PEOs retain the first layer of every claim. Insperity is responsible for the first $1.5M per occurrence under its Chubb programme. So each week of lost time the coordinator fails to prevent is the PEO's cost.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The coordinator has responsibility without control. They cannot inspect the site, cannot create a light-duty job at a company with no desk work, and cannot make an owner return a call. Yet they are measured on lost-time days and claim costs. The emotional load is real: injured workers in pain and out of income, owners who feel the PEO is "costing them", and the occasional case that turns litigious, where every missed date the coordinator owned becomes evidence.

The paperwork never stops. Every state has its own forms, filing windows and wage statements, and the information needed to complete them, such as the worker's average weekly wage, hire date, job duties and concurrent employment, is scattered across payroll, the client and the worker.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A claims workspace that pre-fills each state's injury report from payroll and HR records the PEO already holds, keeps a timeline of every claim's statutory and medical dates, and drafts the next contact for each party: the follow-up to the owner, the modified-duty offer, the plain-language update to the worker. It would suggest realistic transitional duties for the client's industry and size, and flag claims whose pattern (no contact in ten days, a lawyer letter, a disputed wage) predicts escalation, so the coordinator spends their time on the claims that will go bad.

## Impact If Solved
Getting reports in sooner and workers back on modified duty earlier cuts lost-time days, which drive most indemnity cost. A coordinator who stops filling forms by hand can give real attention to the claims that need it, at an estimated several hours a week reclaimed per coordinator (estimate). The PEO keeps more of the deductible layer it already pays for.
