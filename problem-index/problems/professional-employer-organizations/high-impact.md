# The Client the Underwriter Should Have Declined

**Industry:** [[professional-employer-organizations|Professional Employer Organizations]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A PEO's margin is decided when a new-business underwriter admits a 25-person prospect to the master health plan and the workers' comp programme at a price fixed for a year, on a read of the group that no model matches and nobody grades against what the client later cost.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #bayesian-inference #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A broker sends a prospect: a 28-person dental group, a 40-person HVAC contractor, a 19-person software firm moving off an ACA small-group plan after a 30% renewal. The PEO's new-business underwriter gets a census (dates of birth, zip codes, salaries, current coverage tiers), a handful of personal or group health questionnaires, a prescription-history morbidity score, the incumbent carrier's renewal letter, three to five years of workers' comp loss runs and an experience mod, and sometimes the client's unemployment history. Within days, because the broker's quote deadline is real, the underwriter decides three things. Should the client come into the master health plan at all? At what composite rate? And what workers' comp classes and rate per $100 of payroll? Pricing then holds for a year. Insperity's 10-K says one year for most clients and two for the middle market.

Experienced underwriters make this call with a read they cannot fully specify. A census skewed to one family's surname and a recent Humira fill means one thing at a 15-person firm and another at a 60-person firm. A carrier renewal that is high because the carrier is exiting a market differs from one that is high because a claimant is in treatment. An owner who wants to move mid-year, a broker who brings a lot of declines, a contractor whose "office" class payroll looks too big: these are pattern judgements built over thousands of quotes. The PrestigePEO underwriting deck spells out the stakes. The average PEO client has 25-35 eligible employees. Roughly eleven of them are needed to fund each $25,000 of forecast annual claims, so a single specialty drug can make a group unprofitable. In one cross-PEO study, 15% of groups produced half the claims.

The PEO learns whether the underwriter was right only slowly and partially. Health claims arrive over the plan year and sit with the carrier in a fully insured plan. Comp losses develop for years. And close to a fifth of clients leave every year: Insperity's retention ran 81-83% in 2024-2025. Nobody joins the underwriter's reasons at the door to the client's eventual cost. So the PEO cannot say which of its underwriters, which risk flags and which exceptions are actually predictive.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's reasoning is never captured. The underwriting file holds the inputs and the decision (approve, rate-up, decline, approve with a workers' comp-only offer), not why. Overrides granted under sales pressure are often recorded only in email. Capturing the read means asking a busy underwriter, at quote-season peak, to tag the two or three things that drove each call, in seconds rather than minutes.

The labelling problem is severe. A group's first-year loss ratio is a noisy label: the deck reports that for every 165 groups, 25 run unexpectedly well and 25 unexpectedly poorly, and that R-squared on all models is "still relatively low". One premature birth or one cancer diagnosis swamps everything the underwriter saw. The label is also censored: declined groups are never observed, so the data shows only the risks the underwriter accepted, and clients that leave early take their outcome with them. Experts disagree with themselves too. The same underwriter rates a borderline group differently in November, with a plan to fill, than in March. Any honest label needs credibility weighting, pooling across similar groups and a measured test-retest ceiling for the underwriters themselves.

The deployment problem is speed and incentive. A recommendation has to arrive inside the underwriter's existing workbench before the quote goes out, cost the broker no extra day and explain itself in the underwriter's vocabulary. If it only says "decline" it will be overridden by sales leadership exactly when it matters. Privacy adds friction: questionnaire answers and prescription histories are protected health information collected under authorisations, and carriers control much of the claims data.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An acceptance ledger first, a model second. Every quote gets a structured record: inputs, the decision, the price relative to manual rate, any override and who approved it, and the underwriter's two or three driving reasons, drafted automatically from the file and notes and confirmed with a click. Each accepted client is then followed at the client level: health claims net of pooling, workers' comp incurred losses developed to ultimate, unemployment charges, and the date and reason it left.

On the ledger, learn the underwriter's read. Model expected client-level loss ratios with credibility weighting toward similar groups, use reject-inference methods to account for declined groups, and model time-to-termination so early leavers stop biasing the result. Then surface the patterns an experienced underwriter applies, with their track record: "groups like this with this renewal profile ran 118% in year one". The aim is to make the best underwriter's judgement available to the newest one and to the sales leader deciding whether to override, not to automate the decision away.

## Impact If Solved
For a PEO with 100,000 worksite employees, moving a few points of loss ratio on newly admitted groups is worth tens of millions of dollars of gross profit a year (estimate): Insperity's 14% gross profit fall in 2025, from benefits costs, is the scale of the error being priced. It also turns the underwriter's judgement, the industry's least documented asset, into something a PEO keeps when that underwriter retires or leaves for a competitor.
