# The Candidate Assessment Nobody Grades

**Industry:** [[executive-search-firms|Executive Search Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A search firm's product is a partner's judgement that this candidate will succeed in this role and will actually move, and the firm never joins that judgement to the tenure and performance that follow, so the partners whose instincts work cannot be told from those whose instincts do not.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #large-language-models #survival-analysis #gradient-boosting #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
By the time a client meets a shortlist, a consultant has interviewed perhaps ~15–20 candidates (estimate), taken off-list references on the finalists, and written a report on each that ends in a recommendation. The report reads as analysis, but the recommendation rests on things the consultant cannot fully specify. That this CFO's account of a failed integration is too tidy. That a candidate who has worked only for founders will not survive a private-equity sponsor's monthly operating review. That a reference praising "strategic vision" is a polite way of saying the person did not execute. That a candidate who asks about the counteroffer policy in the second interview is not going to resign. Experienced consultants call this pattern recognition, and it is tacit knowledge in exactly the sense the term is used for a craftsman: built over hundreds of searches, expressed as a feel, and impossible to hand to an associate as a rule.

The outcome of every one of these judgements is recorded somewhere, and nowhere the firm looks. For a public company, the departure or demotion of a named executive officer is disclosed on a Form 8-K within four business days, and the appointment of the successor follows. For private companies, a press release, a sponsor's portfolio page or a changed biography tells the same story more slowly. The Center for Creative Leadership puts outright failure of new executives in the first 18 months at between 38% and more than half. Yet search firms measure placements against the guarantee period, commonly six to twelve months, and against the client's satisfaction at acceptance. Heidrick & Struggles reported 418 search consultants and an average fee of $146,000 per search in 2024; Korn Ferry opened more than 6,300 engagements in fiscal 2025. Across that volume, no firm publishes, or as far as public material shows, keeps, a graded record of which consultants' recommendations survived.

The consequence is that the firm cannot say which parts of its process carry information. It cannot tell whether its assessment frameworks predict anything, whether a consultant's "strong yes" means more than another's "yes", or whether the derailers it flags actually derail. When a senior partner retires, the pattern recognition that won the firm its CEO practice retires too, and the CRM keeps the candidate records without the reasoning.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the reasoning has to be captured from the expert at the moment of assessment. Consultants write candidate reports for the client, not for the firm, so the report is persuasive rather than diagnostic: it argues for the candidate and leaves out the doubts that drove the ranking. The doubts surface in the debrief call with the associate, in the margin of the interview notes, or not at all. A system that asks the consultant to fill in a structured form after every interview competes with the next flight and will be ignored.

The labelling problem has three layers. Tenure is a noisy label for quality: an executive leaves after a takeover, a strategy change or a new CEO's arrival for reasons unrelated to the assessment, and a poor hire can survive for years in a forgiving organisation. Experts disagree with themselves: the same consultant reads the same candidate differently after a recent placement failed, and two partners interviewing one candidate often rank them differently. And the sample is selected: the firm only observes outcomes for candidates who were hired, so its record says nothing directly about the candidates it screened out. Honest grading needs dated, structured assessments on candidates who were presented and not hired, with their later careers followed too.

The deployment problem is cultural and commercial. Partners are paid on origination and placement, and a scorecard of their recommendations is a scorecard of them. Clients may object to their executives' assessments being reused, even in aggregate. And any assistance must arrive inside the interview cycle — before the debrief, before the shortlist goes out — and be quicker than the consultant's own recall, or it is noise.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An assessment ledger first and a model second. Every interview, reference and recommendation is attached to a short structured record — the competencies assessed against the brief, the evidence for each, the concerns, the confidence, the predicted risk — drafted automatically from the consultant's own notes and call transcripts and confirmed in seconds. Placed candidates are then followed through public filings, press releases and biography changes at 6, 12, 18 and 36 months; presented-but-not-hired candidates are followed too, giving a counterfactual of sorts. Departures are coded by reason where it can be inferred, so that a takeover is not counted against the assessment.

On top of the ledger, learn the expert's recurring reads. Pair interview and reference text with the consultant's own annotations ("too polished", "founder-dependent", "won't resign") and the later outcome, and surface those patterns, with their track record, to the associate writing the next report and the partner inheriting the client. The goal is not to replace the consultant's judgement but to make the best consultants' patterns visible, testable and transferable.

## Impact If Solved
A firm that can show a client its placement survival record by role, sector and ownership type has a sales argument no competitor without the history can match, in a market where Korn Ferry says more than 83% of fiscal 2025 assignments came from clients served in the prior three years. Internally, a firm that knows which assessment signals predict an 18-month failure can avoid some of the replacement searches its guarantees force it to run for free. If ~10–15 of every 100 placements fail inside a 12-month guarantee (estimate) and the ledger lets the firm avoid a fifth of them, that is ~2–3 free replacement searches saved per 100 placements, worth ~$0.3–0.45M of consultant capacity at Heidrick's 2024 average of $146,000 per search (estimate) — before counting the client relationships those failures put at risk. And the firm keeps a retiring partner's judgement on record instead of losing it with them.
