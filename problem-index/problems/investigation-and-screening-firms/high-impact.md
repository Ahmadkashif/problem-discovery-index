# The Surveillance Day That Comes Back Empty

**Industry:** [[investigation-and-screening-firms|Investigation & Background Screening Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Insurers buy claimant surveillance by the day on an adjuster's hunch, the veteran investigator's sense of when and where a subject will move is never recorded, and nobody can say which assignments were worth ordering until the invoice arrives.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #causal-inference #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A workers' compensation adjuster with a claimant who says they cannot lift more than ten pounds, an auto liability examiner facing a soft-tissue demand, a disability carrier reviewing a long-term claim: each can order surveillance. The order goes to an investigation firm, usually for one to three days at an hourly rate with a cap the carrier sets (an estimated ~$65–125 an hour plus mileage), timed ahead of an independent medical exam, a deposition, a hearing or a settlement conference. An investigator drives to the claimant's address before dawn, sets up where they can see the door without being seen, and waits. If the claimant leaves, the investigator follows, films what they do and writes it up. If the claimant does not, the carrier pays for ten hours of a closed front door.

Whether a day produces anything depends on judgements the investigator makes before and during it. Which address the claimant actually sleeps at. Whether this subject works a cash job on weekday mornings or never goes out before noon. Whether the car in the driveway means they are home. Whether to sit through a quiet afternoon or end the day and save the hours for Saturday, when the claimant's social media shows a fishing trip planned. Whether the subject has noticed the van. Experienced surveillance investigators are good at this in the way experienced hunters are — they "get a feel for a subject" within a few hours and can tell a junior where to park on a street they have never seen. They cannot write the rules down, and the firm that employs them keeps no record of the reasoning, only the hours and the footage.

On the carrier side the decision is just as unrecorded. One claims administrator's own report gave case-level activity rates of 72–83% across states, which says nothing about how many individual days were empty or how many cases with activity changed anything. Adjusters order surveillance from habit and suspicion; special investigation units approve it against budgets; the result goes into the claim file and is rarely joined back to the ordering decision. Insurers spend heavily on a service whose yield is invisible to the people buying it and whose best practitioners leave with their judgement intact.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's decisions are made alone in a car and recorded, if at all, as a narrative log written that night: "0612 arrived, observed silver sedan in driveway; 1430 no activity, terminated." The reasons for a vantage point, a pre-dawn start, a decision to stop at 2:30 or to come back on Saturday are not in the log. Capturing them means asking a tired investigator to record intent at the moment of decision, on a phone, without breaking cover.

The labelling problem is that "activity" is a poor label. A claimant filmed carrying groceries may or may not contradict the medical restrictions; the adjuster and the defence attorney often disagree on what footage is worth, and the same reviewer grades similar footage differently depending on the claim. The outcome that matters — whether the evidence changed the claim's cost or result — arrives months later, if the carrier records it at all, and is confounded by everything else that happened to the claim. Experts disagree with themselves on whether a quiet day was bad luck or a bad plan.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. A recommendation that arrives after the investigator has chosen a parking spot is useless, and one that tells a twenty-year veteran where to sit will be ignored unless it is visibly right. On the carrier side, an assignment score must be available when the adjuster is deciding, inside the claim system, and must not read as a fraud accusation, because unfair claims practices rules constrain acting on suspicion alone.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A structured assignment record first and a model second. Each assignment carries the facts known at ordering (claim type, restrictions, injury, upcoming exam and hearing dates, address confidence, household, vehicles, prior surveillance), the investigator's plan and the decisions made in the field (start time, vantage, stay or terminate, reasons in a word or two by voice), and the result graded on a short shared scale by the investigator and the adjuster. Logs and video timestamps fill in most of it automatically.

On that record, a model predicts the probability and likely timing of meaningful activity for a given subject on a given day — a time-to-event problem within the day and across days — and recommends whether to order, which days to run and when to start. A second layer learns the veteran's pattern: subjects whose profiles resemble those a senior investigator read correctly, surfaced to the junior on the next assignment as "subjects like this one moved before 7 a.m. on weekdays." The carrier sees yield per hour by vendor, claim type and timing rather than invoices alone.

## Impact If Solved
If even a fifth of empty surveillance days could be avoided or moved to a better window, carriers would save that share of a budget estimated at ~$1.5–2.5B a year across the industry while getting more usable evidence before the decisions it was bought for. For investigation firms, a documented, transferable read of subjects turns the most experienced investigator's judgement into something a firm keeps when that investigator retires, and turns a commodity hourly service into one bought on demonstrated yield.
