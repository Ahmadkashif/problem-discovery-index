# The Furnisher File That Passes Every Edit and Is Still Wrong

**Industry:** [[credit-bureaus|Credit Bureaus]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A bureau's load edits catch malformed furnisher files and pass well-formed wrong ones, so the accuracy of millions of credit files depends on a few data-reporting analysts who know what each furnisher's file normally looks like and cannot write that knowledge down.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #autoencoders #change-point-detection #gradient-boosting #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #compliance #data-integration

## The Problem
Every month a nationwide bureau receives Metro 2 files from thousands of furnishers (on the order of ~10,000, an estimate). A large card issuer's file carries tens of millions of account records; a credit union's carries a few thousand. Each record states the account's balance, credit limit, account status code, the 24-month payment history profile, the date of first delinquency, special comments, the consumer's identifying data and the ECOA code saying whose debt it is. The bureau's load process applies format and logic edits — a closed account with a balance, a date in the future, a status that contradicts the payment history — and returns reject and warning reports to the furnisher.

The edits are good at malformed data and blind to plausible data. The failures that do the most damage are file-wide and internally consistent. A mortgage servicer migrates its platform and every delinquent account's date of first delinquency resets to the conversion date, which silently extends the seven-year reporting window on thousands of consumers. A student loan servicer changes how it reports deferment and its deferred accounts flip from current to a status that scoring models read differently. A collection agency buys a portfolio and starts reporting accounts the seller still reports. A furnisher's file arrives with, say, a third fewer records than last month because one product line was dropped from the extract. Every record in these files passes the edits.

The people who catch them are data-reporting and data-quality analysts. An experienced analyst looks at a furnisher's load summary — record counts by status, balance distributions, the share of accounts newly delinquent — and "knows" that this month's file is wrong, or that a large shift is real (a hurricane-zone forbearance programme, a portfolio sale announced last quarter). That read is built from years of watching the same furnishers, knowing which ones convert systems in the autumn, which servicers report disaster accommodations correctly, and which collection agencies re-age. It is tacit knowledge in the plain sense: pattern recognition over hundreds of files that the analyst cannot reduce to a rule, or the bureau would already have the rule in its edits.

When the analyst misses a load, or never sees it because the furnisher is small, the error surfaces months later as disputes, complaints and lawsuits — one consumer at a time, long after it entered every lender's decisions.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the analyst's judgement leaves almost no trace. When a load is held, the record is a ticket — "held, contacted furnisher, resubmitted" — not the features the analyst looked at or the reason the shift looked wrong. When a load is accepted, nothing is recorded at all, so the system never learns what the analyst decided was normal. Capturing the expertise means instrumenting the review itself: logging what the analyst examined, what they flagged and why, at the moment they do it, during a monthly cycle in which hundreds of files arrive within days of each other.

The labelling problem is that ground truth arrives late, partially and with bias. A bad load is confirmed when the furnisher sends a mass correction or when disputes cluster on that furnisher months later, but disputes over-represent errors that hurt consumers and that consumers notice. A file-wide shift can be correct (a genuine forbearance programme) or wrong in ways nobody ever disputes (an error that raises scores). Analysts also disagree with themselves: the same shift reads as alarming after a recent bad conversion and as noise in a quiet month, so a labelled sample needs repeated review to estimate how consistent the expert actually is.

The deployment problem is the load window. Files must post quickly because lenders pull reports every second, and holding a large furnisher's load leaves millions of files a month stale. A model that flags too many files will be ignored within a cycle; one that cannot explain which field moved, for which segment, against which history, cannot be acted on faster than the analyst's own look at the summary.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A load review record first and a model second. Every furnisher load is profiled at the field and segment level against that furnisher's own history — distributions of status codes, payment-history transitions, delinquency dates, balances and record counts — and against peers of the same type. The analyst's decision on each flagged file (accept, hold, contact, correct) and a one-line reason are captured in the review screen, not in a separate ticket.

On top of that record, learn the analyst's read. Unsupervised models of each furnisher's normal file surface distribution shifts and change points; a supervised layer trained on past holds, mass corrections and later dispute clusters learns which shifts experienced analysts treated as errors and which as real events. The output is a ranked queue of loads with the specific fields and segments that moved, the closest past case and what the analyst did then — available for every furnisher, including the small ones no analyst currently looks at.

## Impact If Solved
Catching a single bad load from a large servicer before it posts prevents errors in hundreds of thousands of files (illustrative) and the disputes, complaints and litigation that follow them; extending review to the long tail of small furnishers addresses the part of the file nobody checks today. For an industry whose CFPB complaint volume more than doubled in a year and whose largest regulator now asks consumers to dispute with the bureau first, moving accuracy upstream from the dispute queue to the load is the most valuable change available.
