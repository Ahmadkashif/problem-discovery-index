# The Billposter on the Catwalk

**Industry:** [[outdoor-advertising-companies|Outdoor Advertising Companies]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The billposter gets back daylight, fewer return trips and a structure history that warns them before they climb onto the catwalk that is failing.
**Tags:** #cnns #object-detection #optimization-fundamentals #dynamic-programming #worker-facing #automation #quick-win

## The Problem
Posting crews change the copy on static faces: rolling vinyl onto bulletins, pasting paper on posters, replacing lamps and fixing torn faces. They work from a route sheet and a stack of vinyls that has to match it. A crew may cover ~10–25 poster panels or a handful of bulletins in a day (estimate), with the busiest days bunched at the start of each sales period, when every contract starts at once. Each stop means finding the structure, sometimes behind a landowner's locked gate or past a dog. Then they climb a ladder to a catwalk ~20–60 feet up (estimate), clip into a lifeline, install in whatever wind the day brings, photograph the finished face for proof of posting and drive on. A wrong vinyl on the wrong face, a missing photo or a face posted a day late becomes a makegood that someone in the office will blame on the crew.

The danger is real and old. NIOSH's investigation of a 2000 Oklahoma death found a crew chief fell about 30 feet from a catwalk without standard guardrails at a company whose fall protection policy was not enforced. Ontario issued an order after a billboard maintenance worker's fatal fall in Toronto in 2025. The structures are mostly decades old, and what a crew learns about a bad catwalk, a loose ladder or a rotten platform usually stays in that crew's heads.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Billposting is skilled, physical, outdoor work that pays by the job or the hour. The worst parts are not the climbing itself. They are the wasted trips: the vinyl that didn't arrive, the gate code nobody passed on, the face that was already posted, the photo the office rejected so the crew has to go back. Then there are the nights finishing a route to hit a start date. A billposter who knows a structure is sketchy has no reliable way to warn the next crew, and no record that they raised it if something goes wrong.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A crew app that builds the day's route from contract start dates, vinyl arrival, weather windows for wind and rain, and structure access notes. It carries each structure's history: catwalk and ladder condition from past photos, landlord contact and gate details, hazards flagged by previous crews. At each stop a photo is checked on the spot against the expected creative and face ID, so a wrong posting is caught before the crew leaves, and the same photo is screened for structural problems such as corrosion, missing guardrail sections or lamps out. Hazards a crew flags reach the next crew and the maintenance queue automatically, and lone-worker check-ins are part of the climb.

## Impact If Solved
Fewer return trips and rejected proofs give crews back hours each week in the start-of-period crush. Structure hazards get recorded and fixed rather than remembered. The crew's photo, already required for proof of posting, becomes the company's main source of structure condition data, so a billposter's warning about a failing catwalk counts for something.
