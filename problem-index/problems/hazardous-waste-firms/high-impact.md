# The Profile That Doesn't Match the Drum

**Industry:** [[hazardous-waste-firms|Hazardous Waste Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A TSDF's approval chemist decides from a generator's paperwork whether a waste stream is what it claims to be, and the judgement that catches the bad profiles before the truck arrives lives in a few senior chemists' heads, untested against what the receiving lab later finds.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #large-language-models #transformers #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #compliance #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Before any hazardous waste moves to a treatment, storage and disposal facility (TSDF), the generator submits a waste profile: the process that produced the stream, the safety data sheets for what went into it, analytical results or a statement of generator knowledge, the RCRA waste codes it claims, the DOT shipping description and the expected container and volume. At a commercial incinerator or treatment plant, an approval chemist in technical services reads it and decides three things. Does it fit the facility's permit and Waste Analysis Plan? Which treatment train should take it, such as direct burn, fuel blending, stabilisation before landfill or wastewater treatment? And what conditions go on the approval? A busy approval desk reviews dozens of new and recertified profiles a day (estimate), and many TSDFs require each profile to be recertified every year (estimate).

The paperwork is where the risk enters. The generator certifies the profile, but the person filling it in is often an EHS coordinator copying last year's form, a plant that has changed a solvent or a lab clearing out a stockroom. When the load arrives, the receiving lab samples it and fingerprints it against the profile. One EPA training example samples a tenth of the containers on every shipment and every bulk load (search summary). A mismatch becomes a discrepancy that must be resolved or reported, a load held at the gate or sent back, a re-profile, and sometimes an off-spec feed event at the incinerator or a treatment residue that fails its land-disposal standard. Handlers ship ~2 million manifested loads a year (EPA), so even a small mismatch rate is thousands of held loads.

Experienced approval chemists catch a share of these before the truck rolls, and they cannot fully say how. They know that a certain plant's "spent solvent" always carries more water than its profile says, that a profile citing generator knowledge with no analytics for a reactive-sounding process deserves a call, that a sudden volume jump on a recertified profile usually means a process change nobody wrote down, and that a lab-cleanout profile from a particular university will hold surprises. This is pattern recognition built over thousands of profiles and the loads that followed them, and it is the kind of judgement a junior chemist takes years to acquire. When a senior approval chemist retires or moves to a competitor, the TSDF keeps the profile archive and loses the read.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's reasoning is barely recorded. An approval is logged as approved, approved with conditions, or rejected, with a line of notes at best; the phone call to the generator that changed the decision is not in the system. Joining the approval to its consequences means linking four records that live in different places: the profile in the TSDF's ERP (Terralink's TDX, WASTELINQ or a consolidator's own system), the fingerprint and full-analysis results in the LIMS, the discrepancy and rejection record now filed through e-Manifest, and the treatment outcome in operations logs. Few facilities have ever joined them, and capturing the chemist's reasoning going forward has to cost her seconds, not minutes, inside an approval queue that is already the bottleneck.

The labelling challenge is that the outcome is partial and selected. Only the fingerprinted subset of containers is tested, so a clean receipt is weak evidence that the profile was right. Profiles the chemist rejected never ship, so their outcomes are never observed. And experts disagree with themselves: the same chemist applies a looser read at the end of a quarter with the incinerator short of feed than in a week when the tank farm is full, and two chemists at the same desk will condition the same profile differently. Labels need repeated review of a sample of profiles to measure that disagreement before a model is judged against it.

The deployment challenge is speed and authority. The Waste Analysis Plan fixes the acceptance procedure in the permit, so a model cannot change what gets tested. It can only change what the chemist looks at first, and it must surface its concern faster than the chemist's own recall of the generator. Customers measure the TSDF on approval speed, so anything that slows the queue will be switched off.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A profile ledger first and a model second. Every profile, recertification and amendment is stored as structured fields (claimed codes, constituents and ranges, generator-knowledge basis, process description, volume, container) linked to the generator's history, the receiving lab's fingerprint results for every load shipped under it, every discrepancy, rejection and re-profile, and the treatment outcome. The ledger is built from records the facility already keeps and shows the chemist, on one screen, how every past load under a profile compared with its paperwork.

On top of the ledger, learn the senior chemists' read. A model scores each incoming profile for the probability that loads under it will be discrepant or rejected, and points to the fields and history driving the score: the generator's past mismatch rate, an implausible change from last year's version, a constituent listed on an SDS but missing from the profile, or a generator-knowledge claim on a process that historically needed analytics. The chemist still decides and the Waste Analysis Plan still governs testing. The model orders the queue, drafts the questions to send the generator, and records which concern the chemist acted on, which builds the labels the next version needs.

## Impact If Solved
A held or rejected bulk load costs ~$2,000–5,000 in freight, demurrage, re-handling and re-profiling (estimate). A large TSDF receiving ~40,000 loads a year with ~3% discrepant (estimate) carries ~1,200 such events. Catching a third of them at approval would save ~$1–2M a year per facility (estimate), and would also cut the off-spec feed events and failed residues that put the permit at risk. Keeping a retiring chemist's read in the ledger protects the most expensive knowledge a TSDF holds.
