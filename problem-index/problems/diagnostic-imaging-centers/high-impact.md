# The Protocol Read: Turning a One-Line Order Into the Right Exam

**Industry:** [[diagnostic-imaging-centers|Diagnostic Imaging Centers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced radiologist reads a one-line order and a patchy history and knows which exam and protocol will answer the question; nobody records that judgement, so the centre repeats scans, denies on mismatched authorizations and loses the skill when the protocoller retires.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #transformers #large-language-models #bert #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
An outpatient centre receives a few hundred advanced-imaging orders a day (estimate), many by fax or an e-referral that copies the fax. Each carries a CPT code, an ICD-10 code and a line of free text: "MRI brain, headache", "CT abdomen/pelvis, pain", "MRI L-spine, radiculopathy, prior surgery". Before the patient arrives someone must turn that into a protocol: which sequences, which slice coverage, contrast or none, which coil, whether the patient needs sedation or a creatinine check, and whether the order is even the right exam. At most centres a radiologist does this in batches between reads, at perhaps 20–40 seconds an order (estimate), or a lead technologist does it from a laminated binder.

The best protocollers carry a body of judgement nobody wrote down. A history of "headache, worse lying down, vision changes" in a young woman means a venogram, not a routine brain. A lumbar spine with prior surgery needs contrast to separate scar from recurrent disc. "Rule out stroke" ordered for an outpatient on day ten is not an emergency protocol. "Pain" on a CT order from an oncology practice means the staging protocol, with the chest included. A shoulder MRI from a particular orthopaedic surgeon means an arthrogram, because that is what he always wants and never writes. Some orders are simply wrong: a CT for a question only MRI can answer, or a duplicate of a study done last month elsewhere.

When the judgement is missing, the cost lands in three places. The patient is called back for a second scan, or the study goes to the radiologist unable to answer the question, and the report says so. The authorization, obtained for the order as written, no longer matches the exam performed, and the claim denies. And the most experienced protocollers, often the same radiologists the shortage is stretching thinnest, spend minutes of every hour on clerical triage that a junior cannot safely take over.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the protocol decision is recorded and the reasoning is not. The RIS stores the protocol code chosen and sometimes a technologist's note. It does not store what in the history drove the choice, which alternatives were considered, or what the radiologist would have changed after seeing the images. Capturing the expert means logging the decision at the moment it is made, in a protocolling screen that radiologists already find slow, without adding a click.

The labelling problem is that "right protocol" has no single answer and the outcome label is buried. Radiologists in the same group protocol the same order differently and change their own minds; site preferences, scanner capabilities and referring physicians' habits all shift the answer. The honest label is whether the study answered the clinical question without a repeat, recall or addendum. That signal is scattered across report text, later orders for the same patient and technologist notes, and it is confounded: a good protocoller prevents the failure that would have proved them right.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. A protocol suggestion has to appear in the worklist the radiologist already uses, be right often enough to accept with one keystroke, and abstain clearly when it is unsure, which for contrast decisions in patients with renal disease or prior reactions must mean deferring to a human. It must be faster than the expert's glance, or it will be ignored, and wrong suggestions on contrast are a patient-safety matter, not a nuisance. The federal push that would have helped, CMS's Appropriate Use Criteria programme, was paused and its regulations rescinded from 1 January 2024. No outside rule now makes appropriateness a recorded decision at the centre.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A protocol ledger first, then a model. Every order is parsed into structured fields (exam, laterality, contrast, indication, relevant history, referring physician, prior imaging) and joined to the protocol chosen, the technologist's in-scan changes, the final report and anything that followed: an addendum, a recall, a repeat order within 30 days, or a protocol-change note. That join is the label set the industry has never assembled.

On top of it, a model proposes a protocol for each incoming order with a calibrated confidence and the evidence behind it ("prior lumbar fusion in 2023 noted in referral; group protocol: with and without contrast"). High-confidence routine orders are protocolled for one-click radiologist sign-off. Low-confidence or safety-sensitive ones (contrast in reduced kidney function, implants, paediatric sedation) are routed to the senior protocoller with the reason flagged. The same pass checks the order against the guideline the patient's radiology benefit manager will apply, which is licensed content or, for Medicare Advantage, Medicaid and federally facilitated exchange plans from 2027, documentation requirements read from the payer's Prior Authorization API. It also checks against the ACR Appropriateness Criteria where the centre holds permission to use them. It flags orders likely to be the wrong exam before the authorization is requested, not after the scan.

## Impact If Solved
If routine orders were auto-protocolled for sign-off, a five-radiologist group protocolling ~600 orders a day would recover on the order of 3–5 radiologist-hours daily (estimate), the equivalent of reading another 40–80 studies. Catching protocol mismatches before the scan would cut callbacks and authorization denials for "exam performed differs from exam authorized", each of which costs a full scanner slot or a written-off technical fee. Keeping senior protocollers' judgement as a graded, reusable record means it no longer leaves when they retire or move groups.
