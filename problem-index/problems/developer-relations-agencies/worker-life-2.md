# The Community Manager Absorbing the Support Queue

**Industry:** [[developer-relations-agencies|Developer Relations Agencies]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Developers ask their questions where the humans are, so the community channel becomes an unofficial support desk staffed by someone with no support tooling and no ticket queue.
**Tags:** #bert #large-language-models #gradient-boosting #k-nearest-neighbors #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
A developer community exists for discussion, and what it receives is support requests. A developer with a broken integration posts in the community channel rather than opening a ticket, because a human replied there last time and the support form did not.

The community manager handles them. Without a ticket system, so nothing is tracked. Without service level structure, so everything is immediate. Without escalation paths, so a genuine product bug is relayed by pasting a message into an engineering channel and hoping. And without the visibility that support functions have, so the volume they are absorbing does not appear in any staffing calculation.

The questions repeat heavily. The same setup problem, the same misunderstood configuration, the same version incompatibility, asked weekly by different people, answered fully each time because the person asking deserves an answer.

And it never stops, because the community is global. Questions arrive overnight, and a channel where the last three questions went unanswered signals to a newcomer that nobody is home.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is a support function performed without support resourcing, by someone whose role was defined as something else, and whose absorbed volume is invisible to the organisation precisely because it is not ticketed.

The always-on expectation is the sharpest part. Community managers describe checking channels before sleeping and on waking because an unanswered question ages badly in public, and there is usually no rotation because the team is one or two people.

The repetition is demoralising in the specific way that answering the same question for the fortieth time is, particularly when the person knows the durable fix — a documentation page, a better error message, a setup change — and has no route to make it happen. They are the person with the clearest view of where the product confuses people and the least ability to act on it.

And emotional labour runs through all of it: frustrated developers, occasionally rude ones, in public, with the community's tone depending on how the manager responds.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Answer the repeated questions automatically, grounded in the documentation and the community's own answer history, with a visible deferral to a human when the question is outside what can be answered confidently. In a technical community a wrong answer is worse than none, because the person acting on it will lose an afternoon.

Route the rest properly. Classifying incoming messages into support issue, bug, feature request or discussion, and creating the appropriate ticket or issue automatically, gives the community manager a real escalation path and makes the absorbed volume visible for the first time.

Turn repetition into documentation. A question asked twenty times is a ranked documentation gap, and drafting the page from the community's own answers is the fix that stops it recurring — the loop this role can see and cannot currently close.

Distribute the answering. Identifying community members who answer well, recognising them, and routing questions matched to their expertise builds the self-sufficiency that determines whether a community scales past its staff.

And make coverage a schedule rather than an obligation. Explicit rotation, with automated coverage for the hours nobody is on, replaces the personal habit of checking at midnight.

## Impact If Solved
Community managers absorb a support function that nobody counts, with no tooling, no escalation and no off-hours coverage. Grounded automatic answering with honest deferral, proper classification and routing, and converting repeated questions into documentation address the volume, the invisibility and the repetition together — and distributing the answering across the community is what turns a staffed help desk into a community that works.
