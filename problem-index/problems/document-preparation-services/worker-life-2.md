# Transcribing Evidence, Verbatim, All Day

**Industry:** [[document-preparation-services|Document Preparation Services]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Evidentiary transcriptionists listen to violence, grief and abuse word for word for hours, with no control over exposure and no safeguarding, so the work that most needs an experienced ear burns out the people who have one.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
A transcriptionist on a law enforcement or insurance account works through a queue of recordings the client needs in writing: a body-worn camera clip from an arrest, a recorded interview with a child witness, a 911 call, a jail phone call that may contain a threat, an insurer's recorded statement from someone describing a fatal crash. The transcript must be verbatim, with every speaker attributed, every pause and inaudible passage marked, often certified as true and accurate, because a prosecutor, defence lawyer or claims investigator will rely on it and may have to defend it in court.

That standard forces the worst kind of listening. The transcriptionist cannot skim. A scream, a threat or a description of injuries has to be replayed until every word is right, often a dozen times, because the poorest audio usually comes from the worst moments. Files arrive without warning about their content, and the next one in the queue could be routine or harrowing. Most of this work is done alone at home by contractors paid per audio minute, with no debrief, no colleague, and no way to switch to something else when a file is too much.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The cost is psychological and cumulative. A qualitative study of transcriptionists, Kiyimba and O'Reilly's grounded-theory work on secondary traumatic stress, found emotional distress, feelings of helplessness and a profession with no safeguarding protocols. Its authors recommended bringing transcriptionists inside the research team and its safeguards. Commercial evidentiary work carries the same exposure at much higher volume, and the transcriptionist usually sits outside any team at all.

It also sets a ceiling on careers. The people who get good at this work, who can untangle overlapping voices on a bodycam recording and know an agency's conventions cold, are the ones most exposed to it, and many leave. The agency then loses its most defensible transcripts along with the people who produced them.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A work environment that treats exposure as something to manage. A content screen runs before assignment, using the machine draft and audio cues to flag violence, distress and child victims, so transcriptionists know what a file holds and can choose, or be rotated, rather than being ambushed. Exposure is tracked per person over the day and week, with limits the coordinator can see. Tools cut repeated listening by putting the machine draft, a confidence map and speaker separation in front of the human, so the replays go to the words that are genuinely uncertain. Access to debriefing and counselling is built into the contract price instead of left to the individual.

## Impact If Solved
A transcriptionist who knows what is coming, can limit their exposure and spends their replays on the genuinely uncertain seconds gets back some control over a job that currently offers none. Aiming replays with a confidence map and a second listener could cut repeated listening on hard segments by ~30–50% (estimate), roughly 1–2 fewer hours a day of exposure to distressing audio across an 8-hour shift (estimate), with no loss of accuracy. Experienced evidentiary transcriptionists staying longer is also the cheapest quality gain an agency can buy, because their accuracy on the hardest audio is what makes a transcript survive cross-examination.
