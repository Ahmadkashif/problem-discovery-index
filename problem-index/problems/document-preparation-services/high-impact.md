# The Editor Who Knows What the Doctor Meant

**Industry:** [[document-preparation-services|Document Preparation Services]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced healthcare documentation editor corrects a speech-recognition draft by knowing how this physician talks and what is clinically plausible, and that judgement, logged in every edit, is thrown away the moment the report is signed.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #transformers #seq2seq #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #worker-facing #compliance

## The Problem
A radiologist dictates forty reports between cases, fast and in a reading-room monotone, with the dictation microphone half-covered. Back-end speech recognition turns each one into a draft, and a healthcare documentation specialist, the job once called medical transcriptionist, listens and corrects it. The draft is usually fluent and occasionally dangerous. "Hypo" heard as "hyper", "no evidence of" dropped before a finding, left heard as right, fifteen milligrams heard as fifty, a drug name the recogniser has never seen turned into an English word. The editor catches these, and the best editors catch them in a way they cannot fully explain.

What experienced editors carry is knowledge of a specific speaker and a specific body of medicine. This orthopaedist always says "the patient tolerated the procedure well" whether or not he says it. That cardiologist mumbles her negatives, and the draft will keep dropping them. This ED physician dictates the HPI out of order and expects it reassembled. A phrase that sounds right is anatomically impossible for the procedure named in the header. Editors describe it as learning an author: after a few hundred reports from one dictator they hear what was meant through what was said. AHDI's own guidance tells editors to use "contextual clues", reference "previous reports" and re-listen. That is an instruction to apply exactly this tacit pattern recognition, which the guidance cannot specify.

The outcome of every one of these judgements is recorded with unusual precision: the machine draft and the edited final are both stored, so every correction is a logged diff. Quality is then graded on a random sample of reports against AHDI's 2025 best practices: a minimum score of 98.0, three points off for a critical error that could affect patient safety, one point for a noncritical error. But the diffs are not mined for what the editor knew, the QA sample covers a few percent of reports, and when an experienced editor leaves the vendor, which happens constantly in a shrinking piece-rate trade (BLS projects 4% fewer jobs by 2035), her knowledge of two hundred dictators leaves too. The new editor relearns them one critical error at a time.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem looks solved and isn't. The diff records what changed, never why. An edit that fixes a dictator-specific habit, an edit that corrects a recogniser error any listener would catch, and an edit made because the editor judged the clinical content implausible all look the same in the log. Neither does the diff record the edits not made: where the editor heard something doubtful, checked a prior report and left the draft alone, or flagged the passage to the physician as unclear. Capturing the reason means asking the expert at the moment of the edit, inside a production queue where she is paid per line and every second of explanation costs her money.

The labelling problem is that experts disagree with themselves and with each other. Two QA auditors scoring the same report against the same AHDI categories will not always assign the same severity, which is why the best-practice document spends pages on auditor consistency. An editor who flags a passage on Monday may let the same phrasing through on Friday at the end of a quota. Ground truth is sometimes unknowable: if the audio is genuinely ambiguous, the "correct" text is whatever the physician confirms when they sign, and many sign without reading. Severity labels also depend on context the editor may not see, such as whether the misheard dose would have been caught downstream.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. Editors work in a queue under a turnaround clock, at a few hundred lines an hour. Help that arrives as a suggestion they must evaluate slows them down. It is only adopted if it is faster than the editor's own ear and right often enough that she stops double-checking it. It also has to respect who owns the audio: each health system's recordings are protected health information held under a business-associate agreement, so a model that learns across accounts needs permission most contracts never anticipated.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An editing environment that treats the edit as data. As the editor works, the system classifies each correction into a small set of reasons — recogniser error, dictator habit, clinical-plausibility catch, formatting or template rule — inferred from the edit pattern and confirmed with a single keystroke only when it is uncertain. Over a few weeks it builds a profile of each dictator: the phrases they drop, the negatives they swallow, the template they expect, the drug and device names they use. It pre-applies the high-confidence habits and highlights the passages where this dictator's drafts have historically needed a clinical catch.

On top of the profiles sits a plausibility layer: a model that reads the draft against the report type, laterality, procedure and medication context and flags statements an experienced editor would question. It does not rewrite them; it points to the place and plays the audio. Every flag and every edit is scored against AHDI severity in the background, so QA becomes a review of the cases the model and the editor disagree on, not a random sample. When an editor leaves, her dictator profiles stay with the account.

## Impact If Solved
A documentation vendor that captures its editors' dictator and clinical knowledge can bring a new editor to the accuracy of a veteran on a familiar account in days rather than months. It can also concentrate its scarce human attention on the passages where critical errors actually occur, the errors that fail a report and can hurt a patient. With speech-recognition editing paid at a few cents a line, the gain is margin and survival for the vendor, a defensible quality record for the health system, and for the editor a working day spent on judgement rather than on fixing the same dropped negative for the four hundredth time.
