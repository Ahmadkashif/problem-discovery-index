# Matching Assistants to Clients

**Industry:** [[virtual-assistant-services|Virtual Assistant Services]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Matching is done by a recruiter reading a profile and a client brief, and a mismatch costs three months of context-building before anyone admits it.
**Tags:** #contrastive-learning #gradient-boosting #bert #k-nearest-neighbors #confidence-intervals #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #word-embeddings

## The Problem
A client describes what they need and an agency proposes an assistant. The match is made by a recruiter from a skills profile, a timezone requirement, an hourly rate and an impression from an interview.

The things that determine whether it works are mostly not in the profile. Communication style — how much initiative the client wants taken versus how much confirmation they want sought — is the most common cause of failure and is rarely assessed on either side. Tolerance for ambiguity, industry familiarity, the specific software the client lives in, and whether the client is capable of briefing clearly all matter more than a skills list.

When a match fails it fails slowly. Both sides invest months in building context before concluding it is not working, and the replacement restarts from zero. Agencies treat replacement as a service feature, which it is, and as a routine cost, which it is for them — the cost falls on the client's time and on the assistant, who has just lost a placement.

And the assistant's own preferences are barely considered. Assistants are matched to whoever is available, in whatever timezone, on whatever work, with limited say.

## What Already Exists
Agency matching is a recruiter-led process supported by skills taxonomies and interview assessment. Some platforms offer trial periods or paid pilots. Marketplace models let clients browse profiles directly. Personality and working style assessments exist and are used inconsistently. Replacement guarantees are a standard commercial term, which is itself an admission of how often matching fails.

## The Customisation Gap
The agency holds the outcome data — which pairings lasted, which ended, and when — across hundreds of placements, and does not use it. What predicts a durable placement is learnable from that history: combinations of working style, task mix, timezone overlap, client briefing quality and industry context. Matching against learned durability rather than against a skills checklist is the obvious application of an asset every agency already has.

Working style assessment is the specific missing input. Capturing how a client actually wants to work — initiative versus confirmation, synchronous versus asynchronous, tolerance for error versus tolerance for questions — and matching it explicitly addresses the most common failure mode, and it requires a short structured instrument rather than a model.

Early failure detection is the third gap. A struggling placement is visible weeks before either party raises it: exchange volume rising, corrections increasing, response latency growing, scope narrowing. Surfacing that early allows a correction — a briefing conversation, a task reallocation — rather than a replacement.

And the assistant's preferences should enter the match. Timezone burden, task type and client working style affect assistant retention substantially, and an agency optimising only the client side is optimising for the party whose churn costs less.

## Impact If Solved
Matching failure is the industry's dominant cost, paid in months of wasted context-building and in assistant placements lost through no fault of the assistant. Durability-based matching from the agency's own placement history, explicit working style assessment on both sides, and early failure detection that permits correction rather than replacement address a problem that replacement guarantees currently paper over.
