# The Bill of Materials Only the Senior Engineer Can Check

**Industry:** [[it-value-added-resellers|IT Distributors & Value-Added Resellers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A reseller's data-centre and networking quotes pass the vendor's configurator and still arrive incomplete for the customer's site, because the knowledge of what goes missing — the optic that doesn't match the existing switch, the power cord for the wrong PDU, the licence tier the feature needs — lives in a few senior pre-sales engineers and is recorded nowhere.

## The Problem
A mid-sized reseller sells a hospital a refresh of its core network and two racks of servers. The account manager gathers the requirement; a pre-sales engineer builds the configuration in the vendor's tool — Cisco Commerce Workspace for the switches, the server vendor's configurator for the compute — and exports the bill of materials into the reseller's quoting system, where it is priced with the distributor's cost, the registered-deal discount and the reseller's installation services. The configurator has done its job: every part is valid with every other part. It has not asked whether the 25G optics are coded for the customer's existing core switches, whether the power cords match the PDUs in the customer's racks, whether the rails fit cabinets that are shallower than standard, whether the software tier includes the feature the customer's security team asked for, or whether the support contract will co-term with the one already running.

The engineers who catch those gaps do it by eye. A senior pre-sales engineer scans a forty-line BOM and says, "these optics won't come up on their 9300s", "that's a C13 cord and their racks are C19", "they'll need the advantage tier for that", "you've quoted one PSU per chassis". None of this is in the configurator, much of it is not in any document, and the engineer learned it from installs that went wrong. In a large reseller a handful of such people review the deals that matter; at a small one the owner does it, or nobody does.

When the check is missed the cost lands after the purchase order. A field engineer arrives on site to find optics that won't link, and the install slips a week while replacements ship. The reseller absorbs a change order it can't bill, a return with a restocking fee, or the cost of a second visit, on a hardware deal whose gross margin may be ~5–10 points (estimate). The vendor's order is already booked, the distributor has shipped, and the customer remembers that the reseller got it wrong. Across a year of solution deals, ~5–15% needing a post-order change order is common in trade practice (estimate), and the share is highest exactly where the reseller has the least senior capacity.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection problem is that the expert's correction happens in conversation and markup, not in a system. The senior engineer's fix is a Teams message, a red line on a spreadsheet or a quiet edit before the quote is sent, so the quoting system records the corrected BOM and never the error it replaced. The evidence that would teach a model — the original configuration, the expert's edit, and what happened at install — sits in three places: the quote archive, the vendor order and RMA history, and the field engineer's ticket. Capturing the expert at the moment of review, without slowing them down, is the first and hardest step.

The labelling problem is that experts disagree with themselves and with each other. One engineer always adds spare optics; another trusts the customer's inventory list; the same engineer accepts a configuration in a quiet week and flags it during quarter-end. Install outcomes are a noisy label too: a missing cord may be fixed from the customer's own spares and never logged, while a change order may reflect the customer changing its mind rather than an error in the quote. The site context the engineer reads — existing switch models, rack and power standards, current licence entitlements — is often known only from the last project and from memory of the account.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. A quote review that takes longer than the senior engineer's two-minute scan will be skipped at quarter-end, when most deals are booked. A tool that flags every possible omission will be ignored within a week. And a reseller will not put an automated checker between its engineers and the customer unless the check is visibly grounded in that customer's installed base and the reseller's own install history, rather than generic vendor rules the configurator already applies.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start with a review surface, not a model. Every solution quote is diffed between the configurator export, the senior engineer's edits and the final quote, and the engineer marks each edit with a one-click reason (fit to site, missing accessory, licence tier, support co-term, customer change). The reseller's install tickets, change orders and returns are joined back to the quote lines they touched. Within months that produces the labelled history the trade has never had: configurations, the expert's corrections and the field outcome.

On top of it, a completeness and fit checker reads each new BOM against three things — the vendor's compatibility rules, the customer's installed base and past orders, and the reseller's history of what went missing on similar configurations — and surfaces a short ranked list: "optics coded for the wrong platform", "no power cords for C19 PDUs on this account", "feature requires a higher licence tier", "support contract won't co-term". Each flag cites the past install or order that supports it, so the engineer can accept or dismiss it in seconds, and each dismissal is captured as a label.

## Impact If Solved
If a reseller cuts post-order change orders on solution deals from ~10% to ~4% (estimate), on $200M of data-centre and networking sales at ~8% gross margin it recovers on the order of $2–4M a year in unbilled parts, return fees and repeat site visits, and frees its senior engineers to review the next deal rather than repair the last one. The larger gain is that the judgement survives a departure: when the engineer who knew every account's racks retires, the reseller keeps the record of what they caught.
