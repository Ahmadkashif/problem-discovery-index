# The Production Rate Only the Chief Estimator Knows

**Industry:** [[heavy-civil-contractors|Heavy Civil Contractors]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every unit price a heavy civil contractor bids is a production rate the chief estimator carries in their head. The firm already records what its crews actually produce and what every competitor bid, but nobody joins the three, so the judgement is never checked and it leaves when the estimator retires.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #bayesian-inference #feature-engineering #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
Take three illustrative lettings. A Corps of Engineers district advertises a project: rehabilitate a jetty with 40,000 tons of armour stone, dredge an access channel, drive a sheet-pile cutoff wall. A port authority lets a wharf extension with 300 steel pipe piles. A short line railroad bids out 12,000 track feet of new siding with two turnouts. Each bid schedule is a list of pay items with estimated quantities, and the contractor writes a unit price against each one. The low responsive bidder wins.

Behind every unit price is a production rate. How many tons of stone a crane barge places per ten-hour shift in this wave climate. How many piles a crew drives per day when the borings show dense sand over a clay layer at 60 feet. How many cubic yards an hour a 24-inch cutter suction dredge moves through this mix of silt and shell at this pumping distance. How many track feet a gang lays per work window when the host railroad grants six hours a night. The chief estimator sets these numbers. They read the plans, the geotechnical report, the specifications and the site visit notes. They recall similar jobs, adjust for the owner (one Corps district inspects harder than another), the season, the crew and the equipment available, and they write a rate. Mobilisation, risk and margin are then spread across the items.

This is tacit knowledge in the strict sense. Ask a 30-year chief estimator why they cut the pile rate by 20% (illustrative) on this job and the answer is a feel: the borings look like the job in Mobile that went badly, the access is bad, this owner's inspector will reject anything off-plumb. A junior estimator applies the rate from the last job in the estimating database. The difference between them is the difference between a firm that wins at a margin and one that wins and loses money. The judgement is concentrated in a few people per firm, many near retirement, and it leaves with them. The general-contractor form of the same judgement, square-foot costs and bid/no-bid calls on buildings, is mapped at [[niches/general-contractors/estimating-bidding/profile|Estimating & Bidding Operations]]. Heavy civil differs because every pay item is priced from a crew production rate in ground and water conditions the drawings only partly describe.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the estimator's reasoning is recorded only as its output. HeavyBid, B2W or InEight estimates hold the final crew, equipment and production assumptions per item, but not why the rate moved off the firm's standard. The adjustments (soil, water, access, owner, season) live in a margin note or nowhere. Capturing them means asking the estimator to tag each adjustment while bidding, in the least forgiving week of the job's life, when bid day is three days away.

The labelling problem is that the outcome label is noisy and partial. The firm only learns the true production rate on jobs it wins, and foremen's cost-code time cards record it imperfectly: hours get charged to the wrong code, quantities are estimated at the end of the shift, and changes, weather and owner delays mix into the rate. Losing bids reveal something different, namely where the market priced the same item, through public bid tabulations, but those prices bundle competitors' own rates, risk and unbalancing. Experts also disagree with themselves. The same estimator prices the same item differently in a full year and a hungry year, so "the right rate" is partly a strategy choice.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. An estimator on bid week will not wait for a model or read a report. A suggested rate has to arrive inside the estimating system as they build the item, with the comparable jobs and actuals behind it, and it has to be right often enough that checking it is faster than ignoring it. Firms also guard their production data as their core competitive asset, so any vendor that pools it across contractors starts with a trust deficit.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A production ledger first, then a model. For every won job, join the bid item's assumed crew, equipment and rate from the estimate to the field actuals from cost-code time cards and quantity reports, cleaned for miscoding, weather days and documented changes. That gives each pay item an assumed-versus-achieved pair. For lost bids, attach the owner's published bid results: every bidder's unit price on the same items where the owner posts a full tabulation (DOTs, and Corps districts that post line-item abstracts), bidder totals otherwise, and the government estimate where the owner publishes it.

On the ledger, learn the estimator's adjustments. Predict achieved production per crew-shift from item type, material classes extracted from the boring logs and specs, water depth, access, season, owner district, crew and equipment. The model is a gradient-boosted or hierarchical Bayesian one, with prediction intervals, because a rate with no uncertainty band is worse than useless to someone pricing risk. Use a language model to extract the job conditions from the geotechnical report and specifications into structured features, so the model sees what the estimator reads. Surface the result inside the estimating screen as a suggested rate with its interval, the three most similar past jobs, their actuals, and where the market priced the item, and log every override with a one-click reason so the next estimate learns from it.

## Impact If Solved
If a mid-size heavy civil firm bidding ~$300M a year cuts its production-rate error on its largest items by a third (estimate), it avoids most of the one or two jobs a year that lose money on a mis-priced dominant item. That is often the whole year's margin at ~3–8% net (estimate). The larger prize is continuity. The chief estimator's judgement becomes a ledger the next estimator inherits, with a track record attached.
