# The Nurse's Read of a Patient Going Downhill

**Industry:** [[general-hospitals|General Hospitals]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Experienced ward nurses sense deterioration hours before the vital signs move and act on it through extra checks, extra notes and a call to the rapid response team, but hospitals deploy sepsis alerts that ignore that signal, miss most cases and fire on almost a fifth of patients.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #lstms-and-grus #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
On a 30-bed medical-surgical unit at 3 a.m., a nurse with fifteen years' experience is carrying five or six patients. One of them, a 74-year-old two days out from a bowel resection, has vital signs that are all inside normal limits. The early warning score is green. The nurse is not comfortable anyway. The patient is a little more confused than at midnight, slightly mottled at the knees, and answered a question oddly. So she goes back every half hour instead of every four. She documents an extra set of vitals and a free-text note ("pt appears more lethargic, will monitor"), pages the resident, and an hour later calls the rapid response team. The lactate comes back at 4.1. The patient is septic and goes to the ICU early, rather than late.

This is tacit knowledge in the plainest sense. Nurses call it "the feeling" or "nurse worry", and the nursing literature has studied it for decades under that name. It comes from thousands of patients who looked a certain way before they crashed. The nurse cannot write it as a rule, and a newer nurse or a traveller on a 13-week contract does not have it. Hospitals lose it constantly. RN turnover was 17.6% in 2025, first-year turnover is higher still, and travel nurses averaged $91 an hour (NSI 2026). [[skilled-nursing-facilities|Skilled Nursing Facilities]] has its own version, [[problems/skilled-nursing-facilities/high-impact|the resident who is "not right" before the vitals say so]], but the two reads differ. In a nursing home an aide spots a deviation from a resident baseline she has known for months. On a ward the nurse reads a stranger's trajectory over hours against frequent vitals and labs, so the hospital signal shows in the tempo of documentation rather than in departure from a known baseline. [[physician-practices|Physician Practices]] has a third version, [[problems/physician-practices/high-impact|the physician's sense that a patient is sicker than they look]]. That judgement forms once, in a single visit, and is graded weeks later by an admission or a diagnosis. The ward nurse's read is renewed every hour in the flowsheet and graded within the shift.

What hospitals deploy instead are alerts built on vitals and labs. The Epic Sepsis Model is embedded in the most widely used EHR. In Michigan Medicine's external validation of 38,455 hospitalisations it scored an AUC of 0.63 with 33% sensitivity. It missed 67% of sepsis cases and alerted on 18% of all hospitalisations, with a positive predictive value of 12% (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2021). Nurses learn to dismiss it. Meanwhile SEP-1 sepsis bundle compliance now feeds Hospital Value-Based Purchasing (secondary source), so the hospital pays for late recognition in money as well as in lives.

## Why It's Unsolved
**The data-collection problem.** The nurse's read is not charted as a judgement. It shows up only as side effects: an extra assessment, a note written at an unusual hour, a PRN pain medication withheld, a call to a physician. Capturing it directly would mean asking a nurse to score "how worried are you" every hour while she carries six patients, a documentation tax nobody will pay. The CONCERN trial showed the side effects can be read instead. Its model used the timing and frequency of nursing documentation as evidence of heightened surveillance, and in a cluster-randomised trial across ~60,000 patients in two health systems it reduced mortality risk by 35.6% and length of stay by more than half a day (Nature Medicine, 2025). But documentation habits differ by unit, by EHR build and by shift, so a model trained on one hospital's flowsheets carries that hospital's habits.

**The labelling problem.** The label is confounded by the expert's own action. When a nurse acts on her worry and the patient goes to the ICU early, the deterioration that would have proved her right is partly prevented. Sepsis onset is itself defined after the fact from cultures, antibiotics and organ dysfunction, with several competing definitions (Sepsis-3, CMS SEP-1, CDC Adult Sepsis Event) that disagree on timing. Nurses also disagree with themselves. The same nurse is more vigilant after a recent bad outcome on her unit and less so at the end of a double shift.

**The deployment problem.** An alert must arrive before the nurse's own concern to add anything. It must be precise enough that a unit with an 18% alert rate does not learn to ignore it, and it must fit the workflow: a coloured badge on the patient list, not a pop-up. It also must not deskill. If juniors defer to a green badge, the system has replaced the experienced read with a worse one. And because a sepsis prediction tool that drives treatment can be regulated as software as a medical device, the hospital's committee will ask for validation on its own population before go-live.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A surveillance model that treats the nurse as a sensor rather than an end user. It reads, hour by hour, the documentation metadata that reveals concern: assessment frequency against the ordered frequency, off-schedule vitals, free-text note length and terms ("lethargic", "not himself", "family concerned"), PRN patterns, rapid response and physician pages. It combines these with vitals, labs and the medication record, and outputs a calibrated probability of rapid response activation, unplanned ICU transfer, sepsis onset or death in the next 12 and 24 hours. It shows a three-level badge on the unit's patient list and a short "why" ("documentation frequency doubled since 01:00; two off-schedule vitals; new confusion term").

Validation is done on the deploying hospital's own data before go-live, with lead time against the nurses' own escalations reported as the headline. A second layer captures the experienced nurse's read directly, but only at the moment she escalates. A one-tap reason code on the rapid response call builds a labelled corpus of expert concern that newer nurses can learn from.

## Impact If Solved
If a hospital could reproduce even part of the CONCERN effect (35.6% lower mortality risk, half a day less length of stay), a 300-bed hospital would save several unplanned ICU transfers and deaths a month and ~5,000–8,000 bed-days a year (estimate). It would also improve SEP-1 and readmission performance, which now carry payment consequences. Just as important, the experienced nurse's read would stop leaving with her. The unit would keep a record of what concern looked like before deterioration, and travellers and new graduates could see it.
