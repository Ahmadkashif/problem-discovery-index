# The Tester With Three Reports Owed

**Industry:** [[penetration-testing-firms|Penetration Testing Firms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Engagements are booked back to back because utilisation is the business model, so the writing for each one happens in the evenings during the next one.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #gradient-boosting #time-series-forecasting #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
Testing firms run on utilisation. A tester's calendar is booked with engagements back to back, and the report for each is due after it ends — which is when the next one has already started. The predictable result is report debt: two or three write-ups owed, each getting worse as the memory of the engagement fades.

The quality cost is direct. A report written three weeks after the testing, from notes taken while testing, misses nuance the tester understood at the time. Clients receive a thinner document than the work deserved, and the tester knows it.

Context switching is constant. Each engagement is a different client, stack, scope and set of rules, and a tester may hold three at different stages simultaneously. Security testing is deep concentration work and fragments badly.

Underneath sits the expectation of continuous personal research. The skills depreciate quickly, staying current requires reading, experimenting and following disclosures, and none of it is billable — so it happens in personal time, on top of the report debt.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is skilled work squeezed by a business model that prices only the testing days. The writing is unbilled in effect, the research is unbilled explicitly, and both are required for the work to be good.

The burnout pattern is well recognised in the profession. People enter because the work is genuinely interesting, spend a few years on back-to-back engagements with report debt, and leave for internal security roles at client organisations — which is why firms lose their most experienced testers to their own customers.

The quality tension is its own stress. A tester who cares about the work knows that the report they are writing at eleven at night after a full day on another engagement is not their best, and delivering it anyway erodes something.

And the sales relationship creates friction. Engagements sold on a scope the tester considers inadequate mean either inadequate coverage or unbilled hours, and the tester absorbs the choice.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Capture during, write after. Evidence associated with findings as they are discovered, notes structured rather than free-form, and a draft assembled from that material means the writing starts from something substantial rather than from a blank document and a three-week-old memory.

Generate the repeatable parts. Weakness explanations, reproduction steps from captured traffic, and standard remediation guidance are formulaic; the tester's contribution is the client-specific impact analysis and the judgement, which is the part worth their time.

Schedule the writing. Utilisation models that book testing days and assume the writing is free produce the debt by construction, and the fix is commercial rather than technical — but a firm that has measured how many hours reporting actually takes can price it, and currently nobody measures it because it happens in the evenings.

Support the research. Firm-level tracking of new techniques and disclosures relevant to the stacks the firm tests, curated rather than individually chased, turns a personal-time obligation into a shared capability.

## Impact If Solved
Report debt is the defining working condition of this profession and the reason experienced testers leave for client-side roles. Capturing evidence during testing and generating the repeatable text returns most of the writing time; measuring it makes it priceable; and a firm that fixes this retains the people whose expertise is the entire product.
