# The Operator's Read of an Alarm

**Industry:** [[security-alarm-integrators|Security Alarm Integrators]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A central-station operator decides in seconds whether an alarm is real, using a feel for zone sequences, account habits and callers' voices that nobody records or grades, and verified-response cities and AVS-01 scoring now turn that feel into a public score that decides whether police come.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #lstms-and-grus #cnns #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #worker-facing #compliance

## The Problem
A burglary alarm arrives at a UL 827 central station as a short sequence of events. The panel armed in away mode at 7:52. The front door opened at 2:14 a.m. without a disarm inside the entry delay, and the living-room motion detector tripped eleven seconds later. The operator sees the event, the account's notes and its history, and, if the dealer sold it, a camera clip. They call the premises, then the first contact on the list. That is enhanced call verification, which most ordinances now require before a dispatch request. Then they decide: cancel, keep trying, or request police. For a fire signal or a panic button the script is shorter. For a burglary signal the decision is a judgement.

Experienced operators make that judgement on patterns they cannot write down. A single door contact with no follow-on motion at a house whose owner works nights reads differently from a door-then-motion sequence at a house that has been armed all weekend. A glass-break detector on a known-faulty zone in a restaurant kitchen is noise; the same detector at a jewellery store is not. A caller who gives the right passcode with a half-second hesitation and background noise gets a second question. A three-second clip in which a shape crosses the frame is a moth, a shadow from a passing car or a person, and veterans are faster and more often right. Operators say they develop a feel for an account after a few months of handling its signals. That feel is tacit knowledge in the exact sense the vault uses: pattern recognition built from repeated exposure, with no specification behind it.

The consequence used to be private. Between 94% and 98% of dispatched burglar alarms are false, and police absorbed the waste. Now cities are pushing it back. Seattle stopped responding to unverified alarms on 1 October 2024, and Vallejo, Madison and San Jose have tried similar rules. Others bill false dispatches to the alarm user through permit programmes. TMA's ANSI/TMA-AVS-01 standard asks the station to classify each call for service from level 0 (no call) to level 4 (confirmed threat to life). Level 2 means confirmed or highly probable human presence. That score now travels to the 911 centre over ASAP in more than 150 cities. The operator's private read has become a published, auditable rating, and a station that over-scores will be ignored by dispatchers as surely as one that cries wolf. The judgement that decides whether the product works is made by a workforce with heavy turnover, and the station keeps no measure of which operators read alarms well.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's read happens in seconds inside the automation platform, and only its output is logged: a disposition code and a few typed words. The cues the operator actually used are not captured as features. Which zones mattered, what the caller's voice sounded like, which frames of the clip were decisive: none of it is stored. Capturing them means instrumenting the operator's screen and audio during live handling without slowing a workflow that UL 827 requires to run at signal-priority speed. Historical data is spread across platforms such as Manitou, MASterMind and SIMS, in each station's own disposition vocabulary.

The labelling challenge is worse than in most tacit-knowledge problems, because the ground truth is usually missing. A cancelled alarm is labelled false by the customer who cancelled it, and a customer standing beside an intruder can do the same under duress. A dispatched alarm returns a police disposition only sometimes. ASAP and some PSAPs send back clearance codes, many don't, and "no evidence found" is not the same as "false". Operators also disagree with themselves. The same sequence is read differently at 4 a.m. after twenty nuisance signals than at 9 p.m. on a fresh shift. Any honest label set needs the disposition plus a delayed outcome: a police report, an insurance claim, a dealer service ticket showing a faulty sensor. It also needs repeat reads of the same events by several operators to measure how far they agree.

The deployment challenge is speed and accountability. The score has to be on the operator's screen before they pick up the phone, faster than their own recall of the account, or it is ignored. A model that is right 95% of the time and wrong on the one real break-in a month creates a liability no station will accept unless the operator remains the decision-maker and the model's role is recorded. Dealers, who own the customer, must agree to the data use, and wholesale stations serve hundreds of dealers under different contracts.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A validation layer inside the station's automation platform. It turns each alarm event into features: zone type and sequence timing, arm state and recent history, the account's own false-alarm pattern, weather, the device's trouble history, the ECV call outcomes, and video-classifier output where cameras exist. It produces a calibrated probability that the event is a real intrusion, mapped to the AVS-01 level and shown with the three cues that drove it. The operator confirms or overrides, and the override is captured as a label with its reason.

Behind it sits an outcome ledger, which is the real asset. Every disposition joins later to police clearance codes returned over ASAP, dealer service tickets showing a sensor fault, customer reports and insurance claims. Operators and models can then be graded on the same events. The station learns which operators read which alarm types best. It can show a city its false-dispatch rate by score level, and it can bring new operators to veteran accuracy faster by training them on graded cases.

## Impact If Solved
A station that halves false dispatches without missing real intrusions keeps its customers dispatchable in verified-response cities. It also protects them from permit fines that drive attrition, and it earns police priority for its high-score calls. Across a business whose whole value is a promise of response, a measured and defensible alarm score is the difference between a monitoring contract that means something and one a city has learned to ignore.
