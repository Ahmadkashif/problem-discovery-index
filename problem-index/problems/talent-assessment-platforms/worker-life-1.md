# The Candidate Scored by Something They Cannot See

**Industry:** [[talent-assessment-platforms|Talent Assessment Platforms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** An hour of unpaid assessment, a score they never see, criteria they are never told, a rejection with no reason, and no way to ask.
**Tags:** #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #gradient-boosting #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #compliance #worker-facing #bayesian-inference

## The Problem
A candidate applying for a role is asked to complete an assessment: a cognitive test, a personality inventory, a coding exercise, a recorded video interview, or several of these. It takes between thirty minutes and several hours, unpaid, frequently before any human has looked at their application.

They are told almost nothing. Not what the assessment measures, not how it is weighted against the rest of their application, not what score they achieved, not what threshold applied, and not why they were rejected. The rejection arrives as a template.

The time cost is substantial and falls unevenly. A candidate applying to twenty roles may complete twenty assessments, which is a working week unpaid, and the burden is heaviest on people who are unemployed, working multiple jobs, caring, or otherwise time-constrained — which is a selection effect on who completes rather than on who is suitable.

Accessibility is a persistent problem. Timed assessments disadvantage candidates with disabilities in ways that accommodation processes handle inconsistently, and candidates frequently do not know accommodations exist or fear that requesting one will mark them.

Video assessment adds its own difficulties: recording yourself answering questions to a camera is an unusual and uncomfortable task that measures comfort with the format alongside whatever it intends, and automated scoring of speech and language carries known risks of penalising accents, dialects and non-native speakers.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is a consequential decision about someone's employment made by an instrument they cannot see, on criteria they are not told, with no feedback and no appeal. The asymmetry is total and the consequence is access to work.

The unpaid time is a real transfer. Assessment has shifted screening cost from the employer to the applicant, in volume, and is borne most heavily by those least able to bear it.

The absence of feedback is the specific frustration. A candidate who fails an assessment learns nothing — not whether they were close, not what the gap was, not whether to try again — so twenty applications produce no learning and no improvement.

And the automated scoring of behaviour and speech is the part that has attracted scrutiny for good reason. When a system scores how someone speaks, the risk of penalising difference unrelated to job performance is real, and the industry's own retreat from facial analysis demonstrates that these concerns have substance.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Tell the candidate what is being measured and why. A plain statement of the construct, the approximate weighting and the expected duration before they start is basic respect and materially improves completion — and completion bias is a measurement problem for the employer as well as an equity problem.

Give feedback. A score band with an explanation, and where possible an indication of the gap, costs little and converts a black box into something a person can respond to. Several established publishers do provide candidate feedback reports and it should be standard.

Make accommodation the default posture. Offering accommodation options clearly and without requiring disclosure of a diagnosis, and validating that the accommodated form measures the same construct, addresses a barrier that currently depends on a candidate's willingness to identify themselves.

Bound the unpaid time and say so. An explicit total time commitment across the whole process, and payment for assessments beyond a reasonable threshold, would end the escalation that has made multi-hour unpaid exercises routine.

And provide an appeal route. A candidate who believes a technical failure, an accessibility barrier or a scoring error affected their result should be able to say so and have it looked at — which is the minimum for an automated decision with this consequence, and is the direction regulation is heading.

## Impact If Solved
Assessment now sits between a very large number of people and access to work, and it operates with no transparency, no feedback and no appeal while consuming unpaid time that falls hardest on the least advantaged. Disclosure, feedback, default accommodation, bounded time and a genuine appeal route are all achievable and inexpensive, and they change the relationship from an opaque filter into a process a person can participate in.
