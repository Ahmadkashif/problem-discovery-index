# Counsel on First-Pass Redlines

**Industry:** [[contract-lifecycle-platforms|Contract Lifecycle Platforms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** In-house counsel stops spending its most expensive hours making the same twelve edits to the same twelve clauses, and starts spending them on the negotiations where judgement changes the outcome.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #bert #transformers #word-embeddings #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #automation #worker-facing

## The Problem
An in-house lawyer reviewing a counterparty's agreement performs a first pass that is substantially the same every time. Cap the liability. Make the indemnity mutual. Strike the automatic renewal or add a notice period. Fix the governing law. Add the data protection terms. Remove the unilateral amendment right. Narrow the licence grant.

The same twelve edits, to the same twelve clauses, in slightly different language each time because every counterparty's paper words things differently.

Then comes the part that requires a lawyer: which of these positions actually matters for this deal, what to trade for what, whether the commercial context justifies accepting something the playbook dislikes, and how hard to push given who the counterparty is and what the business needs.

The first pass consumes the majority of the time and produces almost none of the value. It is done by people who are expensive, in short supply, and were hired to exercise judgement.

At high volume it also degrades. A lawyer on their ninth agreement of the day performs a less careful first pass than on their first, and the errors that result are precisely the ones the first pass exists to prevent.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
In-house legal teams are chronically under-resourced relative to the volume the business generates, and the pressure is constant rather than periodic. The backlog is always there and always growing.

The work also inverts the reason people move in-house. The attraction is being close to the business and shaping decisions; the reality for many is a redline queue that leaves no time for the advisory work. Lawyers describe this specifically as the disappointment of in-house practice.

There is a quality anxiety on top. Volume pressure means the first pass is sometimes hurried, and a missed uncapped indemnity is career-relevant. Working faster than you are comfortable with, on documents where errors are serious, is a durable source of stress.

## What a Solution Looks Like
The first pass drafted, not performed. Given the counterparty's paper, the company's playbook and its executed history, the standard edits can be proposed as redlines with the rationale attached, and counsel reviews, adjusts and adds what only they can.

Absence flagged as prominently as presence, since the missing limitation of liability is the finding that matters most and is the one a fatigued reviewer misses.

Positions ranked by what is achievable. A redline that this counterparty has rejected in every prior negotiation is worth knowing about before it is sent, and the vendor's cross-customer history answers that where no individual company can.

Consistency enforced across the team, so the same clause is treated the same way regardless of which lawyer picked it up and how late in the day it was.

And the boundary held firmly: the tool proposes standard positions and the lawyer decides everything about strategy, trade-offs and what this particular deal warrants. The judgement is the job; the typing is not.

## Impact If Solved
First-pass redlining consumes the majority of in-house legal time and produces the least of its value, and it degrades under exactly the volume pressure that makes it necessary. Drafting it returns counsel to the negotiation and advisory work that motivated the role, and improves the consistency of the pass that catches the serious problems.
