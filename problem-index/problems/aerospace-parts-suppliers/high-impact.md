# The Indication Only the Level II Can Call

**Industry:** [[aerospace-parts-suppliers|Aerospace Parts Suppliers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every rotating engine part and structural casting passes a certified inspector who decides, by eye and experience, whether a glowing line in a penetrant booth or a shadow on a radiograph is a crack, and that call (which sets both the escape risk and how much scarce capacity is scrapped) is recorded as a tick on a traveller and never as evidence a model or a successor could learn from.

## The Problem
At an engine-parts supplier, a turbine disk, a compressor blade, a shaft or a structural casting gets its last word from a person. After machining and cleaning, the part goes through fluorescent penetrant inspection (FPI): penetrant applied, excess washed off, developer dusted on, and a Level II inspector, certified under NAS 410, examines it under UV-A light in a darkened booth. Castings also go through radiography, and some forgings and rings through ultrasonic inspection. For every indication, the inspector decides whether it is relevant (a crack, a lap, a seam, porosity breaking the surface) or non-relevant (a scratch, a machining mark, a fixture witness, penetrant trapped in a radius), measures it against the customer's acceptance criteria, and accepts, rejects, or sends the part back through the process.

Experienced inspectors are good at this in ways they cannot fully describe. They know a real crack "bleeds back" after wiping and a scratch does not. They know which radius on which part number always holds penetrant and which bore always shows a witness mark from a fixture. They can tell from a radiograph whether a density change is gas porosity, shrinkage or a film artefact by comparing it against reference radiographs they have memorised more than consulted. They also know when to distrust the process itself: a background that is too bright means poor washing, and a re-process is safer than a call. Juniors take years to build this, and the supplier depends on a handful of Level IIs and one Level III per site (estimate).

Both kinds of error are expensive. A missed crack in a life-limited rotating part is a safety event, and quality escapes in aerospace end in customer containment, audits and sometimes airworthiness action. A false reject scraps a part that may have cost ~$5,000–$50,000 in forging or casting and machining (estimate), with replacement lead times measured in months while engines wait. In 2025 Howmet's Engine Products segment earned a 33.3% EBITDA margin on castings and rings, and Airbus's CEO named the Pratt & Whitney engine shortage as the pacer of its 2026 deliveries. Scrap at final inspection is lost capacity in the tightest part of the chain.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the evidence disappears. FPI indications exist for a few minutes under UV light and are judged by eye; the traveller records "accept" or "reject, indication at location 3" and the inspector's stamp. Nobody photographs the indications that were accepted, so there is no record of what an acceptable indication looks like on this part. Digital radiography leaves images, but dispositions are written at part level, not drawn on the image. Capturing the expert's call means putting calibrated UV-A cameras in the booth and asking for a tap or a mark at the moment of decision, during production, without slowing a certified process that Nadcap audits.

The labelling problem is that experts disagree with each other and with themselves. Inspection reliability studies have long shown that detection of small surface cracks varies with the inspector, the lighting and the part (recalled), and the same inspector will call a borderline indication differently at the end of a shift. Ground truth is rare and late: sectioning a rejected part, a CT scan, a crack found at the next shop visit or in service. Most "labels" are therefore the inspector's own disposition, which is the thing being modelled, so a model trained naively learns the average inspector's errors.

The deployment problem is that any change to an inspection method must be accepted by the customer and survive a Nadcap NDT audit. The certified inspector stays the decision-maker for the foreseeable future, so a model can only be a second reader, a consistency check, or a triage that sends hard calls to the Level III. It must work at booth speed and never add a step the inspector sees as paperwork. Defence-part images are ITAR-controlled, which rules out shared cloud training across customers without care.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A capture layer first. Fit FPI booths with UV-A-calibrated cameras that image each indication when the inspector marks it, record the disposition and the measured length, and link both to part number, serial, heat lot, operation and process parameters (penetrant line, dwell, wash pressure). For radiography, let the reader draw the indication on the digital image as part of the disposition. Over months this builds the first part-level library of accepted and rejected indications with context.

On that library, train detectors and segmenters per part family, with transfer learning from general defect imagery. Run them as a second reader that flags indications the inspector did not mark and borderline calls that differ from the site's history, and as a triage that routes the hardest cases to the Level III. Calibrate against the rare hard truth (sectioning, CT, downstream findings) and against the inspectors' own repeat-read agreement. The same library becomes the training set for new inspectors: "here are the last 200 calls on this radius, and how they held up."

## Impact If Solved
If a plant processing ~50,000 FPI parts a year cuts false rejects by even a quarter of a percentage point, it keeps ~125 parts a year (estimate) worth ~$0.6–6M at the values above, out of the scarcest capacity in the industry. The larger value is fewer escapes and a disposition record that survives the retirement of the inspectors who carry it today.
