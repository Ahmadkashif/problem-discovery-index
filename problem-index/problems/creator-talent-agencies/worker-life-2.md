# Business Affairs and the Ninety-Day Invoice

**Industry:** [[creator-talent-agencies|Creator Talent Agencies]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Somebody at the agency spends their month chasing brands for money already earned, reconciling payments against deals whose terms live in PDFs, and telling creators their payment is still coming.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #large-language-models #time-series-forecasting #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Creator deals are paid on terms that would be considered aggressive in most industries — sixty and ninety days are normal, and the clock frequently starts at an approval milestone that itself slipped. Payment arrives from the brand, or from the brand's agency, or from a procurement system that requires a purchase order nobody obtained, and it must then be reconciled against the deal, split with the creator, and reported.

The person doing this works from a spreadsheet and a folder of contracts. Matching an incoming payment to a deal is manual because the remittance advice references an invoice number, the invoice references a purchase order, and the purchase order may cover several deliverables across several creators. Partial payments, deductions for deliverables the brand claims were late, and currency differences all require investigation.

Chasing is most of the job. An email template, a follow-up, an escalation to the manager, a call to the brand's accounts payable. Some brands are reliably late in a way everyone knows and nobody has recorded; some go quiet entirely and a deal that was celebrated as a win six months ago becomes a write-off nobody discusses.

And the creator asks. Repeatedly. For most creators the payment is a meaningful share of their annual income and the delay is a cash flow event, so the business affairs person is fielding anxiety from the roster about money that is not theirs to release.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is a role that absorbs a structural problem — an industry with long payment terms and weak collection leverage — and converts it into individual chasing. The person has no authority over the brand, limited leverage, and full visibility of the consequence, which is a creator who cannot pay their editor.

It is also invisible work, in the sense that nobody notices when it goes right. A deal collected on time produces no acknowledgement; a deal collected six months late produces a conversation about why it took so long. The reconciliation work in particular is skilled and detailed and appears in nobody's report.

And the knowledge accumulated — which brands pay, which require a purchase order first, which agency's accounts payable needs a specific format, which contact actually responds — lives entirely in this person's head and email archive. When they leave, collection performance measurably deteriorates and nobody can explain why.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Structure the payment terms from the contracts themselves, so the expected date of every payment is known, the milestone that triggers it is tracked, and an overdue invoice is a system state rather than a discovery.

Predict the risk. Brand payment behaviour is highly patterned and predictable from history — this brand pays at ninety-one days on average, this one requires a purchase order before invoicing, this one disputes at the approval stage — and surfacing that at the point of deal negotiation lets terms reflect the risk rather than discovering it afterwards.

Automate the reconciliation. Matching remittances to invoices to deals to creators is a matching problem with strong structure, and it is most of the detailed work.

Escalate on a schedule rather than on a person's memory, with the correspondence drafted and the escalation path defined, so chasing is a process that runs rather than a task someone gets to.

And make the creator-facing position honest. A creator should be able to see what is due, when it is expected, what stage it is at, and what is being done about it, without asking — which removes the anxious inbound and is straightforward once the terms are structured.

## Impact If Solved
Late and failed collection is a direct and substantial loss in a business with thin margins, and it lands hardest on creators for whom the payment is a livelihood. Structuring the terms, predicting brand payment risk before the deal is signed, and automating reconciliation and escalation converts an invisible manual grind into a managed process — and preserves the collection knowledge that currently walks out of the door with one person.
