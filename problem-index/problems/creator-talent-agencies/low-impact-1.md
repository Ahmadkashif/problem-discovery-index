# Brand Matching and Deal Flow

**Industry:** [[creator-talent-agencies|Creator Talent Agencies]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Deal flow comes from who a manager knows, which means the same twenty brands see the same forty creators and the rest of the roster waits.
**Tags:** #contrastive-learning #word-embeddings #bert #graph-neural-networks #gradient-boosting #k-nearest-neighbors #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact

## The Problem
An agency's revenue is deal flow, and deal flow is relationships. A manager with strong brand contacts in beauty places their beauty creators; a creator whose audience would be perfect for an outdoor equipment brand waits because nobody at the agency knows anyone there. The distribution of deals across a roster is therefore shaped by the agency's contact network rather than by fit, and the mid-roster — creators who are good but not the first name a manager reaches for — is chronically underplaced.

On the inbound side, briefs arrive from brands and agencies describing what they want in the language of reach and demographics. Matching them to the roster is done by a person reading the brief and thinking of names. That works, is fast, and is limited to what that person can hold in mind, which for a forty-creator roster across a dozen categories is less than it feels like.

The information that would improve both directions is present and unused: which of the roster's past deals performed, for which brand categories, with what audience response, and which brands have historically been good partners versus difficult ones.

## What Already Exists
Influencer marketing platforms — CreatorIQ, Aspire, Captiv8 — run discovery for brands, not for agencies, and their data does not flow back. Agencies use CRM to track brand relationships. Marketplaces exist where creators and brands find each other directly, and are mostly used at the smaller end. Some larger agencies have built internal databases of past deals. Social analytics give audience demographics that everyone in the chain treats with suspicion.

## The Customisation Gap
The generic discovery tools answer the brand's question — find me creators — and nobody answers the agency's, which is which brands should be approached for this creator, and which creator best fits this brief given everything we know about our own roster's performance.

The signal that makes the agency version better is proprietary: its own historical deal outcomes. An agency with three years of deals knows which of its creators produce for which brand categories, which content formats worked, and which audiences converted. Turning that into a fit model is the natural use of the one dataset an agency has that nobody else does.

The outbound direction is where the value is concentrated and where nothing exists. Given a creator, ranking the brands most likely to want them — from category fit, audience composition, existing brand relationships in the space, competitor activity, and the brand's known appetite for creator work — turns the underplaced mid-roster into an addressable pipeline rather than a waiting list.

And the brand side needs a quality record. Which brands pay on time, approve efficiently, respect the creative, and exercise the rights they bought is knowledge that exists in managers' heads and determines whether a deal is worth taking at the price offered. Structuring it is a small change that makes every subsequent negotiation better informed.

## Impact If Solved
Deal flow concentration is why most rosters have a long tail of creators generating little revenue despite being commercially viable, and the constraint is the manager's contact list rather than market demand. Outbound brand ranking built from the agency's own outcome history addresses exactly that tail, and a structured record of brand behaviour improves the quality of the deals taken as well as the quantity.
