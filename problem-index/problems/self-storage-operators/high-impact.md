# The Rent Increase That Guesses Who Will Leave

**Industry:** [[self-storage-operators|Self-Storage Operators]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every month an operator decides how much to raise thousands of existing tenants' rents. The move-outs that follow are recorded to the day, but the judgement that sized each increase is not, so it is never graded and it leaves with the person who made it.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact #compliance

## The Problem
A self-storage tenant moves in at a web rate set to win the click, often with a first month for $1. Five or six months later the existing-customer rate increase (ECRI) arrives. Public Storage describes its rule in its 2025 10-K: long-term tenants get increases "every six to twelve months", sized by "evaluating the additional revenue from the increase against the negative impact of incremental move-outs". Extra Space says existing tenants receive increases "at least annually". Most of an operator's growth in rent per occupied foot comes from these letters, not from new customers. At Public Storage in 2025, cumulative increases to long-term tenants offset falling move-in rates.

At the REITs a model proposes each increase. Across the rest of the industry the size is chosen by a person. That person is the revenue manager at a regional operator or third-party manager, or the owner of a single facility doing it at the kitchen table. Experienced ones "know" things the ledger does not say. A contractor storing tools on autopay for three years will absorb 15% without noticing. A tenant who moved in after a divorce and visits every weekend is emptying the unit and should get nothing. A business tenant in a 10x30 will call the district office, then pay. A store across the road just opened at half the price, so this month's letters should be smaller. At an industry training reported by Slate in January 2025, operators said their first increases ran from 20% to 100%. Choosing between those numbers is the judgement.

The outcome of every decision is recorded perfectly: the tenant paid the new rate, moved out within 60 days, or stopped paying and went to lien. What is not recorded is the reasoning. Nobody notes that this tenant got 8% instead of 25% because they looked like they were leaving, or what the revenue manager expected to happen. The counterfactual is missing too: what a smaller increase would have kept. When the revenue manager leaves, or the owner sells to a REIT, the feel for which tenants bear an increase leaves too.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection challenge is that the expert's reasoning has to be captured while they size the batch, inside the screen where they approve increases, and it is mostly visual and contextual. It rests on the tenant's file, the store manager's comment, the gate log, a competitor's sign. A revenue manager approving 400 letters on a Tuesday will not write 400 reasons. Capture has to be a one-click override reason or a quick flag ("moving out", "business, won't leave", "complained last time"). The model must also reconstruct, after the fact, which signals were on screen when the decision was made.

The labelling challenge is that a move-out is a noisy and confounded label. Tenants leave for reasons the increase did not cause: the house sold, the divorce settled, the move finished. Increases were not assigned at random. Larger increases went to tenants the expert judged stable, so a naive model learns that big increases cause few move-outs. Experts also disagree with themselves. The same revenue manager sizes a similar tenant differently in May, when units are scarce, and in December, when the store is half empty. Honest labels need a matched comparison or deliberate small randomisation of increase sizes, a 90- to 180-day outcome window, and repeated scoring of a sample to measure the expert's own consistency.

The deployment challenge is that the recommendation must arrive in the batch screen, faster than the expert's own scan, with a reason the expert accepts. It must also be defensible to a regulator. California's 30-day notice rule, Los Angeles County's emergency cap and New York City's 2026 settlement with Extra Space mean every recommendation must carry its notice date and a check against local caps. It must be explainable without referring to who the tenant is. A recommendation that looks like targeting vulnerable tenants is a legal risk, not a feature.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An increase ledger first and a model second. Every proposed increase is stored with the tenant's tenure, in-place rent against the current street rate for the same unit type, payment and access history, unit size and the local competitor set. The record also holds the expert's final size and a one-click reason for any override, and the outcome at 30, 90 and 180 days. The ledger is graded continuously: for each band of increase size, how much net rent was gained after the move-outs it caused.

On the ledger, two models. A move-out risk model learns the experienced revenue manager's read, which tenants leave after an increase, from their override reasons and from the outcomes. It is calibrated so that "15% risk" means 15 in 100 leave. A dose-response model estimates how risk rises with increase size for each kind of tenant, using the small random variation the operator deliberately builds into its batches. The two combine into a recommended increase per tenant that maximises expected net rent over the next year. It respects state notice periods and emergency caps, and it shows the expert the reason.

## Impact If Solved
A mature 500-unit facility collecting ~$700,000 a year in rent might earn ~3–6% of that from increases (estimate). Sizing them to cut induced move-outs by even one percentage point while keeping the uplift is worth ~$10,000–20,000 per store a year (estimate). Across the industry's $20.8B of receipts, each percentage point of better-sized increases is ~$200M a year. Recording the reasoning also gives the operator what New York City's settlement now demands: a documented basis for every increase, kept when the revenue manager leaves.
