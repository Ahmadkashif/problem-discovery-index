# The Designer in Round Six of Stakeholder Opinion

**Industry:** [[product-design-studios|Product Design Studios]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A designer defends the same decision across six rounds of review against stakeholders whose objections are preferences, with no evidence available to either side.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #bert #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #hypothesis-testing #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #tacit-knowledge-ml

## The Problem
Design review at a client is a meeting where people with different authority express preferences about an interface. Some of those preferences are informed by knowledge the designer lacks — a regulatory constraint, a support pattern, a strategic commitment. Many are aesthetic, and they arrive with organisational weight attached.

The designer's position is structurally weak. They have research from a handful of participants, professional judgement, and craft arguments. The stakeholder has an opinion and a title. In the absence of evidence, the opinion wins, and the designer either accepts a change they believe is wrong or spends political capital they may need later.

Then the rounds accumulate. A new stakeholder joins at round four and reopens a decision settled at round one. Feedback arrives in three channels, from six people, partly contradictory, with no record of what was already decided and why. The designer reconciles it, produces another version, and presents again. Work that was substantively finished in week three is still being revised in week nine.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is the part of the job designers describe as the reason they leave agencies. The craft work — the part they trained for and are good at — is a minority of the hours, and the majority is advocacy in rooms where the terms of the argument are unfavourable.

The erosion is cumulative. A designer who has had forty decisions overruled by preference learns to anticipate the overruling and to design defensively, proposing what will be approved rather than what they think is right. That is a real loss of professional agency and it is invisible from outside, because the work still ships.

And the revision churn is demoralising in a specific way: the fifth round rarely improves anything, everyone in the room knows it, and it happens anyway because there is no mechanism to close a decision.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Make the decision record real. Every substantive decision, the options considered, the rationale, and who agreed — captured as a durable artefact rather than as a slide in a deck that gets superseded. That single change closes the reopening loop, because a stakeholder who joins late can be shown the decision rather than relitigating it.

Consolidate and classify the feedback. Comments arriving across channels should be deduplicated, grouped, and separated into the categories that matter — a constraint the designer did not know about, a preference, a misunderstanding of the intent, a genuine usability concern. Those require entirely different responses and are currently handled as one undifferentiated pile.

Bring evidence into the room. Where the studio has pattern-level outcome data from comparable work, the argument changes character completely: this arrangement produced this result across eleven comparable projects is a different kind of statement from I think this is better. That is the direct payoff of the outcome measurement problem this industry has otherwise not solved.

And make the cost of rounds visible. Revision cycles are billable hours with a known cost and a measurable marginal improvement, and showing that curve to a client is usually enough to end the sixth round.

## Impact If Solved
Revision churn and unstructured stakeholder feedback are where design engagements lose margin and where designers lose their enthusiasm for the work. Durable decision records, classified feedback and evidence in the room shorten the cycle and change who wins the argument — which determines both what ships and whether experienced designers stay in client services.
