# Scoping and Pitch Estimation

**Industry:** [[product-design-studios|Product Design Studios]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Studios price fixed-fee engagements for discovery work that by definition does not know what it will find, using an estimate somebody produced from memory of the last similar project.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #confidence-intervals #survival-analysis #k-nearest-neighbors #time-series-forecasting #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #bayesian-inference

## The Problem
A prospective client describes a project. The studio produces a proposal with a scope, a timeline, a team shape and a price, usually fixed. The estimate is built by a principal recalling comparable work, adding a contingency that reflects how nervous they feel, and adjusting to what they think the client will accept.

The variance is enormous and asymmetric. Projects overrun far more often than they underrun, and the causes are recurring and knowable: a client stakeholder who joins late and reopens settled decisions, an engineering team whose constraints were not surfaced during discovery, a legal review nobody scheduled, a research phase that finds the actual problem is different from the stated one. Each of these has happened at every studio many times and none of them appear in the estimate.

Winning the work has its own cost. Competitive pitches involve concept work, sometimes substantial, performed unpaid against a probability of winning that nobody calculates. Studios routinely spend a meaningful share of senior capacity on pitches, and almost none of them know their win rate by client type, deal size or pitch format.

## What Already Exists
Professional services automation — Productive, Harvest Forecast, Float, Runn — tracks time, utilisation and project profitability after the fact. Proposal tooling handles document assembly. Most studios maintain a rate card and a template estimate spreadsheet. Larger firms inside consultancies have access to structured estimation methodologies that were designed for software delivery and transfer poorly to design discovery.

## The Customisation Gap
Every studio holds the data that would improve its estimates and uses it only for invoicing. Time tracked by project, phase and person, against the original estimate, across several years, is a labelled dataset of estimation error — and the drivers of overrun are recorded alongside it in project notes, change orders and the timing of scope changes.

What is missing is the estimate expressed as a distribution. A proposal that says twelve weeks is asserting a certainty nobody has; one built from comparable past projects with an interval, and with the specific overrun risks named — number of stakeholders, whether engineering is engaged during discovery, whether a legal review is in scope — is both more honest and more defensible in the commercial conversation.

The second gap is pitch qualification. Win rate by client characteristics, deal size, sector, whether the brief came inbound or through a network, and how many firms are competing, is computable from a studio's own CRM and would let a firm decline the pitches it loses. Unpaid pitch work is one of the largest uncosted expenses in this industry and nobody has measured its return.

## Impact If Solved
Overruns come directly out of margin in a business that has little, and the causes are recurring rather than novel. Estimates as calibrated distributions with named risk drivers change both pricing and the conversation about change orders, and win-rate modelling addresses an unpaid expense that consumes senior capacity nobody is billing for.
