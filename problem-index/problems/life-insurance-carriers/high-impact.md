# The Underwriter's Read of the Medical File

**Industry:** [[life-insurance-carriers|Life Insurance Carriers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A senior life underwriter reads a few hundred pages of physician records and assigns a risk class that sets the premium for thirty years, and the carrier never joins that decision, or the reasoning behind it, to the mortality that follows.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #large-language-models #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
An application for $2 million of term life on a 54-year-old arrives with an MIB check, a prescription history, a lab panel and, because the applicant disclosed a cardiac work-up three years ago, an attending physician statement. The APS is a stack of faxed and scanned office notes, echocardiogram reports, specialist letters and lab printouts, often a couple of hundred pages and sometimes far more (estimate). A senior underwriter reads it, applies the carrier's underwriting manual (usually licensed from a reinsurer), and assigns a class: preferred plus, preferred, standard, or a table rating with a flat extra. That class fixes the price of a policy that may stay in force for thirty years.

The manual gives debits and credits for each impairment, but the expert's value lies in what the manual cannot say. An echo result that is technically normal but has trended over three studies. A cardiologist's letter whose hedged phrasing means the doctor is worried. A pattern of missed follow-ups that says more about mortality than the diagnosis itself. A combination of three mild impairments that the manual scores independently and the experienced underwriter knows interact. Underwriters describe this as developing a feel for a file, and the industry's own audits show how much rides on it: when accelerated decisions are fully underwritten after the fact, about one case in five lands in a different class. The SOA's 2024 monitoring of 30 accelerated programmes found ~81% agreement on audited cases.

The outcome of each judgement is recorded, eventually, with great precision. Carriers run actual-to-expected mortality studies by issue year, class, face band and underwriting path, and report experience to the NAIC under VM-51. They do not join the outcome back to the underwriter, the evidence that drove the class, or the reasoning in the case notes. Meanwhile the senior underwriting workforce is ageing, accelerated programmes have taken the easy cases away from the juniors who used to learn on them, and the cases that reach a human are the hardest ones.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the reasoning lives in the underwriter's head and a few lines of free-text case notes. The workbench records the final class, the debits applied and the requirements ordered. It does not record which pages of the APS drove the decision, which findings were discounted and why, or how confident the underwriter was. Capturing that means instrumenting the expert during the task, while they work a queue measured in cases per day, and every extra click is a tax on the most expensive person in new business. The source documents are themselves hard: scanned, handwritten, out of order, from dozens of EHR systems, and governed by HIPAA authorisations and the carrier's own retention rules.

The labelling problem is double. The outcome label, death, is rare and slow: a cohort of underwritten lives takes a decade or more to produce credible mortality by class, and it is confounded by lapse, since the healthiest lives lapse first and the remaining block worsens. The judgement label is unstable: underwriters disagree with each other, and with themselves when the same file comes back months later. Underwriting audits show this, and so do the reinsurers' facultative quotes, which often differ by a table or more on the same case. So any model trained to copy one underwriter learns that underwriter's noise. A model trained on mortality alone has too few deaths per impairment to learn anything.

The deployment problem is speed and accountability. The model has to be faster than the underwriter at the point of decision, which means a summarised, page-linked view of the APS within minutes of arrival, not a score that arrives after the case is closed. It must also leave the underwriter accountable. Carriers answer to regulators who, under New York's Circular Letter No. 7 and Colorado's governance regulation, now expect documented testing of any model or external data that influences an underwriting decision.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A reading assistant first and a judgement model second. Every incoming APS is converted into a structured, page-linked medical timeline: diagnoses, test results with trends, medications and adherence, specialist opinions, follow-up gaps. Each finding is mapped to the carrier's manual, so the underwriter starts from a proposed debit-credit worksheet and edits it rather than building it from scratch. The underwriter's edits, overrides and short confirmations become the captured reasoning the workbench never stored.

On top of that record, learn the experts' recurring reads. Pair the extracted findings and the senior underwriters' adjustments with the outcomes that are observable early: reinsurer facultative decisions, post-issue audit classes, contestable-period claims, and early-duration lapse and mortality. Then extend to long-term mortality through the carrier's and its reinsurers' experience. Use the model to show a junior underwriter what a senior one would have flagged, to route cases to the right level of expertise, and to measure consistency across the team.

## Impact If Solved
Underwriting cycle time on fully underwritten cases falls from weeks to days, the senior underwriters' judgement survives their retirement, and the carrier can see which underwriting decisions carry mortality information. On a block where a one-class misclassification costs on the order of 10%–25% of expected mortality for the affected lives (estimate), a few points of improvement in class accuracy across a large new-business book are worth more than the entire underwriting budget.
