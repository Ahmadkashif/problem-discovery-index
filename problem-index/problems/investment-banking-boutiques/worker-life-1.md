# The Analyst Building Pitches That Will Not Win

**Industry:** [[investment-banking-boutiques|Investment Banking Boutiques]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** First- and second-year analysts spend most of their nights building pitch books on spec, most of which do not convert, rebuilding pages a colleague built last month for a different logo.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #word-embeddings #k-nearest-neighbors #transformers #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
A sector MD has a meeting on Thursday with the founder of a company that might sell in eighteen months. On Monday evening the analyst is told to put together a book: situation overview, industry landscape, trading comps, precedents, an illustrative valuation range, a list of likely buyers, and the firm's credentials in the sector. The analyst opens three old books from the same sector, copies the pages that look closest, re-spreads the comps, updates the logos, and starts the long cycle of page turns — the associate marks up a printout, the VP marks up the associate's version, the MD changes the storyline on Wednesday night and half the pages are rebuilt.

Most of these pitches do not become mandates. A boutique might pitch several times for every engagement it wins, and some pitches are simply relationship maintenance with no mandate in prospect. The analyst knows this, and builds the book anyway at full intensity, because the formatting standard is absolute and a misaligned logo is noticed in a way that a weak argument is not.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The hours are the job's defining feature — eighty- and ninety-hour weeks are normal in a live deal period, and the industry's own attempts to cap them (protected Saturdays, hour caps announced by several large banks) are widely reported to be honoured unevenly. What makes the pitch work particularly corrosive is that it feels pointless: the analyst is re-deriving pages that already exist somewhere in the firm's drive, for a meeting that will mostly go nowhere, and learning very little after the first dozen.

The skill the analyst came to learn — how a business is valued, why a buyer would pay for it, how a story is built for a board — is crowded out by production. Analysts who leave after two years often say they spent the majority of their time on formatting and reconciliation rather than analysis.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A firm-wide page library that is actually searchable: every page from every past book indexed by sector, page type, company and date, so that "the best recent industry landscape page for specialty chemicals" is a query rather than an hour of opening files. Draft pages generated from that library and from current data — comps refreshed, credentials filtered to the relevant sector and deal size, logos and numbers updated — so the analyst starts from a defensible draft rather than a blank or stale template.

Markup capture: the MD's and VP's edits recorded against the draft, so the firm learns which storylines and page types a given MD actually wants, and the next analyst does not rediscover them at midnight.

## Impact If Solved
Pitch production is the largest single block of junior banker time not tied to a live mandate. Cutting the rebuild and reformat portion — plausibly half of it — returns evenings to the analyst class and frees the hours that remain for the analysis they were hired to learn.
