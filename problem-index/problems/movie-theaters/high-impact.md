# The Film Buyer's Monday Holdover Call

**Industry:** [[movie-theaters|Movie Theaters]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every Monday a film buyer decides, theatre by theatre, which films keep their screens and showtimes for the week, from weekend grosses and a memory of how each market behaves, and nobody ever grades the call against what the seats, the snack bar and the film rent would have done under a different one.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #bayesian-inference #time-series-forecasting #causal-inference #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A film buyer at a regional circuit is responsible for anywhere from a few dozen to several hundred screens (~150–400 is typical, estimate). An independent booking agent does the same job for many small owners at once. The week has a fixed rhythm. Weekend grosses arrive Sunday night and Monday morning. Monday and Tuesday are spent on holdover calls with each distributor's sales office: which films stay, which lose a screen or drop to matinees, which leave. The new films opening Friday are slotted around them, and showtimes go to the ticketing system by mid-week so that Fandango and the circuit's app can sell them (cycle timing recalled from trade practice, estimate). Multiplexes "wait to see how current films perform on Friday and Saturday" before fixing most of the next week's showtimes (Marketplace).

Every decision trades screens against terms. The rent is usually a sliding scale agreed per film, so the distributor takes the largest share in the first weeks and less as the run ages. A film in its fourth week that fills a third of a small auditorium may earn the theatre more per seat than a new opener in the same room, because more of the ticket stays in the building and the audience still buys popcorn. Distributors also expect commitments: a minimum run length, a number of screens, showtimes in the large house. The buyer weighs all of it in a phone call.

What experienced buyers know is mostly local and mostly unwritten. This suburb turns out for faith-based releases and ignores prestige dramas. A horror sequel collapses in the college town on its second weekend but holds in the blue-collar town across the river. The Spanish-language audience in one market buys on Sunday afternoon, not Friday night. A family film's Saturday matinee gross is a better predictor of its third week than its opening Friday. A CMX Cinemas executive called scheduling "an art, honestly." This is tacit knowledge in the craftsman's sense: pattern recognition built across hundreds of releases and thousands of theatre-weeks that the buyer can't fully state. When a senior buyer retires or a circuit is sold, the next buyer starts over.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the buyer's reasoning is never captured. The ticketing system records what was booked and what it sold; it doesn't record what the buyer expected, which alternatives were weighed, or which concession the distributor extracted in exchange for the big house. The decision is made on the phone and in a spreadsheet the buyer built. Capturing it means asking a person with forty holdover calls to make on a Monday to record an expectation for each, and that only happens if recording takes seconds and returns something useful the same day.

The labelling problem is that the outcome is a noisy, censored grade of the decision. A film's second-week gross depends on the competition that opened against it, the weather and the distributor's marketing spend, none of which the buyer controls. Only the booked option is ever observed: nobody sees what the film would have done on two more screens. Buyers disagree with themselves too. The same buyer reads the same opening differently after a run of surprises, so labels need repeated judgements on a sample to measure how consistent the expert is before a model is trained to match them. The true objective is theatre profit (admissions net of film rent, plus concession margin), and the rent side sits in settlement records that are often negotiated after the run.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. Recommendations have to arrive Sunday night, before the buyer picks up the phone, and be faster to read than the buyer's own glance at the grosses. Buyers are judged on their relationships with distributors and won't follow a model that ignores a commitment they made last month. The larger circuits have analysts but guard their methods. The independents, where the knowledge is thinnest, can't afford a data team, and their bookers work across many owners' theatres with no shared system.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A booking ledger first, then a model of the buyer's read. Every booking decision (screens, auditorium sizes, showtime count by daypart, holdover or drop) is attached to a one-line expectation drafted from the system's forecast and adjusted by the buyer with a click: "expect ~$4,000 next weekend, holding for the Sunday Spanish crowd." Each record is later graded against what the theatre actually earned net of the rent at the settled terms, and against a counterfactual estimate from comparable theatres that made the other call.

On top of the ledger, learn each market's patterns from the history the circuit already has: theatre-by-film-by-day admissions and concession sales, showtimes, auditorium capacities, local demographics, the competition opening each week, and the buyer's past overrides of the national forecast. The model's job isn't to replace the buyer but to make a senior buyer's local read available, with its track record, to a junior buyer or to the booking agent covering forty small towns. It surfaces "the last six family films in this market held 70% into week three; you're dropping this one at 45%" before the call.

## Impact If Solved
Screen time is the exhibitor's scarcest asset and film rent its largest cost (~49–57% of admissions at the two largest public circuits, derived from filings). A booking process that holds the right films a week longer and drops the wrong ones a week earlier moves a few percent of admissions to the theatre's side of the split and fills more seats at the snack bar, where margins run ~80% (estimate from ~20% concession cost ratios). Across ~$8.9B of annual box office even a one-point improvement in net film margin is worth ~$90M a year (estimate). Keeping a retiring buyer's market knowledge in the circuit, rather than losing it with the person, is the second prize, and for independents served by booking agents it is the difference between an agent's memory and a shared record.
