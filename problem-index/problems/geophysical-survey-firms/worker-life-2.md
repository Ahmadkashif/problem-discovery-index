# The Report Written After the Field Day

**Industry:** [[geophysical-survey-firms|Geophysical Survey Firms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The project geophysicist who processed and interpreted the data also writes the report, builds thirty map figures and drafts the caveats — usually at night, for three projects at once, under a client's design-meeting deadline.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
At a small geophysical firm, one person carries a project from field to signature. After the crew demobilises, the project geophysicist processes the data, picks the targets, and then writes the deliverable: methods, equipment and settings, field conditions, QC results, an interpretation narrative, limitations, and a stack of figures — plan maps of each method, depth slices, profiles with annotated hyperbolas, anomaly tables, a CAD file for the designer. A routine utility designating or site-characterisation report runs twenty to sixty pages (estimate); a munitions classification report adds dig lists and QC seed tables formatted to the quality plan.

Most of it is re-assembly. Equipment paragraphs, method descriptions and limitation statements are copied from the last report and edited; figures are rebuilt in Oasis montaj or ArcGIS with new extents and legends; the anomaly table is retyped from picks. The client wants it before a design meeting or a regulator's review date, and the same geophysicist has two other projects in processing.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The writing pushes the work that requires judgement — the interpretation — into the smallest slot, and fatigue shows up as errors: a figure with last month's legend, a depth reported in feet that was measured in metres, a caveat left in from a different site. Because the report is signed, the mistakes are personally theirs. Many geophysicists entered the field for the field and the physics; they describe the reporting backlog as the part of the job that makes them consider leaving.

It also blocks growth. A junior who can collect and process cannot yet write a defensible limitations section, so the senior writes all of them and becomes the bottleneck for every project in the office.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A drafting assistant that reads the project's own records — field logs, instrument settings, QC results, processed grids and the interpreter's picks — and produces a first draft of the methods, QC and results sections, an anomaly table generated directly from picks, and a figure set rebuilt to the firm's template, every statement linked to the record it came from. Limitations are proposed from the actual site conditions logged (clay soils, rebar density, GPS outages) rather than pasted boilerplate. The geophysicist edits the interpretation and signs.

## Impact If Solved
Cutting report assembly from one to three days per project to a few hours gives each project geophysicist back on the order of a working day a week, removes the copy-paste errors that undermine signed work, and lets juniors draft reports under review earlier in their careers.
