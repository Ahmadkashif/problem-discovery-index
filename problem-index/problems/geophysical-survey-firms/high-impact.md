# The Anomaly Call That Never Meets Its Dig

**Industry:** [[geophysical-survey-firms|Geophysical Survey Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every target a geophysicist calls in a radargram, magnetic grid or EM map is later excavated, cored or drilled by someone else, and because that result never returns to the interpreter, the industry's core skill is neither measured nor kept when the interpreter retires.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #object-detection #transfer-learning #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A near-surface interpreter opens a day's data. On a GPR profile across a city intersection there are forty hyperbolas; some are pipes and conduits, some are cobbles in the fill, one is a buried slab edge, two are reflections off a parked truck. On an EM61 grid over a former artillery range there are 1,200 anomalies above threshold; a handful are intact projectiles, most are fragments, horseshoes and fence wire. On a magnetic map over a mining claim there are six dipoles a junior explorer wants ranked for drilling. The interpreter decides which features are real, what they probably are, how deep, and how confident to be. That decision becomes a utility line on a design plan, a row on a dig list, a drill collar, a mark on a slab.

The calls that separate a senior interpreter from a junior one are the least written down. The hyperbola whose apex "rings" in a way that means a metallic pipe rather than a rock. The soil whose velocity is wrong because it rained, so every depth is off by a third. The EM decay that looks like a projectile but sits at a depth no projectile fired on that range could reach. The linear anomaly that is a utility because it lines up with a valve box two hundred feet away, not because of anything in the trace. Interpreters describe this as an eye they developed over years: "not all hyperbolas are utilities" is the first lesson a GPR locator learns (Sensors & Software), and the rest of the lessons are never written as rules.

Unusually for tacit knowledge, the outcome of almost every call is eventually observed. QL-B utility designations are checked when QL-A test holes are vacuum-excavated under ASCE 38-22. Every item on a munitions dig list is excavated and identified, and DoD experience is that about 95% of excavated objects are not munitions. Every core drilled through a scanned slab confirms or refutes the marks; every exploration hole tests a target. But the excavation log, the test-hole report and the drill log belong to the client, arrive after the project has closed, and are filed by project. The firm keeps a folder of reports; it does not keep a record of which of its calls were right.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the interpreter's reasoning is never captured at the moment of the call. Picks are made as points and lines in Oasis montaj, RADAN or a CAD file, stripped of the confidence and the reason, and the processed data, the picks and the deliverable live in different files. Capturing the expert would mean recording what they looked at and how sure they were, during the busiest hours of the project, with no visible return to them. The ground truth, meanwhile, has to be pulled back from the client: test-hole sheets from the SUE designer, dig results from the munitions contractor, core records from the general contractor, drill logs from the explorer.

The labeling problem is that ground truth is partial and biased. Only the anomalies selected for excavation are verified; the ones called "not a target" are rarely dug, so misses surface only as utility strikes or a munitions item found later. Excavation records are themselves inconsistent: a test hole that finds nothing may mean the line was misplaced or that the crew dug in the wrong spot. And interpreters disagree with themselves: shown the same radargram a month apart, an experienced interpreter will call marginal hyperbolas differently, and two seniors at the same firm rank the same EM anomalies in different orders. Site conditions — clay that attenuates GPR, magnetic soils, rebar-dense slabs — change what "looks like" a target, so a model trained in sandy Florida fill fails in Midwestern clay.

The deployment problem is speed and liability. Field interpretation happens on a tablet before the crew leaves site, or overnight before a design meeting; any assistance must be faster than the interpreter's own scan of the data and must not slow a crew that bills by the day. And the signer carries the liability: a geophysicist who accepts a model's call and misses a gas line owns the miss. Tools will be adopted only if they rank and explain rather than decide.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A verified-call ledger first, a model second. Every anomaly pick is stored with its location, depth, interpreted class, confidence and a short reason drafted from the interpreter's own annotations, and every downstream verification — test hole, dig result, core, drill hole — is joined back to the pick that predicted it, with offsets and outcome. Over a few hundred projects the firm can say, by method, soil type, target class and interpreter, how often its calls were right and by how much its depths were off.

On top of the ledger, learn the experienced interpreter's eye. Train detectors on radargram and EM-grid patches labelled by verified outcome rather than by pick, with site-condition features (soil velocity, attenuation, clutter density) so the model knows when it is out of its depth. Surface ranked candidates with the interpreter's historical accuracy on similar features, so a junior sees "senior interpreters called this pattern a utility 82% of the time, verified in 61 test holes" instead of a bare score.

## Impact If Solved
A firm that knows its own verified hit rate can price risk, defend its work, and train juniors on graded examples rather than on whoever is in the office. For munitions response, where clutter is about 95% of what gets dug, every percentage point of debris removed from a dig list without missing a target of interest is weeks of excavation saved per site; for utility designating, fewer misplaced lines means fewer redesigns and strikes. Retaining a senior interpreter's verified pattern library through retirement turns the industry's most expensive recurring loss into something the firm keeps.
