# Designating in Live Traffic

**Industry:** [[geophysical-survey-firms|Geophysical Survey Firms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A utility designating technician spends the day walking EM locators and GPR carts along highway shoulders, medians and intersections with their eyes on a screen instead of the traffic — and the corridors that matter most are the ones with the least protection.
**Tags:** #cnns #object-detection #optimization-fundamentals #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation

## The Problem
QL-B designating under ASCE 38-22 covers the project corridor, and on highway, transit and intersection projects the utilities run under the travel lanes. To trace a line with an electromagnetic locator the technician follows it wherever it goes, across turn lanes and through intersections; to cross a corridor with GPR they push the cart across the road. Lane closures need DOT or city permits, cost money for traffic control, and are often restricted to nights or off-peak windows, so much of the work happens from a shoulder behind a few cones and a truck with an arrow board, or at night under portable lights (estimate).

The task demands attention in two places at once. A locator's signal strength and depth readout, or a GPR screen showing hyperbolas, needs the technician's eyes; the traffic passing a metre away needs them too. Crews are often two people, and the spotter role is the first thing cut when a job is tight. In summer the asphalt is far hotter than the air, and the technician walks it for hours in high-visibility gear; in winter the night shifts stack on top of day shifts when a DOT schedule slips.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is personal risk. US work-zone crashes kill hundreds of people a year (estimate), and a designating crew has none of the barrier protection a paving crew gets, because its work zone moves every few minutes. Near-misses on shoulder work — a mirror passing close, a distracted driver drifting toward the cones — go unrecorded and never appear in a bid (judgement; no first-hand worker account was found in research).

The heat and the hours wear on the body and on sleep. Night closures mean switching between day and night shifts within a week, and a technician who is tired is slower to notice a car coming. Firms bear the schedule; the technician bears the exposure.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Less time on foot in live lanes, and warning when that time can't be avoided. A pre-job plan built from utility records, imagery and the corridor geometry that predicts which segments can be designated from the shoulder, which need a crossing, and which justify a proper closure — so exposure is planned rather than improvised. Vehicle-towed multi-channel GPR and EM arrays that collect a closed lane at speed and leave the picking to the office, so the technician is in the lane for minutes rather than hours. And a camera on the work truck that detects a vehicle entering the work zone and warns the technician through a wearable vibration before they look up.

## Impact If Solved
Fewer hours on foot in active lanes per corridor, fewer improvised crossings, and seconds of warning on the rare intrusion — the difference between a near-miss and a fatality. Planned closures and towed collection also cut the night shifts that leave technicians exhausted for the following day's work.
