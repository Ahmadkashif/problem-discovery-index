# The Cataloguer Behind the Backlog

**Industry:** [[libraries-and-archives|Libraries & Archives]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The cataloguer who can describe a Ukrainian pamphlet, a donated local history collection or a 19th-century map is often the last one in the building, working a backlog that grows faster than she can clear it while also fixing the bad vendor records nobody else sees.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #k-nearest-neighbors #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation #quick-win

## The Problem
Most of what a library acquires arrives with a usable record: copy cataloguing pulls a WorldCat record (OCLC added 25.3 million new records in FY2025) or a vendor's shelf-ready record and loads it. What remains is the hard tail — foreign-language and non-Roman-script material, gifts and donated collections, self-published and local books, theses, maps, scores, media and special collections items — that needs original cataloguing: descriptive fields under RDA, subject headings from LCSH, a call number in Library of Congress or Dewey classification, name authority work, and increasingly linked-data identifiers.

That tail lands on a shrinking group of people. Technical services departments have been cut for two decades, and the cataloguers with language and subject skills are retiring. The backlog sits on shelving trucks for months or years, invisible to patrons because an uncatalogued item cannot be found. Meanwhile the same cataloguer handles record-quality fixes: duplicate records, wrong editions attached, vendor records with missing subjects, and batch loads that break the catalogue.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Original cataloguing is skilled, slow work, and it is measured badly: administrators see a backlog count, not the judgement in each record. Cataloguers describe the job as constant triage — which truck to work, which records to accept as "good enough," which fixes to ignore — and the guilt of knowing that whatever they skip disappears from discovery. The isolation matters too: in a small or mid-size library there may be one cataloguer, with no one to check a hard subject heading or a name authority question.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A cataloguing assistant that sits in the cataloguer's editor. For an item in hand — title page photo, table of contents, any digital text — it drafts a full record: transcribed descriptive fields, candidate LCSH headings and classification numbers with the evidence for each, matched name authorities, and the nearest existing records in the shared catalogue so duplicates are caught before they are created. Confidence is shown per field; the cataloguer accepts, edits or rejects, and each correction teaches the assistant. Batch-load quality checks flag vendor records missing subjects or attached to the wrong edition before they reach the public catalogue.

## Impact If Solved
If a draft record cuts original cataloguing time from an estimated ~45–60 minutes to ~15 minutes for the common hard cases (estimate), one cataloguer clears several times as many items, backlogs measured in years shrink to months, and the cataloguer's time moves from typing fields to the judgements only she can make.
