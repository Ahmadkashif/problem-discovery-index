# Takedowns Counted, Effect Unmeasured

**Industry:** [[brand-protection-firms|Brand Protection Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** The contract is priced and renewed on listings removed, the same operators return within days under new accounts, and nobody measures whether counterfeit sales actually fell.
**Tags:** #causal-inference #survival-analysis #graph-neural-networks #bayesian-inference #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A brand protection programme reports removals. Listings taken down, sellers suspended, domains seized, apps delisted — totalled monthly, trended upward, and presented as the programme's output. Brands renew on those numbers.

The numbers measure activity. Practitioners describe the observable pattern candidly: a removed listing is replaced by the same operator under a new account within days, frequently with the same images, the same supply and the same customers arriving through the same search terms. A rising takedown count is consistent with a programme working and equally consistent with a counterfeit operation expanding, and nothing in the report distinguishes them.

The outcome that matters is not measured because it is difficult and because nobody has been required to measure it. Whether the brand's own sales rose, whether the price of the counterfeit fell or rose, whether the volume of infringing supply at any moment declined, whether consumers encountering the brand were less likely to receive a fake — these are the questions, and the reporting answers none.

Enforcement is also aimed at the least effective layer. Removing a listing costs the operator minutes; removing the seller account costs a little more; disrupting the payment channel, the logistics route or the supply source costs substantially more and is done far less often, because the listing is what the platform process makes easy to remove and what the contract counts.

And the brand cannot judge the programme. With no effect measure, a brand comparing two vendors, or deciding how much to spend, has only the volume number — which rewards whichever vendor files the most notices, regardless of accuracy or effect.

## Why It's Unsolved
The counterfactual is hard and has been treated as impossible. What would infringing supply have been without enforcement requires either a comparison group or variation in enforcement intensity, and programmes enforce everywhere continuously because that is what they are paid to do.

The brand's own sales data — the closest thing to an outcome — sits with the brand and is not shared with the enforcement vendor, and the relationship between counterfeit supply and genuine sales is confounded by everything else affecting demand.

The commercial structure is the main obstacle and it is explicit here. A vendor paid per notice, or renewed on notice volume, has no reason to build a measure that might show the notices accomplish little. The same structure rewards volume over precision, which is also the mechanism behind the overreach problem.

And the platforms hold data that would settle much of it. Marketplace operators can see whether a removed seller's customers went to another infringing seller or to the brand, and that view is not shared with the enforcement layer.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Measure infringing supply, not enforcement actions. The observable quantity is the volume of apparently infringing listings available at a point in time, at what prices, from how many distinct operators — and tracking it continuously turns a takedown count into a stock measure. A programme that removes a thousand listings a month while the standing supply is flat is buying nothing.

Create variation deliberately. Enforcement intensity can be varied across product lines, marketplaces or geographies for defined periods, and the resulting comparison is the only honest way to establish what enforcement achieves. It costs a brand something and it is the experiment that would settle a category-wide question.

Measure at the operator level. Whether an enforcement action removed an operator from the market or moved them to a new account is determinable from the detection record — image reuse, shipping patterns, pricing behaviour, account creation timing — and operator survival after enforcement is a far better effect measure than listing counts.

Compare interventions. Listing removal, account suspension, payment disruption, logistics disruption and supply-source action have very different costs and very different effects on operator survival, and a firm with the detection record can estimate which actually work. That is the analysis that would move enforcement to the layer where it matters.

## Impact If Solved
Brands spend substantially on programmes measured by an output that is consistent with total failure, and the pricing model rewards volume over both precision and effect. A standing supply measure, operator-level survival analysis and deliberate enforcement variation would let a brand know what it is buying — and would move spend toward the payment, logistics and supply interventions that the current metric makes invisible.
