# Captioning a Lecture You Have Never Seen

**Industry:** [[court-reporting-firms|Court Reporting Firms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A CART captioner writes live text for a deaf student through a two-hour organic chemistry lecture with no slides, no syllabus and no partner, and every error appears on the student's screen as it happens.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #word-embeddings #k-nearest-neighbors #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #quick-win

## The Problem
CART providers, most of them trained stenographers or voice writers, write realtime captions for deaf and hard-of-hearing people in classrooms, workplaces, medical appointments and public meetings. Increasingly they work remotely, booked through a captioning agency or a university's disability services office, sometimes for several back-to-back sessions across time zones. BLS expects CART demand to grow as the population ages.

The captioner rarely gets what she needs to prepare. She needs the instructor's slides, the reading list, the names of the people who will speak, and the technical vocabulary for the week. Without them she meets reaction mechanisms, drug names, case citations or software jargon cold, at speaking speed. In a courtroom a reporter can stop the proceeding to ask. In a lecture she cannot, and nobody fixes the transcript tonight, because the live text is the service. Budgets usually pay for one captioner per session, so there is no partner to switch with on a long class. Each error shows at once on the screen of a person relying on it to follow the lecture.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The pressure is constant and personal. The student is not an abstract client but someone whose grade depends on reading what the professor said, and captioners describe carrying that responsibility through every session. Meanwhile, institutions offer automatic captions as a cheaper substitute, so the captioner's skill is judged against a free tool on exactly the technical and multi-speaker content where she outperforms it. That case is hard to make without preparation.

The work is also unstable. Sessions are cancelled when the student is absent, rates are set by agencies, and the captioner has no evidence of her own accuracy to bring to a rate conversation.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A preparation assistant that pulls whatever the course or meeting has published, such as the syllabus, slide decks, reading lists, past session transcripts and the speaker list. It turns them into a ranked list of terms and names with suggested briefs and dictionary entries, loaded into the captioner's CAT software before the session. It should learn from her past sessions which terms she struggled with in this course and surface them first. After the session, a sampled accuracy report on technical terms gives her and the client evidence of what human captioning delivers.

## Impact If Solved
Thirty minutes of automated preparation per course week (estimate) could turn the hardest sessions into manageable ones and raise accuracy on exactly the terms that matter to the student. It would give the captioner a factual answer when an institution proposes replacing her with automatic captions.
