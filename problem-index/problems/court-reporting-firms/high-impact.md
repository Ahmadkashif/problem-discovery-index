# The Reporter Who Knows When to Stop the Room

**Industry:** [[court-reporting-firms|Court Reporting Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced stenographer knows within a second which passages her writing or the audio will not survive, and whether to interrupt or write through. That judgement decides whether the certified record has gaps, and it is retiring with the people who hold it.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #transformers #lstms-and-grus #seq2seq #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #compliance

## The Problem
A stenographic reporter writes phonetic chords at 200-plus words a minute. CAT software translates them through the reporter's personal dictionary, tens of thousands of entries and briefs built over a career (estimate), and streams the text to counsel's screens in realtime. On top of the mechanical skill sit three judgements that nobody writes down.

The first is in the room. Two lawyers talk over each other, a witness mumbles a dosage, counsel reads an exhibit aloud at double speed, an engineer uses a part number nobody has spelled. The reporter decides in about a second whether to stop the proceeding ("I'm sorry, could you repeat the dosage?") or keep writing and recover the passage later. Interrupt too often and the examining lawyer loses rhythm and complains to the agency. Interrupt too rarely and the transcript carries a gap nobody can fill, because an answer given over someone else's question may not be on the audio either. Veterans make this call well and juniors make it badly, and neither can say how. The second judgement is in the writing. Reporters know their own conflicts, the strokes that translate to two different words, and which briefs keep realtime readable when an expert starts using a new term. The third is in the edit, done that night: resolving untranslated and mistranslated strokes from context, audio and the exhibit list, then certifying the result under licence.

The workforce holding this judgement is old. NCRA's Ducker study put the median reporter at 51 in 2013, and nearly half of Texas's certified shorthand reporters were first certified 30 or more years ago. The replacements are mostly voice writers and digital reporters. A digital reporter is trained in weeks, monitors a multi-channel recording, and faces the same interrupt-or-continue decision without any stroke-level signal of trouble. Even the transcriptionist who later types the audio cannot ask the witness to repeat anything.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's judgement is only half-recorded. CAT files keep every stroke with a timestamp, its translation, the synchronised audio and the edits that followed, so the decision to correct is logged. The decision to interrupt shows only as a colloquy line ("THE REPORTER: I'm sorry, could you repeat that?"), and the decision not to interrupt leaves no trace at all. The files sit on independent contractors' laptops under each reporter's own dictionary. Many reporters overwrite the raw translation when they edit, and no agency pools the files, because the reporter, not the agency, certifies the transcript and owns the notes.

The labelling challenge is that the certified transcript is both the output and the only ground truth. Independent checks are rare: errata from witnesses, a disputed passage read back in court, a motion over the record. Reporters disagree with themselves. The same reporter resolves an ambiguous stroke differently at 11 p.m. on a rough draft than three days later on the final, and two reporters' dictionaries make the same stroke mean different things. Any model needs a per-reporter component and a pooled one, plus a deliberate re-edit sample to measure the expert's own consistency.

The deployment challenge is time and trust. A realtime signal has to arrive inside the second the reporter has to decide, without cluttering the screen she is writing to. Reporters also fear, reasonably, that a log of their errors becomes evidence in a licensing complaint or a talking point for the digital side. A tool that measures accuracy must be owned by the reporter and be faster than her own instinct, or it will be switched off.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Pair each reporter's stroke stream, multi-channel audio and colloquy markers with the final certified transcript, and learn per passage how likely the realtime translation is to be wrong, and whether the audio will be good enough to fix it later. During the deposition that becomes a quiet margin flag: "dosage at 10:42, low translation confidence, channels overlapping, audio unlikely to resolve." The reporter keeps the decision, but now knows which moments cannot be recovered later. After the deposition it becomes an edit queue ordered by risk, and a running map of the reporter's own conflicts, suggesting dictionary entries before the same mistranslation recurs.

Run on audio alone, the same model gives a digital reporter's monitor the cue a stenographer gets from her own writing. It also gives students a measurable path from 225 words a minute to writing a record that needs little editing. Kept by the reporter and shared at her choice, the accuracy history becomes evidence for the first time that a particular capture method, in particular hands, produces a reliable record.

## Impact If Solved
If an experienced reporter spends roughly an hour or two editing for every hour on the record (estimate), ordering the edit by risk and resolving recurring conflicts could recover a large share of that time on every job. That gives back evenings and makes same-night rough drafts routine rather than heroic. Fewer unrecoverable gaps means fewer disputed passages and settled statements, a faster route to competence for the new voice writers and digital reporters entering the field, and a factual basis for the licensing fight that both sides now argue from anecdote.
