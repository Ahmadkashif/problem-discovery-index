# Paid by the Page, Booked by Someone Else

**Industry:** [[court-reporting-firms|Court Reporting Firms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A freelance reporter takes jobs from several agencies without seeing what the client paid, what a late cancellation will cost her, or which accounts actually pay for her day once waiting time and scopist fees come out.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #linear-regression #time-series-forecasting #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Most deposition reporters are independent contractors. BLS puts a third of the occupation in self-employment, and Census payroll is under a quarter of the industry's receipts. A freelancer typically works for two or three agencies and some direct clients. Each agency offers jobs by text with an appearance fee and a page rate it sets, and keeps the price it charges the law firm, the expedite surcharge and most of the copy sales to the other parties. Veritext alone drew on more than 11,500 independent reporters in 2018, and the rollups' acquisitions since have left fewer agencies to work for in many cities.

The reporter carries the variance. A deposition cancelled the night before may pay nothing, or a short minimum, depending on an agency policy she rarely sees in writing. A job that starts two hours late, or ends after forty minutes and twelve pages, pays far less per hour than it consumed. Travel, parking, equipment, CAT licence fees and a scopist at roughly a dollar a page (estimate) come out of her side. Agencies decide who gets the long, high-page expert depositions and who gets the short ones, and the decision is not explained.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Income is unpredictable in a profession that already asks two or more years of training and years of practice to reach full speed. A reporter cannot tell which agency or client is worth her mornings, so she accepts almost everything, in case declining costs her future bookings. That is how a day of waiting rooms and cancellations happens. Having no view of the client price also leaves her with no basis for negotiating as rates for transcripts rise. The federal page-rate caps went up roughly 10% in each of FY2024 and FY2025 to help courts recruit and retain reporters, and freelancers have no equivalent signal for their own work.

The loss of control is personal as well as financial. The agency's scheduler, not the reporter, decides her week, and the consolidation of local firms into national agencies means the person deciding increasingly works in another state.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A reporter-owned earnings ledger. It reads job offers, confirmations, invoices and payments from the agencies she works for and computes, per job and per account, the effective hourly rate after waiting, travel, scopist and cancellation losses. It forecasts which bookings are likely to cancel or run short from her own history, and records each agency's actual cancellation and minimum-fee practice. When a new offer arrives, she sees what jobs like it have really paid her. If enough reporters opt in, anonymised rate and cancellation benchmarks by city and agency give freelancers what agencies already have: a view of the market.

## Impact If Solved
Knowing which accounts and job types pay could plausibly move a few hours a week from poorly paid bookings to better ones (estimate), and lift a freelancer's effective rate without working longer. It also gives her documented cancellation and rate histories to negotiate with, and a reason to stay in a profession the supply figures say is losing people faster than it trains them.
