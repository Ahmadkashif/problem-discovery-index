# The Bid-Day Quote Only the Veteran Can Build

**Industry:** [[electrical-distributors|Electrical Distributors]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** The day after this is solved, a two-year quotation specialist can turn a plan set, a Division 26 spec and a contractor's half-finished list into a complete, compliant, competitively priced quote as reliably as the region's twenty-year veteran, and the distributor finally learns which of its quoting habits win jobs.

## The Problem
Every electrical distributor that does project business has a handful of people the contractors ask for by name. Three days before a bid, an electrical contractor emails the drawings for a medical office building, the electrical specification, a lighting fixture schedule, two addenda and a material list its estimator built in Accubid. The list says "MDP per E-601," "LP-1 thru LP-6," 40,000 feet of #12 THHN, and nothing about the fittings, supports, lugs, spare breakers or the generator transfer switch that sits on a different sheet. The veteran reads the panel schedules and one-line diagram, sees that the main switchboard needs a specific short-circuit rating the contractor's list didn't mention, knows that this engineer writes "Square D or approved equal" but rejected a substitute on the last job, notices that addendum 2 changed the fixture type in the corridors, and adds the items every contractor forgets. Then the veteran decides where to ask for special pricing, from which manufacturer, through which rep, and what margin the package can carry against the two other distributors quoting the same contractor.

Almost none of this is written down. The ERP records the quote that went out, if it was entered at all, and later the order if the job was won. It does not record the contractor's original list, the lines the quoter added and why, the substitutions considered and rejected, the SPA requested and granted, or the competing price. Losses are rarely closed out, because the contractor seldom says who won; quote hit rates in project business are commonly ~15–35% (estimate), so most of the record is silent. A busy specialist may build ~5–20 plan-and-spec quotes a week, each ~50–500 lines (estimate), against bid deadlines that bunch up at month end and before holidays.

The knowledge is concentrated and ageing. A strong quoter knows which engineers are strict about basis-of-design, which contractors under-list fittings, which manufacturer's rep will turn an SPA request around before bid day, and which product families have had lead-time trouble that will sink a schedule. When that person retires or moves to a competitor, the branch's hit rate falls and the contractors follow. Newer staff compensate by quoting only what is on the contractor's list, which loses money twice: incomplete quotes create change orders and margin leaks after award, and over-cautious pricing loses the bid. It is tacit knowledge in the classic sense — pattern recognition built from thousands of plan sets, specs, submittal rejections and won and lost jobs that the expert cannot fully specify.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's work happens across a PDF viewer, a spreadsheet, the ERP's quote screen, phone calls to reps and memory. To learn from it, a system must capture the inputs as they arrived (drawings, spec sections, addenda, the contractor's list), every edit the quoter makes to that list, the reasons for additions and substitutions, the SPA requests sent and the prices returned, without asking a specialist on a bid deadline to fill in a form. Historical quotes mostly lack the input side and the edit trail, so the dataset has to be built going forward from instrumented quoting, with history recovered only where the drawing set and the final quote can still be paired.

The labelling challenge is that "correct" has three layers and experts disagree with themselves. A quote can be complete but lose on price, cheap but incomplete, or right on both and still lose because the contractor lost the bid. The win label is missing for most quotes and must be inferred, for example by matching later orders and releases to the job name. Substitution acceptability is only known when the engineer reviews the submittal weeks later, and the same veteran will propose an "or equal" on a design-build job and refuse it on a hard-spec hospital. Honest labels need structured review of a sample by more than one senior quoter, an estimate of how often they agree with each other and with themselves, and outcome tracking for submittal approval, change orders and inferred wins.

The deployment challenge is speed. On bid day the tool must return a checked bill of materials, flagged gaps and suggested SPA requests faster than the veteran can scan the drawings — minutes, not hours — and it must show its evidence (the sheet, the spec paragraph, the past job) so the quoter can trust it. A confident omission that surfaces as a change order after award costs more than the tool saves. Distributors also hesitate to put their best quoters' judgement into a vendor's shared model.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A quote ledger first and a model second. Every project quote is captured with its raw inputs, the contractor's list, each addition, deletion and substitution with a one-click reason, the SPA requests and responses, the final price, and the outcome: won (inferred from orders), lost, or unknown, plus submittal results and post-award change orders. The specialist's corrections to the system's suggestions become labels without extra effort.

On top of the ledger, three capabilities. A completeness model reads the drawing set and spec sections, extracts panel schedules, one-line diagrams and fixture schedules, compares them with the contractor's list and the distributor's history of similar jobs, and flags what is missing or inconsistent. A substitution model scores proposed equals against the spec language and the engineer's past approvals and rejections. A pricing model estimates win probability against margin for the package, conditioned on contractor, competitor count, market and SPA position, so the quoter sees the trade-off rather than guessing. Each suggestion carries its source sheet or paragraph and a calibrated confidence.

## Impact If Solved
If a plan-and-spec quote that takes a specialist ~8–16 hours falls to ~2–4 (estimate), a quote desk can answer more bids per week without hiring, and the fixes that matter most — missed items caught before award, rejected substitutions avoided, prices set with a measured win rate — protect margin on the jobs it wins. The larger gain is the first honest record of what wins electrical projects: a distributor that knows its hit rate by contractor, engineer, product family and margin band can train a new quoter in months, keep its best accounts through retirements, and stop leaving money in the gap between the contractor's list and the job.
