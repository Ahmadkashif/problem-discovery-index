# The Expediter Who Delivers Bad Dates

**Industry:** [[electrical-distributors|Electrical Distributors]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The project manager gets back the hours spent logging into manufacturer portals and the dignity of telling a contractor about a slip before the contractor finds out.
**Tags:** #survival-analysis #gradient-boosting #large-language-models #data-integration #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #automation

## The Problem
After a project is won, someone at the distributor owns it: a project manager or expediter who tracks every purchase order for switchgear, panelboards, transformers, lighting and long-lead fixtures against the contractor's schedule. They read manufacturer order acknowledgements, log into each manufacturer's portal to check status, chase reps for updates, coordinate releases and staging, and answer the contractor's "where's my gear?" calls. IDEA's ProjectNexus page says promised ship dates keep moving per order, that a project can need 18+ portal logins and that ~40% of inside-sales and project staff time goes to chasing dates (IDEA's own figures). Wood Mackenzie put power transformers at an average of 128 weeks in Q2 2025 and pad-mount distribution transformers at ~75 weeks after order in mid-2024, with pad-mount lead times still rising in 2025.

The work is mostly checking and relaying. Dates change in a portal nobody looked at this week; the acknowledgement PDF lists a different catalogue number from the submittal; a release is held because the contractor's site isn't ready. The project manager learns about a slip when the contractor calls angry, and has to explain a date they did not set and could not see coming.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Expediters carry the blame for other people's delays. They sit between a factory that moves dates without explanation and a contractor whose crews, cranes and inspections are booked around the first date. The work is high-stakes and invisible when it goes well, and a single missed slip on a switchboard can cost the contractor weeks and the distributor the account.

It also eats the day. Hours go to logging in, copying dates into spreadsheets and writing the same update email to several people, leaving little time for the part that needs judgement: deciding what to expedite, what to substitute and when to warn the contractor.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A single project board that reads acknowledgements, ship notices and portal status automatically, reconciles them with the submittal and the contractor's schedule, and flags lines whose date moved or is likely to move based on manufacturer, product family and plant history. It drafts the contractor update for the project manager to send, with the evidence attached, and keeps a dated record of every promise. Industry status feeds such as ProjectNexus would plug in as they arrive.

## Impact If Solved
If portal checking and update writing fall from the ~40% of staff time IDEA reports to under 10% (estimate), each project manager can carry more jobs, slips are reported to contractors days earlier, and the person in the middle stops learning about problems from the customer.
