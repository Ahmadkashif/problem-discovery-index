# The Re-Up Decision Made Before the Last Fund Has Shown Anything

**Industry:** [[pension-funds|Pension Funds]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A public pension commits hundreds of millions to a private fund manager's next fund while the last one is still in its J-curve, on an investment officer's read of the team that is never written down in a form that can be graded, and that read leaves when the officer does.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #large-language-models #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A large public plan runs a private markets programme of a few hundred fund relationships and makes dozens of new commitments a year (estimate). Most of those commitments are re-ups. A private equity, real estate or private credit manager raises a new fund every three to four years (estimate), and the pension must decide within the fundraising window whether to commit again. Two to four years into the current fund, its reported IRR is dominated by fees, marks and the J-curve, so the number the board sees says little about whether the manager is still good.

So the decision rests on what the investment officer has seen. Experienced private markets officers describe a feel for a GP that they cannot write down as a rule. Has the team's deal pace changed? Is a senior partner who used to lead every annual meeting now absent? Are portfolio company marks being held up while comparable public multiples fell? Did the GP stop answering questions at the LPAC? Has the "same team, same strategy" pitch quietly become a larger fund in a different deal-size bracket? These reads come from annual meetings, quarterly letters, capital account statements and years of watching the same GP. The memo that goes to the consultant and the board records the conclusion and a performance table, not the signals.

The outcome is recorded perfectly, but ten years later, when the fund's net IRR, multiple and public-market equivalent are known. Nobody joins that outcome back to the officer's stated concerns or the signals present at the time. Meanwhile the officer has moved on, because public pay scales turn investment staff over, and the GP relationship passes to someone who starts the read from zero. The evidence on public-markets hiring is not encouraging: sponsors hire after strong past performance and the hires then deliver no excess return (Goyal and Wahal, 2008). Private markets, where dispersion between managers is widest and the decision least reversible, now carry much of public pension assets: alternatives were 35% of the total in 2022 (Pew).

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the signal lives in the officer's observation of the GP, not in the data room. Capital account statements and quarterly reports are digital. The annual meeting impressions, reference call notes, LPAC exchanges and the "something felt off" are in notebooks, emails or nowhere. A re-up memo written for a public board is cautious by design: it records reasons to approve, not reservations. Capturing the read means capturing it during or straight after the interaction, from a person who is also covering forty other relationships.

The labelling problem is that fund outcomes are noisy, slow and confounded. A fund that lands in the bottom quartile may have been well chosen in a bad vintage. A top-quartile fund may have ridden one lucky exit. The honest label is net performance against a public-market equivalent and against same-vintage, same-strategy peers. Even then experts disagree with themselves: the same officer rates the same GP more harshly after a recent disappointment elsewhere in the programme. Selection is also a problem, because declined re-ups are rarely tracked, yet they are the counterfactual that tells you whether passing was right.

The deployment problem is timing and governance. A re-up recommendation has a fundraising close date set by the GP and a board meeting date set by the calendar. Any support has to arrive while the officer is forming the view, faster than they can recall the history themselves. It also has to sit inside a process where the consultant co-signs and the board approves in public. A score that a board member could read as "the model said no" will not be adopted. A record that shows the officer what they and their predecessors saw last time, and how it turned out, might be.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A commitment ledger first and a model second. Every commitment and every re-up is attached to a short structured record: the thesis for the manager, the specific concerns, the signals observed (team changes, pace, marks, fee terms, conduct at the LPAC), the officer's conviction, and the declined alternatives. The record is drafted automatically from the officer's notes, annual meeting materials, GP letters and capital account statements and confirmed in a minute. Each record is later graded against the fund's net performance relative to vintage peers and a public-market equivalent, with declined re-ups tracked the same way.

On top of the ledger, learn the officers' recurring reads by pairing signals extracted from GP communications and statements with the officers' annotations ("key-person risk", "style drift", "marks stale") and the eventual outcome. Surface those patterns, with their track record, to whoever inherits the relationship. The aim is not to automate the re-up but to pass one officer's pattern recognition to the next, and to tell a CIO which signals have carried information in this programme.

## Impact If Solved
A plan that commits ~$1–3B a year to private funds (estimate for a large state system) and moves even a few re-ups from a deteriorating manager to a better one changes programme returns by tens of basis points over a fund cycle. On a programme of tens of billions that is hundreds of millions of dollars, which comes out of employer contributions if it is missed. Keeping a departed officer's documented read of each GP turns the most expensive recurring loss in a public investment office, staff turnover, into something the plan keeps.
