# The Checker Who Catches the Misfit Before Fabrication

**Industry:** [[drafting-services|Drafting Services]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every sheet a drafting bureau issues passes under one senior checker's eye, and that checker's sense of where the misfit is hiding — the connection that will not bolt up, the elevation that disagrees with the plan, the bar that cannot be placed — is the bureau's real product, unrecorded and impossible to scale.

## The Problem
A steel detailing job for a mid-size commercial building might run to a few hundred erection and shop drawings and several thousand piece marks. The detailers model it in Tekla or SDS2 from the engineer's design drawings, generate the sheets, and hand them to a checker — usually the most experienced person in the bureau, often its owner — before anything goes to the fabricator for submittal. The same pattern holds in rebar detailing, MEP fabrication drawings, millwork shop drawings and architectural redline pickup: a junior produces, a senior checks.

The checker does not read every dimension. They scan a sheet and stop where something feels wrong. A beam-to-column connection where the bolt gauge will clash with the column flange. A top-of-steel elevation that matches the framing plan but not the architectural section the engineer forgot to update. A skewed connection where the detailer took the default angle. A rebar hook that will collide with a column cage in a congested joint. A duct spool that fits the model but not the hanger spacing the installer will use. Experienced checkers describe this as knowing where to look: patterns built over thousands of sheets and the memory of every field fix that came back with their bureau's name on it.

That judgement decides the bureau's economics. A sheet that goes out with an error comes back as "approved as noted" or "revise and resubmit" from the engineer of record, eating days of the 14-day review window AISC 303 allows; or worse, it goes to fabrication, and the error appears at erection, where the fabricator pays for a field fix and back-charges the detailer. The checker is also the bottleneck. One checker reviewing for ten or more detailers (estimate) sets the throughput of the whole bureau, and NISD-linked guidance acknowledges that schedule pressure pushes fabricators to force unchecked drawings into submittal. When the checker retires or moves to a competitor, the bureau keeps its Tekla templates and loses the reason its drawings were trusted.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem comes first. The checker's judgement is expressed as red clouds and notes on a PDF in Bluebeam, or as comments typed into the model, and almost none of it is captured in a structured form. Markups are not linked to the model object they concern, the error category is not recorded, and the checked set is overwritten when the corrected set is issued. To learn the checker's eye, a system has to capture the expert performing the task — every markup, its location, the object it refers to and what kind of problem it flags — without adding a second's work to a review that is already the bureau's constraint.

The labelling problem is that checkers disagree with themselves and with each other. The same checker flags a marginal edge distance on Monday and passes it on Friday when the submittal is due. Some markups are true errors, some are preferences, some are questions for the engineer, and the checker rarely says which. The best label is the downstream outcome — the engineer's review comments, the fabricator's shop queries, the field-fix and back-charge record — but those outcomes sit in the fabricator's and the general contractor's systems, arrive weeks or months later, and are attributed to a project rather than to a sheet or a piece mark. Missed errors are also selected: the errors a checker never flags are only visible if they later fail.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. A checker scans a sheet in minutes. Any assistant must point to the few places worth looking faster than the checker's own scan, with a false-flag rate low enough that they do not stop reading its flags, and it must run on the PDF or model the bureau already has. It also has to survive the economics: a bureau billing ~$90–165 a ton (vendor-published range) cannot spend weeks configuring a system per client.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A capture layer first and a model second. Every checker markup on a PDF or in the model is recorded with its location, linked to the model object or drawing element it concerns, and tagged with a category from a short fixed list — fit-up, elevation conflict, dimension missing, design conflict, standard violation, question for engineer — proposed automatically from the markup's text and confirmed with one click. Every outcome the bureau can obtain is joined back to the sheet and piece mark: engineer review comments, fabricator shop queries, field-fix reports and back-charges.

On top of that record, learn where the checker looks. A model reads each new sheet and model, ranks regions and objects by the probability that the checker would mark them, and explains why with the nearest past markups of the same kind. Rules handle what can be specified — bolt clearances, edge distances, minimum laps — and the learned model handles what the checker cannot write down. The checker reviews the ranked list first and the rest of the sheet second. Over time the record grades the checker too: which markups prevented field fixes, which categories escape most often, and which detailers and clients generate which errors.

## Impact If Solved
If a bureau cut errors reaching fabrication by a third and doubled each checker's throughput, it could take on more tonnage with the same senior staff. Shorter resubmittal cycles would save days inside each 14-day approval window. And the checker's judgement, the reason fabricators trust a bureau's drawings, would stop walking out of the door at retirement. For the fabricators and subcontractors who buy detailing, an honest sheet-level error record would make detailing quality something they can measure when they choose a vendor, instead of something they learn from back-charges.
