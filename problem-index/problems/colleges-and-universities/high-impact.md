# The Aid Offer Nobody Grades

**Industry:** [[colleges-and-universities|Colleges & Universities]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A college sets a different price for every admitted student and adjusts hundreds of them on appeal, yet never records why or measures whether the extra dollars changed anyone's decision, so its single largest revenue lever runs on an aid director's unrecorded read of each family.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #causal-inference #gradient-boosting #large-language-models #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact

## The Problem
At a private college of 2,000 undergraduates the entering class is built between December and May. Admissions decides who gets in. Financial aid decides what each admit pays. The institutional grant comes from a leveraging grid, a table of academic rating against financial need, often set with an enrollment consultancy, which says how much merit and need-based aid each cell receives. Then the real work starts. Families appeal. Some send a competitor's offer, some a letter about a lost job, a medical bill, a divorce or a farm whose assets look larger on paper than in cash. The aid director and two or three senior counsellors decide, case by case, whether to add $2,000, $5,000 or nothing. They also use professional judgment, which federal law lets them apply to a family's FAFSA inputs, to adjust need itself. The counsellors working the verification queue, described in [[problems/colleges-and-universities/worker-life-1|Worker Life 1]], assemble these files but are not among the people who decide them.

The experienced director is good at this, and cannot fully say why. They know which families are shopping offers and will go wherever the number is highest. They know which ones have already decided and want reassurance, and that a phone call will do more than money. They know which competitor offers are real and which high schools send students who melt over the summer. They know when a student who visits twice, asks about the honours programme and appeals for $3,000 is worth $3,000. This is tacit knowledge in the craftsman's sense: pattern recognition built across thousands of files and dozens of May 1 deadlines, held by one person, and lost when that person retires or moves to a rival college.

The outcome of every one of these judgements is recorded. The student deposited or did not, enrolled in the fall or melted, came back for sophomore year or stopped out, graduated or left with debt. The judgement itself is recorded nowhere. The aid system holds the final award, not the reason for the change or the director's estimate of what would happen without it. The college knows its discount rate to a decimal place. It cannot say which of its discount dollars bought enrollment and which were given to students who would have come anyway. That figure decides whether a tuition-dependent college survives the decline in high school graduates.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the director's read has to be captured at the moment of decision, in the busiest six weeks of the year. Appeal decisions are made in email, over the phone and in a shared spreadsheet. Writing down a predicted probability of enrollment and a reason for each one is a tax on people already working evenings. Historic appeals are recoverable only as before-and-after award amounts, which keep the action and lose the judgement.

The labelling problem is that enrollment is a noisy, confounded label for the quality of an aid decision. A student offered more who enrolls might have enrolled anyway; a student offered less who goes elsewhere might have left at any price. Awards follow rules, so the students who got more are systematically different from those who did not, and the effect of a dollar has to be estimated against that selection. Experts also disagree with themselves. The same director is more generous in late April, when the deposit count is behind target, than in March, and grants different amounts to similar families in different years.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. Any assistance has to work inside the aid system, Banner, Colleague, PowerFAIDS or Workday, during the appeal call, faster than the director's own recall. A model that recommends a number without showing which past cases it resembles will be ignored. A model built on cross-campus records from a consultancy runs into FERPA on the half that matters. Applicants and admits who never enrolled are not FERPA students, and their records rest on consultancy contracts nobody has published. The retention and graduation outcomes needed to grade an aid decision belong to enrolled students, and a vendor holding those may use them only for the purpose the college disclosed them for (34 CFR 99.33(a)(2)). So the most informative multi-campus dataset is the one least available.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An aid decision ledger first and a model second. Every appeal and professional-judgment adjustment becomes a short structured record: the trigger (competitor offer, income change, special circumstance), the amount asked and granted, the director's estimate of enrollment probability with and without the change, and a one-line reason. It is drafted automatically from the appeal letter, the family's documents and the call notes, and confirmed in seconds. Each record is graded later against deposit, fall enrollment, second-year retention and graduation.

On top of the ledger, learn the director's read. Pair historic admit records, award changes and appeal texts with outcomes and estimate uplift: how much each additional grant dollar changes the probability of enrollment and retention for students like this one. Correct for the selection the leveraging grid builds in by using the grid's thresholds and year-to-year policy changes as natural experiments. Surface the result in the appeal screen as the five most similar past cases, what was offered and what happened, with the model's estimate beside the director's.

## Impact If Solved
A college that knows which aid dollars buy enrollment can hold its class size at a lower discount rate. At a typical private college with ~$30M in institutional aid (estimate), shifting even 5% of awards from students who would have enrolled anyway to students on the margin is worth ~$1.5M a year in net tuition (estimate). It also keeps the director's judgement on record when the director leaves, and gives the college an evidenced answer when its board, accreditor or rating analyst asks why the discount rate keeps rising.
