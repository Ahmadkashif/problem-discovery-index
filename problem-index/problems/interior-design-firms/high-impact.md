# The Client's Taste Nobody Writes Down

**Industry:** [[interior-design-firms|Interior Design Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced designer reads a client's taste from a few images and reactions and gets a scheme approved in one or two rounds, while a junior takes four or five, and the firm records the surviving selections but never the read that produced them.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #contrastive-learning #transformers #k-nearest-neighbors #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
A residential project begins with a client who cannot say what they want. They bring a Pinterest board with forty pins that contradict each other, a few photographs of rooms they liked in hotels, a sofa they inherited and refuse to part with, and a set of words — "warm", "not too modern", "nothing fussy" — that mean something different to every client who uses them. The senior designer listens, walks the house, watches which samples the client touches and which they push aside, and comes back two weeks later with a concept board, a furniture plan and a palette. Usually the client says yes with minor changes.

That read is tacit knowledge in the strict sense. The designer knows that this client's "warm" means walnut and rust, not cream and brass; that the pins with arched doorways matter and the pins with gallery walls do not; that the client who winced at a velvet swatch will reject every high-sheen fabric however it is presented; that the husband who said nothing in the first meeting is the one who will veto the dining chairs. The designer cannot write down how they know. Asked, they say they "develop an eye for people".

A junior designer given the same inputs produces a competent scheme the client does not like. The second presentation narrows it; the third swaps the sofa; the fourth finally lands. On a flat-fee or capped-fee project, which many residential firms now prefer because clients resist open-ended hourly billing, every extra round is unbilled design time, delays product ordering by weeks, and pushes the install date into the next season. Firms respond by keeping principals in every presentation, which caps how many projects the firm can run and ties the firm's value to one person's eye. When a senior designer leaves, the clients often follow.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the signal lives in the room. The reactions that inform a senior designer — a pause over a swatch, a comment while walking past a showroom vignette, which images the client lingered on — are never captured. Studio software stores the products that ended up on the approved proposal and, at best, the ones that were removed. It does not store what was shown and rejected, in what order, alongside what the client said. Capturing it means asking designers to log reactions during or right after the meeting, and any logging step that takes longer than a minute will be skipped by a designer with six live projects.

The labelling problem is that taste labels are unstable and relational. The same client approves a fabric in a scheme with oak floors and rejects it next to a dark rug. The same designer, shown the same client file twice months apart, will not choose the same three sofas, so there is no single correct answer to learn, only a distribution the designer would accept. "Approved" is also a noisy label: clients approve things to end a long meeting, and the real verdict arrives months later as a return or a complaint after install. Any model needs repeated designer judgements on a sample to estimate how much designers agree with themselves, and that agreement is the ceiling on what a model can match.

The deployment problem is speed and dignity. A senior designer flips through a vendor catalogue and pulls five candidates in the time it takes to open a search tool. A system has to be faster than that, has to work from the trade catalogues the firm actually buys from rather than retail sites, and has to present itself as the designer's memory of this client rather than as a machine that has taste. Designers will reject anything that looks like it is choosing for them, and clients who pay for a designer's eye do not want to learn that a model narrowed the options.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A taste record first and a model second. Every presentation becomes structured data: which products, finishes and images were shown, which were approved, rejected or questioned, and a short reason drafted from the designer's own notes or a recorded debrief and confirmed in a tap. Over a project this becomes a client taste profile in the designer's vocabulary — "rejects sheen", "approves warm woods with matte black metal", "spouse vetoes anything low-slung" — with the evidence behind each line.

On top of the record, learn a joint embedding of product images, fabric and finish swatches, and room photographs from the firm's own history, trained on which items were approved together and which were rejected for which clients. Given a new client's board and early reactions, rank the firm's trade catalogue by predicted approval, explain each suggestion by pointing to the client's own earlier reactions, and flag the items in a draft scheme most likely to be rejected. The junior designer presents a better first round; the senior designer gets a memory of every client they have worked with.

## Impact If Solved
Cutting average presentation rounds on a residential project from four to two removes on the order of 20–40 unbilled design hours per project (estimate) and brings product ordering forward by weeks, which matters because lead times, not design, set most install dates. More valuable over time, it turns the firm's accumulated read of its clients from something that leaves with each senior designer into something the firm keeps and can teach, which is the difference between a studio limited by one principal's calendar and one that can grow.
