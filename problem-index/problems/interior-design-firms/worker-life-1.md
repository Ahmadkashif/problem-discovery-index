# The Designer Who Became an Expediter

**Industry:** [[interior-design-firms|Interior Design Firms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Designers trained to compose rooms spend a large share of every week chasing purchase orders, reading vendor acknowledgements and filing freight claims, and they want that time back for design.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #survival-analysis #gradient-boosting #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration #data-integration

## The Problem
Once a client approves a scheme, the designer's job turns into logistics. Each approved item becomes a purchase order to a to-the-trade vendor. A few days later an acknowledgement arrives as a PDF or an email, and it must be checked line by line against the order: the fabric code and colourway, the leg finish, the dimensions, the quantity, the sidemark that tells the receiver which client it belongs to, the price and the quoted ship date. Errors are common enough that experienced designers check every one. Then the waiting begins: lead times of 8–20 weeks for custom upholstery and case goods are ordinary (estimate), and they slip, often without notice.

The designer spends hours a week emailing vendor reps and customer service desks for status, copying tracking numbers into a spreadsheet, chasing backorders, coordinating with the receiving warehouse that inspects and stores items, and filing freight-damage claims when a crate arrives crushed. The vendor reps change; the portals differ for every manufacturer; the status information that exists is scattered across forty inboxes. Designers commonly describe themselves as procurement coordinators with a design degree, and the logistics share of the week is estimated at 30–50% for a residential designer (estimate).

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The designer took the job to design. The expediting work is invisible to the client, who only notices when it fails, and it lands in the evenings because the daytime is for site visits and presentations. It also carries a particular anxiety: every unchecked acknowledgement is a possible wrong-fabric sofa that will be discovered on install day in front of the client, and the designer is personally blamed even when the vendor made the error.

For juniors and assistants, expediting is often the whole job for the first years. They learn vendor codes rather than design, and many leave the profession before reaching the work they trained for.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An assistant that reads every acknowledgement, invoice, shipping notice and vendor email as it arrives, matches it to the purchase order line, and flags only the mismatches: wrong finish code, changed dimension, missing sidemark, price variance, slipped date. It keeps a live status for every item without anyone typing tracking numbers, predicts the realistic arrival date from the vendor's own history rather than its quote, drafts the chase email when an item goes quiet, and starts the freight claim with photographs and paperwork when the receiver reports damage. The designer reviews exceptions instead of reading everything.

## Impact If Solved
Taking the reading and chasing out of procurement returns something like 8–15 hours a week to a residential designer (estimate), catches wrong-spec acknowledgements before the item ships rather than after it arrives, and gives juniors a path into design work instead of years of expediting.
