# Where the Car Will Be, and Whether to Hook It

**Industry:** [[repossession-services|Repossession Agencies]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A veteran recovery agent reads an assignment and knows which address to sit on, at what hour, and when to drive away from a car that is right there — and that judgement, which decides recovery rate, safety and legal exposure, is recorded nowhere and leaves with the agent.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #feature-engineering #bayesian-inference #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
An agency with three trucks may carry several hundred open assignments at once. Each comes from a lender or forwarder through RDN with the borrower's addresses — home, prior home, employer, references, addresses from a skip trace — a vehicle description, sometimes a licence-plate camera hit from last Tuesday, and a fee paid only if the car comes back. Platform data put recoveries at ~29% of assignments in 2025. The agency cannot work every file every night, so somebody decides which twenty to run, in what order, and at which address and hour.

That somebody is usually the owner or the most experienced agent, and what they know is the textbook case of tacit knowledge. That a borrower who works nights will have the car home between 9 a.m. and 2 p.m., not at 4 a.m. when everyone else runs. That a pickup registered to an apartment complex is more likely parked at the girlfriend's address in the references. That a car scanned three times in one employer lot on weekdays will be there again at 11 on Thursday. That a gated complex with a night guard is a daytime job. That a front porch light, a dog run and three cars in the drive mean this attempt will end in an objection — and that walking away tonight and coming back at a different hour is cheaper than a breach-of-peace claim. None of this is in the assignment. Asked how they choose, agents say they have "a feel for it".

The judgement has three outputs that matter: which address and window to work, whether a camera hit is worth the drive, and whether to attempt or leave when they arrive. Get the first two wrong and the truck burns fuel and hours on files that will not close at a flat fee. Get the third wrong and the agency faces a wrongful-repossession claim, a lost forwarder panel, a licence complaint, or — as agents in Mississippi, Texas and Wisconsin found in 2025 — a gun. When an experienced agent quits or sells the business, the agency keeps the client list and loses the reasoning that made the files close.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's reasoning happens in a truck at night, during the work, and is never written down. What the systems hold is a status update typed afterwards for the lender ("attempted at residence, vehicle not present"), not the choice that preceded it: why this address, why this hour, what the agent saw and why they left. The plate-scan history that would show where a car actually sleeps belongs to the camera network, and the agency sees only hits on its own assignments. Capturing the expert means recording each decision as it is made — address chosen, time, what was observed, attempt or walk away — with almost no typing, or reconstructing it from GPS traces and photos after the fact.

The labelling problem is that outcomes are confounded and experts disagree with themselves. A recovery at 4 a.m. does not prove 4 a.m. was the best window; the car might have been there all day. A walk-away has no outcome at all unless the car is later recovered somewhere else. Two veteran agents shown the same file will choose different addresses, and the same agent chooses differently after a bad week. Labels need to be built from realised recoveries and failed attempts across many files, with the selection of which files were worked accounted for, and with repeated expert ratings on a sample to estimate how consistent the experts are.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. The agent plans the night in a few minutes and decides at the curb in seconds. A ranked list that arrives after the route is set, or a risk flag that interrupts while the agent is reading a scene, will be ignored. It has to sit inside the field app the agent already uses, explain itself in the agent's terms ("seen at the employer lot three Thursdays running"), and earn trust one closed file at a time. It must never tell an agent to proceed into a situation they judged unsafe.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An attempt ledger first and a model second. Every attempt is captured from the field app with near-zero effort: the address, time, GPS trace, a photo, a one-tap reason for leaving (not present, objection, gated, unsafe, hold received), and the outcome when the file closes. Over a few thousand files the ledger becomes the agency's memory.

On top of it, a model ranks each open file's addresses by hour of the week — the probability the car is there, and the expected time to recovery — using the assignment's addresses and their type, the borrower's employment pattern, hit and scan timing, neighbourhood and parking features, season, and the agency's own attempt history. It learns the veteran's reads from their attempts and their outcomes and offers them, with reasons, to the newer agent. A second model scores attempt risk from the same signals plus prior objections at the address and the file's dispute history, so that the plan routes risky files to experienced agents or daylight hours rather than overriding anyone at the curb.

## Impact If Solved
Raising recoveries per attempt-hour by even a fifth on flat-fee work is the difference between a profitable truck and a losing one, and fewer night attempts at hostile addresses means fewer confrontations and wrongful-repossession claims. An agency that keeps its best agent's judgement in a ledger and a model can train a new hire in months rather than years and survive the owner's retirement.
