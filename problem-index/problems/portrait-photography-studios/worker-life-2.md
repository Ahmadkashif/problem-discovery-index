# The Wedding Photographer's Editing Debt

**Industry:** [[portrait-photography-studios|Portrait Photography Studios]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A wedding photographer works a long Saturday, then owes 24 to 40 hours of culling and editing for it while the next wedding is already booked, and the debt compounds all season.
**Tags:** #cnns #transfer-learning #semantic-segmentation #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
A wedding day for the photographer starts with the getting-ready shots in the morning and ends after the last dance: often ten to twelve hours on their feet (estimate) with two bodies and a bag of lenses, 1,500 to 5,000 raw frames on the cards. The second shift starts Monday. Culling the frames to the few hundred worth delivering takes hours; editing them for exposure, white balance and the photographer's signature look takes longer; retouching the portraits takes longer still. Photographers' own rules of thumb put the total at three to five hours of culling and editing per hour of coverage, which for an eight-hour wedding is 24 to 40 hours. Couples are typically promised delivery in six to eight weeks.

In peak season a busy photographer shoots a wedding most weekends. If each one generates a week of editing, the arithmetic does not work, and the backlog grows from May to October. Many photographers outsource editing or buy AI culling and editing tools, Aftershoot at $10–45 a month or Imagen at five cents a photo, but they still review every image because the tools miss the photographer's taste often enough to matter.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The editing debt takes the photographer's evenings and weekdays all season, on top of weekends spent at other people's celebrations. Physical strain from carrying gear on wedding days meets hours at a desk the rest of the week. The stress is personal: the couple emails at week seven asking where their photos are, and the photographer's reputation, which is their whole marketing, rides on the answer. Many leave the work not because they dislike photographing weddings but because they cannot sustain the editing that comes after.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A culling and editing pipeline trained on the photographer's own past decisions: which frames they kept, how they edited them, which faces they retouched and how far. It returns a delivered-ready gallery with a short exception list, frames it was unsure about and edits that departed from the photographer's usual look, so the photographer reviews fifty decisions instead of five hundred. Skin and blemish retouching runs on the portraits the photographer marks, at the strength they choose.

## Impact If Solved
Cutting a wedding's post-production from a working week to an afternoon of exception review would give a full-time wedding photographer hundreds of hours back each season (estimate), deliver galleries in days rather than weeks, and make it possible to take on the weddings they now turn down.
