# The Police Claim Read Before the Verdict

**Industry:** [[self-insured-benefit-pools|Self-Insured Benefit & Risk Pools]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A public entity pool's senior claims manager can tell within weeks which use-of-force or civil rights complaint will end in a seven-figure loss, but records that judgement only as a reserve number, so it is never graded and leaves when they retire.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #large-language-models #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #compliance #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A city's police department is a member of a state municipal pool. A complaint arrives: an arrest at a traffic stop, a broken wrist, a lawsuit under 42 U.S.C. §1983 naming the officers, the chief and the city. The pool's liability claims manager opens a file, sets an initial reserve and assigns panel defence counsel. Over the next few months, that person decides whether to look for an early settlement or harden the defence for summary judgement on qualified immunity. They report the claim to the reinsurer if it may pierce the retention, and feed the reserve into the actuary's next loss projection. The reserve drives the member's experience modifier and next year's contribution.

The decisions that matter most rest on a read the claims manager cannot fully specify. They know which body-camera footage plays badly with a jury, and which plaintiff firms take cases to verdict and which settle. They know which venues produce runaway awards and which officers have a pattern. They know a claim that looks routine at intake will turn when a second complainant appears. In Colorado the stakes moved fast. An $8.75M police settlement by a CIRSA member was a state record until a $9.5M sheriff's settlement broke it. Then came a $14M Denver jury verdict over the 2020 protests and a $19M settlement in 2023. A new state law, S.B. 20-217, had widened police liability. CIRSA's long-time reinsurers left, and members with police departments paid 150% more for law enforcement liability in 2024. A pool's retention, the layer it pays itself, may be $1M to $2M per claim. A misread claim inside that layer comes straight out of the members' surplus.

The pool records every claim's incurred value over time and its final paid amount. It does not record the reasoning behind the first reserve or the settle-or-defend choice, or what the claims manager expected the claim to become. When that manager retires (many pool claims departments are a handful of people), the pool keeps its loss runs and loses the judgement that produced them.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's read has to be captured when it is formed, early, by the expert, on a file that may hold a complaint, a police report, a use-of-force form and a link to video. The read today lives in a reserve figure, a few lines of claim notes and conversations with defence counsel. Asking an adjuster carrying a full caseload to write a structured rationale for every new claim is a tax with no visible return. Defence counsel's case evaluations sit in letters and are often privileged.

The labelling problem is that the outcome is slow, rare and noisy. A seven-figure police claim takes three to six years to close (estimate), and a single pool may see only a handful a decade. The same facts settle for $150,000 in one county and go to an eight-figure verdict in another, so the final paid amount is a noisy label for the quality of the early read. Experts disagree with themselves: an adjuster who has just watched a runaway verdict reserves the next similar claim higher. Reserve philosophies differ between pools, so incurred values are not comparable across them without care.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. The read must arrive inside the first reserve-setting window and before the early-settlement opportunity closes, faster than the manager's own recall, and in a form defence counsel and the board's claims committee will accept. A score that cannot say why will be ignored. A score that leaks into discoverable claim notes creates legal exposure, so privilege boundaries shape the design as much as accuracy does.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A claim-read ledger first and a model second. At notice and at each reserve change, the system drafts a short structured record from the complaint, police report and notes. It covers allegations, injury severity, video present or not, officer history, plaintiff counsel, venue, the claims manager's expected outcome band and their settle-or-defend lean. The manager confirms it in seconds. Each record is graded later against the claim's closed value and time to close.

On top of the ledger, learn the expert's recurring reads. Pool the pool's own closed liability claims (and, where pools agree, a consortium of pools' de-identified claims) with public verdict and settlement data. Train severity models that output a calibrated probability the claim exceeds the retention and an expected time to resolution. Show the cases most like this one and how they closed. The goal is not to replace the claims manager's call but to make a 25-year veteran's pattern recognition, with its track record, available to the adjuster who replaces them.

## Impact If Solved
For a pool whose law enforcement liability charges rose 150% in a year, identifying the claims headed past the retention even a few months earlier changes settlement timing, reinsurer notice and reserve adequacy, which flow directly into members' contributions. Keeping a retiring claims manager's documented judgement turns the pool's most fragile asset into something it owns, and gives the actuary, the reinsurer and the board an evidence base for police liability pricing that today rests on a few people's memory.
