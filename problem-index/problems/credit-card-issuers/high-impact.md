# The Bust-Out Booked as a Credit Loss

**Industry:** [[credit-card-issuers|Credit Card Issuers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Issuers book much of their first-party fraud as ordinary credit loss, so their credit models learn from fraud labelled as default, their fraud models never see it, and the investigators who can spot a bust-out forming carry the pattern only in their heads.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #lstms-and-grus #transformers #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A bust-out runs on a familiar script. An account — opened with a real identity, a synthetic one, or a borrowed one — is used lightly and paid on time for months. The issuer responds as designed: the line goes up. Then, in a few days, the line is drawn to the limit across merchants that sell resaleable goods, cash-like transactions and balance transfers; one or more payments arrive that free up the line before they bounce; the line is drawn again; and the cardholder disappears. The account rolls through the delinquency buckets and charges off about six months later as a credit loss.

Nobody records it as fraud, because nobody decided it was fraud. The fraud team works third-party fraud — stolen cards, account takeover — where a victim calls in. A bust-out has no victim to call; it reaches collections first, and collections codes it as a non-payer who could not be reached. A patent on detecting these schemes says it plainly: bust-outs are often not identified as fraud by issuers because the losses are handled by the collections department. Some institutions book synthetic-identity losses as credit losses and others as fraud, and there is no standard for either. TransUnion put lender exposure to suspected synthetic identities across cards, retail cards, auto and personal loans at $3.3B at end-2024.

Inside the issuer a handful of experienced investigators can see it coming. They look at an account and notice what they cannot quite specify: the payment arrived from a new external account the day after the line increase; the merchant mix turned to electronics and gift cards; the address changed and then changed back; the cardholder called to ask about the available credit rather than the balance; three new accounts opened at other issuers in the same fortnight. "It just looks like a bust-out" is the honest description of their judgement. They review a fraction of accounts, and their conclusions rarely make it back into the label the models train on.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's read happens rarely and is recorded thinly. Investigators review accounts referred by rules, not a sample of the book, so their judgement is captured only on cases someone already suspected, and the case note says "closed — confirmed FPF" without the features that drove the call. Capturing the expert means recording which signals they looked at, in which order, on accounts they cleared as well as those they flagged, during work that is already queue-driven.

The labelling problem is severe. A charged-off account with no contact may be a bust-out, a cardholder who lost a job, or someone who died; the difference is often unknowable after the fact. Investigators disagree with each other and with themselves — the same reviewer is more suspicious the week after a fraud ring was found — and the organisation's incentives push labels around: a fraud loss and a credit loss sit in different budgets, carry different reserve treatment and land on different executives' scorecards. Any label set must estimate intra-rater agreement and accept that a share of outcomes is ambiguous by nature.

The deployment problem is speed. The value is in acting before the bust — holding the payment, freezing the line increase, slowing authorisations — and the bust happens in days. A model that scores accounts at month-end is useless; the read must run on the payment and authorisation event stream and be faster than an investigator's queue, and every action it triggers is an adverse action that needs a reason under Reg B and must not catch the cardholder who simply had a bad month.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A label first and a model second. Every charge-off, closed fraud case and early-stage delinquency gets a structured cause — credit loss, first-party fraud, synthetic identity, third-party fraud, ambiguous — assigned by investigators through a review surface that records the evidence they relied on, including on accounts they cleared. Historical charge-offs are re-reviewed on a sample to estimate how much first-party fraud has been sitting in the credit loss line.

On top of that label set, learn the investigator's read. Sequence models over each account's event history — payments and their source accounts, returns, line changes, merchant categories, authorisation velocity, contact-centre calls, address and device changes, bureau inquiries — trained to predict the investigator's label and the eventual outcome, scoring on every payment and authorisation in near real time. Payments that would free up a line on an at-risk account are held until they clear; line increases are suspended; the investigator sees the case with the signals that drove it. Credit models are then retrained on the corrected labels.

## Impact If Solved
An issuer that separates first-party fraud from credit loss reprices both: the credit models stop penalising thin-file applicants for losses that were actually fraud rings, and the fraud team gains a category it currently cannot see. Stopping a bust-out before the second draw on the line saves most of the loss — often the full limit plus the bounced payment — and capturing what veteran investigators recognise keeps that judgement when they move on, which in fraud operations happens every few years.
