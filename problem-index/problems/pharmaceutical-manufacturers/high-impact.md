# The Root Cause That Comes Back

**Industry:** [[pharmaceutical-manufacturers|Pharmaceutical Manufacturers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A drug plant closes thousands of deviations a year, each against a stated root cause, and nobody checks which root causes held, so the senior investigator's read of what really happened is neither measured nor kept when that investigator leaves.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #large-language-models #transformers #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #compliance #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
A deviation opens whenever production departs from the written procedure: a tablet weight drifts out of range, a cleaning log is unsigned, a granulator alarms, an environmental monitoring plate grows colonies, an assay comes in out of specification. A mid-size generic plant opens on the order of 1,000 to 3,000 a year (estimate). Each is assigned to a QA specialist or a production investigator, who has an internal target of ~30 days (estimate) to establish what happened, decide whether any batch is affected, name a root cause and open a CAPA. The investigation is written as a narrative in the QMS. The batch waits for it.

The experienced investigator does something the procedure does not describe. Reading a short deviation description, they recognise that this is the same seal failure the press had last spring under a different deviation number, that "operator did not follow SOP" keeps appearing on this step because the SOP describes a sequence the equipment does not allow, that a weight excursion right after a changeover means a cleaning residue rather than a dosing fault, or that a lab result is suspect because one analyst's integration habits have been questioned before. They "just know" which stories hold together and which will be back. A junior investigator, under the same 30-day clock, reaches for the root cause the template offers first, and the template's first offer is human error followed by retraining (estimated at 25–40% of stated root causes at many plants).

FDA reads the result. In FY2025, failure to thoroughly review unexplained discrepancies (21 CFR 211.192) was the second most-cited drug observation, with 164 citations plus 32 for incomplete investigation records, behind quality-unit procedures not followed (243). When an investigator finds the same deviation recurring under a closed CAPA, the 483 says so, and repeat observations are what turn a 483 into a warning letter. The outcome of every root-cause call is eventually observable: either the failure mode comes back on that equipment, product or step, or it does not. That outcome is never joined to the call.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's reasoning happens at the moment of reading and is written down only as a conclusion. The narrative in the QMS records what the investigator decided, rarely which past deviations they recalled, which hypotheses they rejected and why. Capturing that means recording the investigator while they investigate (the searches they run, the batch records they pull, the people they interview) without adding work to a role already measured on closure rate. Historical deviations are free text in inconsistent templates, with equipment named three different ways and root-cause categories that changed when the QMS was replaced.

The labelling problem is that the honest label arrives late and is noisy. A root cause is "true" if the corrective action stopped the failure, but recurrence depends on production volume, on whether the same equipment ran the product again, and on whether the next occurrence was even recognised as the same failure. Experts disagree with themselves: the same senior investigator will call a weight excursion a dosing fault in March and a cleaning residue in September, depending on what they saw last. Inter-rater checks on a sample of re-read deviations are the only way to know the ceiling. And the most informative cases, the investigations FDA later criticised, are few.

The deployment problem has two parts. Speed: help has to appear while the investigator is reading the deviation, faster than their own recall and the QMS keyword search, or it will be ignored. Regulation: anything that drafts text into a GMP record falls under computer-system validation and data-integrity rules, and every suggestion becomes part of an inspectable record. A tool that says "this looks like recurrence of DEV-2291" creates a record FDA can read, which is exactly what a weak quality culture fears and a strong one wants.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A recurrence ledger first, and a model second. Every closed deviation is linked, by equipment, product, process step, material lot and failure mode, to every later deviation that might be the same failure, so each root cause carries a running record of whether it held. Built from the plant's own QMS history, the ledger alone shows which root-cause categories, investigators and CAPA types actually stop failures.

On top of the ledger, learn the senior investigator's read. Pair historical deviation descriptions with the investigators' final root causes, the deviations they cited as related, and the recurrence outcome, and train models that, for a newly opened deviation, retrieve the likely related history, rank candidate root causes by how often they held in similar cases, and flag a human-error conclusion where the same step has produced repeated human-error findings. The investigator stays the author and signs the conclusion. The system shows its evidence (the past deviations and batch records behind each suggestion) so the record is defensible under 211.192. FDA's public warning letters and 483 observations, a corpus of what the agency calls an inadequate investigation, supply a second check on the draft before it closes.

## Impact If Solved
A plant that knows which of its root causes hold can stop re-investigating the same failure under new numbers, a recurring cost in investigator hours, held batches and repeat 483 observations. At a site closing 2,000 deviations a year, halving repeat deviations would remove several hundred investigations and the batch holds attached to them (estimate). It would also move the plant's strongest defence at inspection from "we retrained the operator" to an evidenced record of which corrective actions worked. Keeping the senior investigator's pattern recognition through retirement protects the asset FDA most often finds missing: a quality unit that can explain why things go wrong.
