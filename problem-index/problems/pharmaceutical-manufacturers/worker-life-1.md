# Writing Every Step in a Gown

**Industry:** [[pharmaceutical-manufacturers|Pharmaceutical Manufacturers]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A manufacturing operator spends a large share of each shift writing down, in a gown and gloves, what they just did, and is blamed as "human error" when the paperwork, not the product, goes wrong.
**Tags:** #worker-facing #large-language-models #cnns #transfer-learning #automation #quick-win #compliance

## The Problem
On a compression or granulation suite, an operator weighs and dispenses materials, sets up and runs the equipment, takes in-process samples, and records each step in the batch record as it happens: start and end times, weights, equipment IDs, room conditions, line clearance checks, and a second person's verification signature for each critical step. In a hybrid plant much of it is pen on paper inside the cleanroom, with gloved hands, and every error must be corrected by good documentation practice: a single line through the mistake, the correction, initials, date and a reason. A missed entry found by QA days later comes back as a documentation deviation with the operator's name on it.

The work is fragmented by the record. An operator stops the line to find the second person for a verification signature, records a weight that the scale already printed, or copies a lot number from a label into a box. On a busy shift the documentation competes with the equipment for attention, and the errors that follow are the most common deviations a plant opens.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Operators are among the lower-paid people in the plant (packaging and filling operators averaged $43,160 and chemical equipment operators $50,760 in 2023, BLS), yet their names are on the deviations that FDA reads. "Operator error, retrained" goes in the operator's training file. Repeated documentation deviations limit who gets trusted with sterile or high-potency suites. The procedure often describes a sequence the equipment does not allow, so experienced operators work around it, and are the ones caught when an investigator compares the record to the SOP.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Capture at the point of action instead of transcription after it: scales, balances and equipment that write directly to the record; label and lot scanning; a tablet-based record that will not accept an out-of-sequence step and tells the operator why; voice or photo capture for steps done with gloved hands; and a second-person verification that can be done remotely by camera where the procedure allows. For legacy paper records, handwriting recognition flags missing or illegible entries before the record leaves the suite rather than days later at QA.

## Impact If Solved
If each operator spent an hour less per shift on transcription and corrections (estimate), and documentation deviations fell by half, operators would get back attention for the equipment and their names would stop appearing on deviations that the record design caused.
