# The Deficit Decision Made From Memory

**Industry:** [[commodity-brokers|Commodity Brokers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every morning an FCM's risk desk decides which under-margined accounts to chase, extend or liquidate on the strength of what its brokers know about each client, and neither the reasoning nor its track record is ever recorded, so the firm cannot tell good credit judgement from luck.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact #compliance

## The Problem
Futures are marked to market every day. When prices move against a customer, the FCM pays the clearing house that customer's variation margin at the morning settlement, out of the FCM's own resources if necessary, and then has to collect it. On a quiet day the list of accounts below maintenance margin is short. On a day like the spring 2022 grain rally, when CME raised margins at least six times in six weeks, a mid-size FCM can face several hundred accounts in call at once (estimate). The risk manager works the list with the brokers who own the relationships. For each account they decide: issue the call and wait, extend credit until tomorrow, ask for a partial wire, reduce the position, or liquidate now.

Those calls are made on knowledge that is mostly unwritten. The broker knows this feedlot always wires on the second day because its lender approves draws on Tuesdays. That farmer's short corn hedge is matched by grain in the bin, so the call is a financing problem rather than a loss. This speculator has never been in deficit before and has started trading larger contracts after a run of wins. That client "sounded wrong" on the phone this morning. Experienced risk managers describe it as a feel for which accounts will pay, developed over years of watching who did and who didn't. The written record is the margin call notice, a timestamp, and sometimes a line in a CRM.

The outcomes are recorded perfectly. Each call is met, met late, partly met or not met. Each extension ends in payment or in a deficit the FCM must cover, and each liquidation crystallises a loss at a known price. When the judgement fails, the cost is the firm's own capital. Interactive Brokers absorbed about $88M on April 20, 2020, when the May WTI contract settled at -$37.63, and the CFTC later ordered $82.57M of restitution and a $1.75M penalty for supervision failures. When the judgement is too harsh, the cost lands on the client. In 1988 brokers liquidated farmers' short corn hedges near the top after banks pulled hedge lines, leaving them unhedged at the worst moment, though none of the lines had used even 30% of its limit.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's reasoning has to be captured when the decision is made. That happens between the clearing house's settlement and the market open, by phone, with the risk manager and the broker talking over each other. Nobody types a rationale while a list of two hundred accounts is open. What gets written afterwards is a defence, not a reason. Much of the relevant knowledge also sits with introducing brokers and associated persons outside the FCM, and their books are portable: when a broker moves firms, the client knowledge moves too.

The labelling problem is that outcomes are confounded by the decisions themselves. A liquidated account never shows whether it would have paid. An extended account that paid may have paid because the broker phoned the client's banker. So the honest label needs selection-aware methods, not a naive "paid / didn't pay". Experts also disagree with themselves. The same risk manager is more lenient after a calm month and harsher the week after a loss. Two managers looking at the same account often differ, and nobody has measured by how much.

The deployment problem is speed and accountability. Help is useful only if it arrives before the open, ranks the list faster than the risk manager can scan it, and explains itself in the terms the manager already uses. And because NFA Rule 2-9 supervision and DSRO examinations look at how credit decisions are made, an FCM will not let a model extend credit on its own. The model has to support a documented human decision.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A decision ledger first and a model second. Every morning the margin list is pre-assembled with each account's position, its hedge status (with matched cash bushels or physical exposure where the client has declared them), its payment history, the time of its lender's funding window, and the broker's most recent note. When the risk manager acts, the system records the action and a one-line reason chosen from the desk's own vocabulary: "hedge, lender draw pending", "spec, first deficit, size increased". That takes a click, not a paragraph.

On top of the ledger, learn the desk's read. Models predict, for each account in call, the probability and timing of payment under each possible action, and are trained with methods that correct for the fact that liquidated accounts hide their counterfactual. They surface the accounts where the model and the desk disagree most, since those are where either the model is missing context or the desk is running on habit. Each decision is graded weeks later against what happened, by manager and by reason code. That turns a feel into a track record a new risk manager can learn from and an examiner can follow.

## Impact If Solved
Customer deficits come straight out of FCM capital, which also has to cover 8% of customer risk maintenance margin. At a mid-size FCM, deficit and bad-debt losses run to the low single-digit millions of dollars in a normal year and tens of millions in a shock (estimate; Interactive Brokers' 2020 loss was about $88M). Catching the accounts that will not pay a day earlier could cut those losses by ~20–30% (estimate), roughly $0.5–1M in a normal year and several million in a shock, without liquidating hedgers whose calls are only a financing gap. The stakes are existential for smaller firms. Several small FCMs on the CFTC's August 2026 list carry roughly $10–65M of excess net capital, so a single tens-of-millions shock deficit could consume most of it. A graded, recorded credit process lets a mid-size FCM run more margin financing per dollar of capital, keeps farmer and feedlot hedges on through price spikes, and keeps its most valuable judgement when a senior risk manager retires.
