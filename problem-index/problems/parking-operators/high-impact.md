# The Rate Board Set by Feel

**Industry:** [[parking-operators|Parking Operators]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** The area manager decides every transient, event and early-bird rate from a read of the street that is never written down, so the operator cannot tell the owner which rate decisions earned money and loses the read when the manager leaves.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #time-series-forecasting #bayesian-inference #causal-inference #feature-engineering #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
An area manager at a national or regional operator runs ~8–20 facilities (estimate): office garages, hotel and hospital decks, surface lots near a stadium, a mixed-use podium under apartments. Each has a rate board with transient hourly steps, a daily maximum, an early-bird rate, an evening flat rate, event rates and a monthly contract price. The manager sets them. On a Tuesday morning they drive past the office garage at 8:30 and see the "Full" sign come on twenty minutes earlier than last month. They notice the surface lot across the street has dropped its event rate to $25, and that the convention centre has a trade show next week. They raise the early-bird cap, post a $40 event rate for Thursday's game, tell the attendant to stack monthly parkers on the roof, and push the event rate to SpotHero.

This is tacit knowledge in the same sense as a craftsman's. The experienced manager "just knows" how fast a garage will fill on a rainy Monday, how far an event rate can rise before drivers walk three blocks to a cheaper lot, and how many monthly cards a facility can oversell because a share of holders never come in on Fridays. They learned it over years of watching the same blocks. A new manager inherits the boards but not the reasons, and spends a year getting them wrong.

The outcome of every one of these decisions is recorded in detail. PARCS logs each entry and exit, the app and marketplace channels log each booking, and the owner's statement settles the revenue to the cent. What is not recorded is the decision: which rate changed, when, why, and what the manager expected to happen. Owners now measure the result. Mobile Infrastructure Corporation reports revenue per available stall and converted its leases to management contracts in 2024 to align the operator's incentives with revenue growth. It asks operators to "actively manage parking rates based on local insights." When the owner asks why RevPAS fell, say, 6% at a deck, the operator can only list what happened: more remote work, a lost event, a competitor's new garage. It cannot say whether its own rate decisions helped or hurt.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection problem is that the manager's read happens in a car or on a sidewalk, not at a desk. A rate change is entered into PARCS or a marketplace portal as a new number, with no note of the event, the competitor price or the fill pattern that prompted it. Capturing the expert would mean recording the change together with its trigger and the manager's expectation ("should fill by 6, $40 holds"), at the moment they make it, on a phone, during the busiest part of their week. Competitor rates are seen on a board across the street or read off SpotHero and rarely logged.

The labelling problem is that revenue is a noisy label for a rate decision. A good rate loses money when an event is cancelled; a bad one makes money because a rival garage closed for repairs. The honest label is the counterfactual: what this facility would have earned at the old rate. Owners rarely allow experiments, so it has to be estimated. Managers also disagree with themselves. The same manager prices the same Thursday game at $35 in April and $45 in September, depending on how the last game went. And because managers raise rates only when they expect demand, the price history is confounded by the very judgement being learned.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. A recommendation has to arrive before the manager's own read, on a phone, for twenty facilities at once, and has to be right often enough that they stop driving past to check. It must also respect each management contract: some owners approve every rate change, some cap the daily maximum, some tie rates to a tenant lease. A model that ignores those terms will be overruled once and then ignored.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A rate ledger first, then a model. Every rate change across every channel is captured with a one-tap reason (event, competitor move, fill pattern, owner request, monthly oversell) and the manager's expected fill. Most of these are drafted automatically from the event calendar, competitor prices scraped from marketplaces, and the facility's own entry curve. Each change is then graded against what happened and against a counterfactual estimated from comparable days and nearby facilities.

On top of the ledger, the operator learns each experienced manager's recurring reads: how a given facility fills before a home game, how sensitive its drivers are to a $5 move, and how many monthly holders show up on Fridays. Those patterns, with their track record, become hourly occupancy forecasts and rate recommendations for the next manager, who accepts, edits or rejects them with a reason. The public half can be built and tested in two weeks on Seattle's paid-occupancy feed and SFMTA's quarterly rate changes (see the ML opportunities). The private half needs one operator's PARCS history and a few managers willing to log their reasons.

## Impact If Solved
A 3–5% lift in RevPAS from better event and transient pricing (estimate) is worth ~$1–1.5B a year across ~$25–35B of gross parking revenue at operator-run facilities (estimate), most of it paid to owners under management contracts. Keeping a departing manager's read in a graded ledger cuts the year a replacement spends relearning the boards. It also gives the operator its best renewal argument: evidence, facility by facility, of which rate decisions earned the owner money.
