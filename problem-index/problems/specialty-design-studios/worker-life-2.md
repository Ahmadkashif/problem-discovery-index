# The Exhibit Designer on the Show Floor at Midnight

**Industry:** [[specialty-design-studios|Specialty Design Studios]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** When a booth arrives in crates and the build does not match the drawings, the exhibit designer or project manager fixes it on the show floor overnight against a fixed opening, and gets back their sleep and their reputation if the mismatch is caught weeks earlier.
**Tags:** #object-detection #cnns #large-language-models #feature-engineering #worker-facing #compliance #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
Exhibit design ends in a convention hall. The designer or project manager flies in for move-in, meets the union installation crew at the booth space, and watches the exhibit come out of crates. Then the problems surface: a graphic panel printed to the old dimensions, a counter built to a superseded revision, missing hardware, the wrong carpet colourway, a component that was pulled from the warehouse for another show. (Show-rule failures such as header clearance and flame certificates are the separate problem in low-impact-2; these are kitting and version mismatches.) Labour is billed by the hour with overtime after set times, the floor closes at a fixed time, and the show opens at 9 a.m. regardless.

So the fixes happen overnight. The designer re-sketches a detail on a phone, negotiates with the crew lead about what can be cut on site, orders a rush graphic from a local printer, and calls the exhibit house's warehouse three time zones away. A single show season can mean a dozen of these nights, back to back in different cities.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The client sees the booth when it opens, not the night it took. Errors made weeks earlier by someone else — a pull sheet, a revised graphic file, a component list nobody reconciled — land on the person standing in the hall, and are judged as theirs. The hours are long and irregular, the travel relentless in the spring and autumn peaks, and the stress specific: every mismatch found on site is found at the most expensive moment possible, with the crew's clock running.

The deeper cost is that experienced designers know where these failures come from — they have seen the same twenty mistakes for years — but the checking happens in their heads during a walkthrough, and only if they have time.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A pre-show check that compares the as-designed booth with what is actually being shipped: the pull sheet and packing list against the drawing's component list, the graphic files and component revisions against the current model, and photographs of crates and components taken at the warehouse against the expected parts. On site, a phone checklist built from the drawings walks the crew lead and the designer through install in sequence, with photographs logged against each step so missing parts are spotted before the crew reaches them.

## Impact If Solved
Moving the discovery of a mismatch from the show floor to the warehouse turns an overnight rework into a morning email, cuts overtime labour on the client's invoice, and returns sleep to the people who travel the show circuit — plausibly several overnight sessions per designer per season (estimate).
