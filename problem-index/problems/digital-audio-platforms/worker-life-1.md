# The Curator Under Pitch Volume

**Industry:** [[digital-audio-platforms|Digital Audio Platforms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A small editorial team decides what tens of millions of people hear, receives more pitches in a week than it can listen to in a year, and knows exactly what a placement is worth to the artist who does not get one.
**Tags:** #contrastive-learning #autoencoders #transformers #k-nearest-neighbors #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #tacit-knowledge-ml

## The Problem
Editorial curation at a major platform means programming playlists that carry an enormous share of listening. The team is small relative to the catalogue and to the pitch volume: labels, distributors and artists submit far more material than any group of people can hear, and the submission tools mean volume has only increased.

The work is triage under impossible arithmetic. Listen to as much as possible, rely on trusted relationships and sources to filter, and accept that most of what arrives is never heard. That last part is the uncomfortable centre of the job, because a curator knows that a placement can change an artist's year and that the decision not to listen is functionally a decision, made by capacity rather than by judgement.

The pressure is constant and asymmetric. Labels with resources pitch persistently and have relationships; independent artists submit into a form. Curators are aware of this and it is not something an individual can fix from inside the queue.

And the feedback is thin. A curator who adds a track sees streams, which reflect the playlist's own traffic as much as the track's merit, and rarely learns whether the placement built the artist anything durable.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is a job with real cultural power and no adequate instrument, held by people who mostly took it because they care about music and who are confronted daily with the gap between what they can hear and what deserves hearing. The moral weight of that is not incidental — curators describe it as the defining difficulty of the role.

The relationship pressure is its own burden: being the person labels lobby, managing those relationships while trying to keep the programming honest, and knowing that a decision will be read as favour or slight either way.

And the expertise is invisible and unpreserved. A curator's sense of what fits a playlist's identity, what an audience will accept, and when a track is a week early is genuinely skilled and lives entirely in the person. When they move on, the playlist's character drifts and nobody can say precisely what was lost.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Widen the funnel rather than rank the pitches. The highest-value tool is one that surfaces, from the entire catalogue rather than from the pitch queue, recordings that fit a specific playlist's identity — using audio and context representations to find the things nobody pitched. That directly addresses the resource asymmetry, because it does not require an artist to know anyone.

Represent the playlist, not the genre. A playlist's identity is a coherent thing that a curator can hear and no taxonomy captures, and learning it from the playlist's own history — including what was tried and removed — gives a curator a search tool that speaks their language rather than a genre filter.

Make the feedback meaningful. Introduction-to-retention rather than streams tells a curator whether a placement built an audience for the artist, which is the outcome they actually care about and have never been able to see.

Handle the pitch queue mechanically. Deduplicating, grouping by similarity, filtering what is clearly outside a playlist's scope, and drafting responses returns a large share of the volume without affecting the judgement.

And preserve the character. A representation of what a playlist has been, derived from its own history, is the closest thing to institutional memory for a role where all the knowledge is currently in one person's ears.

## Impact If Solved
Editorial placement is one of the few remaining mechanisms by which an unknown recording reaches an audience, and it is rationed by human listening capacity in a way that structurally favours artists with representation. Catalogue-wide surfacing against a learned playlist identity changes who gets considered at all, and retention-based feedback tells curators whether their decisions built anything — which is the question the role exists to answer and currently cannot.
