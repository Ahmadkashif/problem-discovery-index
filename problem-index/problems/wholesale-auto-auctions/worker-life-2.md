# The Lot Driver's Twelve Miles on Sale Day

**Industry:** [[wholesale-auto-auctions|Wholesale Auto Auctions]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A lot driver at a physical auction walks back and forth across a lot of thousands of cars, in heat and rain, to find units whose recorded spot is wrong and whose keys are somewhere else, and gets back their feet, their afternoons and some safety.
**Tags:** #k-nearest-neighbors #time-series-forecasting #optimization-fundamentals #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
On a sale day at a large physical auction, lot drivers (also called drivers or porters) move every running car from its parking spot to the lane, through the block, and back to a sold or no-sale row. Between sales they move cars to the photo booth, the inspection line, the reconditioning shop and the transport loading area. A big auction may run a dozen lanes with hundreds of cars an hour. Drivers are often part-time: ACV's careers page on 2026-10-28 listed part-time lot drivers in Fort Wayne, Indiana, and van drivers in Oklahoma City.

Most of a driver's day is walking. They drive a car to its row, then walk back for the next. When the system's recorded location is wrong because someone parked in the nearest open spot, or the key is in a different box, they walk the rows clicking a fob and listening for a chirp. An estimated 10–15 miles on foot on a sale day is common at large lots (estimate). That happens in summer heat on asphalt, in rain and ice, among moving vehicles driven by people in a hurry.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
It is physically punishing work at the bottom of the pay scale, and it is dangerous: drivers move between cars that others are reversing, and the pressure to keep the lane full means running. The cost of a lost car lands on the driver: a lane gap, a supervisor on the radio, a buyer waiting at the gate. Pay rarely reflects the miles. Turnover is high, and new drivers walk the most because they do not know the lot.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Every move recorded with a location where the car is actually left, captured from the driver's phone or a cheap tag rather than typed. A dispatch list that sequences each driver's next moves so they walk the shortest distance between drop and pickup. Shuttles timed to where drivers will be, not on a loop. A key-location record that follows the key, not the car. And a forecast of which units will be needed when — sold units going to transport, no-sales re-staged for a rerun, inspections due — so cars are pre-staged near the lane before the rush.

## Impact If Solved
Cutting wasted walking by a third would take 3–5 miles off each driver's 10–15-mile sale day (estimate). That means fewer heat and strain injuries and shorter shifts for part-timers paid by the hour. For the auction, lanes that do not wait on a missing car keep conversion up on its busiest hours.
