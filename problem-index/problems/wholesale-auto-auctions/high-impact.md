# The Defect the Inspector Hears and Can't Write Down

**Industry:** [[wholesale-auto-auctions|Wholesale Auto Auctions]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced inspector senses frame repair, a failing transmission or flood exposure in a ~90-second walkaround (estimate) and a lot drive, and the auction keeps neither the suspicion nor the evidence for it, only the line that made it onto the report and the arbitration that followed.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #transfer-learning #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A vehicle condition inspector at a physical auction or in a dealer's back lot inspects fifteen to thirty cars a day (estimate). For each one the routine is set by the auction's condition-report app. Photograph every panel, meter the paint, read and clear trouble codes, check tyres and glass, look underneath, start it cold, drive it a few hundred feet, and assign a grade. ACV's 10-K describes inspectors metering "all paint surfaces" to find prior repairs and reading and clearing diagnostic codes. That part of the job is a checklist.

The part buyers pay for is not on the checklist. An inspector with five years in the lanes hears a lifter tick that fades as the oil warms. They feel a transmission flare between second and third on a drive too short to set a code. They notice the carpet smells faintly of mildew under a fresh shampoo, or the bolt heads on a fender have been turned, or the overspray on a door seal is the wrong colour. They know which model years of which engines go wrong in which way. Then they decide whether each suspicion becomes a report line, a seller announcement, a "check engine" note, or nothing. Experienced inspectors describe this as a feel for the car that a new hire does not have and cannot be told.

The outcome of that decision is recorded with unusual precision. Under the NAAA guidelines a buyer who finds an undisclosed defect costing $800 or more to repair can arbitrate, inside windows as short as Manheim's 10 calendar days for digital sales. The case records what was wrong and what it cost, and whether the sale was adjusted or unwound. Post-sale inspections, guarantee returns under DealShield or ACV's Go Green, and the price the car finally settles at add more labels. The suspicion is never joined to them. If the inspector heard the tick and wrote nothing, there is no record that they heard it. If they wrote "engine noise" and the car sold $900 under MMR without a claim, there is no record that the note cost the seller money it did not need to lose.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the inspector's judgement happens in their ears, hands and nose, in the lane, at production speed. The condition-report app records the conclusion, not the evidence: there is no audio of the cold start, no accelerometer trace of the drive, no close-up of the weatherstrip that caught their eye. Capturing it means instrumenting the inspection itself, with a phone microphone on the engine, the phone's motion sensors on the drive and prompted photographs. That has to happen without slowing an inspector who is paid by the car. ACV's audio engine recordings and OPENLANE's engine-audio analysis show the capture is possible. Neither publishes how the recording relates to what the inspector concluded.

The labelling problem is that arbitration is a biased and noisy label. Most defects never become claims, because the buyer fixes a $600 problem rather than file, because the car was wholesaled again, or because the defect sits under the threshold. That threshold now moves with price, $800 below $50,000 and 2% above. Claims also depend on the buyer as much as the car: some dealers arbitrate everything and some nothing. And inspectors disagree with themselves. The same inspector grades a borderline structural repair differently at 8 a.m. and 4 p.m., and differently in the week after a costly arbitration. Labels need repeated blind re-inspection of a sample to know how much agreement is even possible.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. A model that flags "possible frame repair" must do so before the car runs, inside the inspector's workflow, and faster than the inspector can decide alone. If it flags too often, inspectors ignore it. If it flags what they already saw, it adds nothing. It also has to survive a commercial tension: every extra disclosure lowers the price for the seller, who is the auction's paying client.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Capture first. Add three recordings to the existing inspection: a fifteen-second cold-start audio clip, a motion and audio trace of the lot drive from the phone, and a prompted "anything you noticed but are not sure about" field with a photo or voice note. The inspector's suspicions then exist as data, timestamped and tied to the VIN and the inspector.

Then learn the inspector's read. Pair the recordings and the suspicion notes with the outcomes the auction already holds: arbitration claims and their category, post-sale inspection results, guarantee returns, and the settled price against MMR. Train models that predict, before the car runs, the probability of an arbitrable defect by Appendix I category, with the inspector's own suspicion as a feature rather than a target. Surface the result to the inspector as a prompt: "cold-start audio resembles cars later arbitrated for lifter noise; record or announce". Surface it to the seller's account manager as an expected arbitration cost. The aim is not to replace the inspector. It is to give a new inspector the experienced inspector's ear, with a track record attached. ## Impact If Solved
Arbitration is the most expensive failure in a wholesale sale. With ~8 million units sold a year and an estimated 2–4% drawing claims, at an average adjustment or unwind cost in the high hundreds of dollars, the industry spends somewhere around $150–300M a year on defects the report missed (estimate). That figure excludes the buyers lost after a bad claim. Halving the misses on the categories inspectors already suspect is worth tens of millions a year to a national auction. It would also cut the time it takes a new inspector to report like a veteran from years to months.
