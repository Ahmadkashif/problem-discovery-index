# The DSP Who Can Tell a Resident Is Heading for a Crisis

**Industry:** [[idd-group-homes|IDD Group Homes]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Experienced direct support professionals read the early signs of a behavioural crisis or a medical emergency in residents who cannot speak for themselves, and that read leaves with them, at a turnover rate where two in three leavers go within a year.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #survival-analysis #transformers #gradient-boosting #hidden-markov-models #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #compliance

## The Problem
A group home of five adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities runs on what its staff notice. Several of the residents do not speak, or speak in ways only familiar staff understand. Several have epilepsy, swallowing problems, chronic constipation or a history of aggression or self-injury. A DSP who has worked the same house for three years knows that one man taps the table and paces the hallway for about an hour before he hits someone. She knows that a woman who refuses her evening snack, coughs after drinking and sleeps through her favourite programme is usually two days from an aspiration pneumonia, and that a housemate who keeps pressing his stomach and stops eating needs a bowel check before the weekend. Training material for DSPs groups these conditions as the "Fatal Five": aspiration, dehydration, constipation, seizures and sepsis. They are often unrecognised and considered preventable (Relias, ANCOR and HCQU material, search summary).

None of this is written down in a form that can be learned from. The shift note records that the man "had a good day with some agitation". The behaviour data sheet records an incident only after it happens, as antecedent, behaviour and consequence. The bowel record and fluid log are ticked boxes, completed at the end of the shift. The new DSP, hired three weeks ago, has none of the experienced worker's patterns. She is also the person most likely to be working: across 344,179 DSPs in NCI's 2024 survey, 39% of those on payroll had been with their agency less than a year, and 64% of those who left had stayed under a year (NCI State of the Workforce, 2024).

The consequences are concrete. A missed escalation becomes an assault on a housemate or a DSP, a restraint, a police call or a psychiatric emergency admission. A missed decline becomes an emergency visit for aspiration pneumonia, a bowel obstruction, a prolonged seizure or sepsis, and sometimes a death. Each is a critical incident under the 2024 Access Rule, which from 9 July 2027 requires states to investigate and close at least 90% of them within state timeframes (89 FR 40542). Each costs the agency staff time, investigation exposure and sometimes the placement itself.

## Why It's Unsolved
**Data collection** is the first barrier. The expert's read happens in the moment, during care, while the DSP is cooking, passing medications and redirecting three other people. What she sees (a gait, a facial expression, how fast he eats, a sound he makes) is never recorded as observation. Capturing it means a near-zero-effort way to log a quick observation by voice or a tap at the moment it happens, and the agency has to want those observations kept even when nothing follows. Today's documentation is written for billing and licensing, after the fact, and rarely records the precursor.

**Labelling** is the second. The outcomes are sparse and noisy. A given resident may have a serious behavioural crisis a few times a month and a hospitalisation a few times a year (estimate). Many precursors resolve without incident because the experienced DSP intervened, and those cases are recorded as nothing. This is a selection effect that hides the very skill the model should learn. Experts also disagree with themselves: the same DSP rates the same pacing as "fine" on a calm day and "watch him" after last week's incident. Labels need repeated rating of a sample of observations to estimate intra-rater reliability, and outcome windows need clinical review (was this ER visit for the aspiration the staff saw coming, or for an unrelated fall?).

**Deployment** is the third. The tool has to be faster than the experienced DSP's own recall and usable by a new one on a phone in the middle of a shift. It cannot add to documentation burden, already the most resented part of the job. Alerts have to be few and specific ("Marcus has paced and refused lunch, and last time this preceded an assault within two hours; follow his de-escalation steps 1–3"), or they will be ignored. Every record is protected health information held by the agency, and in some states the record system is run by the state. The model is per-person: residents differ far more from each other than patients in a hospital ward do, so it must learn each person's own signature from little data.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start with capture, not prediction. Give DSPs a fifteen-second voice or tap log for observations, tied to the person's behaviour support plan and health risk plan: "pacing", "refused fluids", "coughing at dinner", "grimacing". Draft the shift note, the bowel and fluid records and the behaviour data sheet from those logs automatically, so capture replaces paperwork rather than adding to it. Ask experienced DSPs to rate a "how worried are you" score for each resident at shift handover. That rating is the tacit judgement itself, made explicit and cheap.

Then learn each person's precursor signatures. Join the observation stream, the handover worry scores, medication and PRN changes, sleep, staffing continuity on each shift and calendar disruptions (a home visit, a day programme closure) to the outcomes: incidents, restraints, ER visits and admissions. Use per-person time-to-event models with shared structure across residents. The output is not a score on a dashboard. It is a short, specific prompt to the DSP on shift, citing the person's own history and the plan's next step, and a weekly summary to the QIDP, nurse and behaviour clinician showing which precursors preceded which events. Over time the experienced DSP's read, with its track record, becomes the house's shared knowledge rather than one person's memory.

## Impact If Solved
A house that catches escalation an hour earlier, and decline a day earlier, avoids restraints, injuries, emergency visits and the investigations that follow each one. Even a one-quarter cut in preventable ER visits and restraints across a 100-home agency would avoid hundreds of critical incident reports a year (estimate), and the hours of DSP, house manager and QIDP time each one costs. New DSPs would carry the house's knowledge from their first shift, which helps them stay past the first year. That matters in an industry where 26% of agencies turned away referrals in 2024 because they could not staff them (NCI).
