# The QIDP Rewriting the Plan After Every Restraint

**Industry:** [[idd-group-homes|IDD Group Homes]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Each restraint, PRN dose or serious incident sends the programme coordinator back into a person's plan, the behaviour data and the committee paperwork, on top of annual plan meetings for a caseload that never shrinks.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #bert #transformers #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #compliance #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
The Qualified Intellectual Disabilities Professional in an ICF/IID, or the programme coordinator in a waiver home, owns each resident's plan. That means writing the individual service or programme plan, running the annual planning meeting, monitoring progress on every outcome monthly, and keeping the behaviour support and health risk plans current with the clinicians. A coordinator often carries 15–30 people across several houses (estimate).

Incidents multiply the work. When a DSP uses a physical hold or a PRN psychotropic, or a resident is hurt or goes to the ER, the coordinator reviews the DSPs' narratives and the behaviour data. She requests a plan review from the behaviour clinician, assembles the packet for the human rights committee and updates the plan and the risk assessment. She also answers the investigator's requests and documents the corrective action. Misuse of restrictive interventions is a federal critical incident category, and from 9 July 2027 states must close at least 90% of investigations and corrective actions on time and review every plan at least every 12 months for 90% of participants (89 FR 40542). Much of the paperwork lands on her desk.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
She took the job to help people build lives, and spends her week copying the same facts between the incident report, the behaviour data sheet, the plan, the committee packet and the monthly progress summary. Notes arrive written at the end of DSPs' double shifts, incomplete and inconsistent, and she has to chase them before she can act. A plan review she could not get to becomes a citation in the next ICF/IID survey or licensing review, under her name. The role turns over too (estimate), and each departure leaves her colleagues with a heavier caseload.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An assistant that does the assembly. When an incident or restrictive intervention is logged, it pulls the DSPs' narratives, the behaviour data for the past month, the PRN record, the current plan and prior incidents. It drafts the plan-review request, the committee packet and the corrective-action entry in the agency's formats, and flags what is missing ("no de-escalation steps recorded before the PRN"). Monthly, it drafts each person's progress summary from the notes mapped to plan outcomes, and it keeps a calendar of every plan, assessment and committee review due across her caseload. She reviews, edits and signs.

## Impact If Solved
If assembly and drafting fall from ~1–2 hours to ~20 minutes per incident, and monthly summaries from ~30 to ~10 minutes per person (both estimates), a coordinator with 25 people and a few incidents a week gets back ~1–2 days a month. That time goes to the residents and the DSPs carrying out the plans.
