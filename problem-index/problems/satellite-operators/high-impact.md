# The Telemetry Signature Only a Senior Spacecraft Engineer Recognises

**Industry:** [[satellite-operators|Satellite Operators]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A satellite tells its operator it is failing months before any alarm trips, in telemetry patterns only a few senior engineers can read, and when they retire the fleet loses the early warning that decides between a planned transition and a dark transponder.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #autoencoders #lstms-and-grus #change-point-detection #survival-analysis #feature-engineering #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A geostationary communications satellite streams several thousand telemetry channels to its operator's mission control centre around the clock: wheel speeds and motor currents, bearing temperatures, battery cell voltages through every eclipse, thruster pulse counts, solar array drive currents, travelling-wave-tube amplifier helix currents, heater duty cycles. The mission control system checks each channel against fixed upper and lower limits. A limit violation pages the console. Everything inside the limits is, as far as the software is concerned, normal.

The engineers who have flown a fleet for ten or fifteen years know that "inside the limits" hides most of the story. A reaction wheel whose friction torque has started to climb shows it first as a slightly higher motor current at the same speed, and a temperature that recovers more slowly after each zero crossing. A battery cell that is losing capacity shows it as a voltage that sags a little earlier in each eclipse season than its neighbours. A travelling-wave tube nearing end of life shows it in helix current noise. None of these trips an alarm until the hardware is close to failing. The senior subsystem engineer sees the pattern in a trend plot, compares it with what a sister satellite on the same bus did six years ago, and says "that wheel is going". They are usually right, and they usually can't write down exactly why.

That read is worth a great deal. It decides whether an operator switches to a spare wheel on its own schedule or loses attitude control during a customer's live event; whether traffic on a failing transponder is moved to another satellite before the customer notices; whether a claim is filed under the satellite's in-orbit insurance policy while the policy is in force; and when a replacement satellite is ordered, which is a decision with a lead time of ~3 years or more (estimate). Job ads for operations engineers ask for exactly this — 24/7 on-call anomaly response, telemetry trending, fleet availability against customer SLAs — and ask for five or more years of experience because the judgement takes that long to build. Operators consolidating fleets (SES absorbed Intelsat's in July 2025) inherit satellites whose history lives in another company's engineers.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's read happens in their head while scrolling trend plots, and is recorded, if at all, as a line in a shift log or an email to the manufacturer. The telemetry is archived in full, but nothing records the moment the engineer first became suspicious, which channels they looked at, or which sister satellite they compared against. Reconstructing those reads after the fact means interviewing engineers about events years old and aligning their recollections to timestamps. Capturing them going forward needs an annotation surface on the console that costs the engineer seconds, not minutes, at three in the morning.

The labelling problem is that anomalies are rare per satellite, every bus type behaves differently, and experts disagree with themselves. A fleet of thirty satellites may see a handful of wheel or battery events a decade. The same engineer who called a wheel "going" in 2019 called a near-identical signature "seasonal" in 2022 because the earlier wheel recovered. Ground truth is late and partial: a wheel that is switched off before it fails never produces a failure label, so the expert's successful early calls look like false alarms in the data. The public ESA anomaly benchmark (ESA-ADB, 2024), built from three ESA missions, showed how hard even careful annotation is, and found that standard detectors fall short of what operators need.

The deployment problem is that the console already has too many alarms. A new detector that pages controllers on every unusual wiggle will be muted within a week. Any tool has to arrive as a ranked shortlist of channels worth an engineer's attention, with the comparable historical cases attached, faster than the engineer could find them in the archive, and quiet the rest of the time. Manufacturers hold the cross-operator view of each bus type but under contract and export control, so an operator usually trains on its own fleet alone.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An anomaly record first and a model second. Every time an engineer flags a trend, opens an investigation or writes to the manufacturer, the console captures a structured note in a few clicks — satellite, subsystem, channels, suspected mechanism, confidence — linked to the telemetry window, and later to the outcome: recovered, switched to redundancy, failed, claimed. Historical anomaly reports and shift logs are mined to seed the record.

On top of it, learn the engineer's reads. Unsupervised models (autoencoders and sequence models per subsystem) learn each satellite's normal behaviour by season, eclipse and load; change-point detection finds when a channel's relationship to its drivers shifts; and a retrieval layer shows "the three most similar signatures in the fleet's history, and what happened next". Survival models estimate time-to-failure for wheels, batteries and tubes, conditioned on the flagged signatures, for the fleet planning and insurance teams. The engineer confirms or dismisses each surfaced trend, and every response becomes a label.

## Impact If Solved
An operator that sees component failures months earlier can move customer traffic, switch to redundancy and schedule manoeuvres on its own terms, protect SLA revenue on transponders that earn millions a year each (estimate), and time replacement satellites and insurance claims better. Keeping a retiring engineer's reads in a searchable, graded record turns the most expensive knowledge in a satellite operator — the ability to see a failure coming — into something the fleet keeps.
