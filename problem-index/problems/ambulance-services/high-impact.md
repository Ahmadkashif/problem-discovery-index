# The Paramedic's Read of Who Is Sicker Than the Vitals Say

**Industry:** [[ambulance-services|Ambulance Services]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced paramedic knows within minutes which "weak and dizzy" patient is septic or infarcting before the monitor shows it, and that read sets treatment, destination and the level of service billed, yet nobody records it as a prediction or learns whether it was right.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #transformers #large-language-models #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A paramedic on a 24-hour shift answers ~12 calls (an estimate). Most look alike on the dispatch screen: fall, weakness, shortness of breath, sick person. At the door she takes in the room before the monitor is out of the bag. The skin is mottled at the knees. The patient is answering a beat too slowly. The pill bottles on the counter say heart failure, and the daughter says he has not eaten since yesterday. Senior medics call this "looks sick", and they mean something specific. They have seen hundreds of patients whose numbers were still normal on scene and who were in septic shock or having a heart attack by the time they reached the emergency department.

That read drives every decision that follows. It decides whether she starts fluids and draws a lactate where her protocols allow, whether she sends a 12-lead to the cardiac centre, and whether she runs lights and sirens. It decides whether she drives past the nearest community hospital to a stroke or STEMI centre twenty minutes further away. It also decides the claim. Medicare pays $551.48 for an advanced life support emergency transport (A0427) and $290.25 for a basic non-emergency one (A0428), as CY2026 urban rates at a GPCI of 1.000, including the 2% add-on (CMS fee schedule). The difference has to be supported by the assessment she documents.

The read is almost never written down as what it is, a prediction. The ePCR captures vitals, interventions and a free-text narrative, written an hour later at the hospital or at the end of the shift. The outcome sits in the hospital's record: admitted to the ICU, sepsis confirmed, cath lab activated, discharged home from the ED. Few agencies receive that outcome systematically. So the crew never learns which reads were right, the junior EMT learns only by riding with the senior one, and the judgement leaves with the medic. Full-time paramedic turnover was 27% and EMT turnover 36% in 2022 (AAA, search summary).

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's read has to be captured at the patient's side, during the busiest minutes of the call, by someone with gloves on. A narrative written after hospital handoff is a reconstruction shaped by what the ED found. The fields that would make the read gradable do not exist on most ePCR forms: the medic's own estimate of how likely an ICU admission, a STEMI or sepsis is, and the cues behind it (skin, mentation, work of breathing, the scene). Collecting them means a near-zero-effort capture surface, perhaps one tap or a spoken phrase at first contact, that does not slow care.

The labelling problem has two halves. The outcome label must come from the hospital, via a health information exchange, a hospital data-exchange feed such as ESO's or a state trauma and stroke registry, and linkage is patchy and delayed. Experts also disagree with themselves. The same paramedic rates a borderline patient differently at the end of a long shift or after a recent bad outcome. Repeated ratings of the same vignettes are needed to measure that ceiling, and the outcome itself is confounded by the medic's actions: the patient who got early fluids may never show the shock she predicted.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. Any assistance has to arrive on scene or in the first minutes of transport, faster than the medic's own read, on a tablet in a moving vehicle with patchy coverage. A tool that second-guesses a senior medic after the fact will be resented. One that tells a new EMT, before they leave the house, that this combination of age, heart rate, temperature and dispatch complaint has a one-in-four chance of ICU admission will be used. And the same record is the claim, so operators will hesitate to store a "severity prediction" that an auditor could read against the level billed.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A severity read captured at first contact and graded later. The crew logs a one-tap impression (stable, sick, critical) and, where it applies, a suspicion (sepsis, STEMI, stroke, occult bleed). That joins the ePCR's structured vitals, dispatch determinant, scene and history fields and the narrative, and is linked to the hospital outcome: ED disposition, ICU admission within 24 hours, sepsis or STEMI confirmation, in-hospital death. Gradient-boosted models trained on structured fields and transformer embeddings of the narrative learn which cues predicted deterioration on this agency's population, with the senior medics' impressions as a feature and as a benchmark.

The output is advisory and fast: a calibrated risk shown as the crew finishes the first set of vitals, with the two or three cues driving it. Back at the station there is a weekly outcome feed telling each medic how their "sick" and "not sick" calls turned out. Over time the system shows the agency which senior medics' reads beat the protocol triggers, so their cues can be taught, and which documentation patterns leave a correctly upgraded call unsupported on the claim. Development runs on synthetic and de-identified records; deployment stays inside the agency's PHI boundary.

## Impact If Solved
If an agency running 50,000 transports a year caught even 2% more of the patients who were sicker than their vitals said (an estimate), that would be about 1,000 earlier sepsis bundles, cath-lab alerts or right-destination transports a year. The same records would close the documentation gap behind ambulance claims' 10.4% improper payment rate, nearly half of it insufficient documentation (CMS CERT, FY2025). And the judgement of a medic who leaves after three years would stay behind as a graded, teachable pattern instead of walking out with her.
