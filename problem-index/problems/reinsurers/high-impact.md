# The Underwriter's Own View of Risk, Never Graded

**Industry:** [[reinsurers|Reinsurers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every property catastrophe treaty is priced on a vendor model that the lead underwriter then loads for what they believe about the cedent, and that adjustment, which moves price more than any other input, is recorded without reasons and never graded against what the cedent's programme later lost.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #bayesian-inference #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
A property catastrophe underwriter at a reinsurer may see ~150–300 programmes in a 1 January renewal season (estimate). For each, the broker sends a submission: location-level exposure or an aggregate by zone, a schedule of the cedent's historical losses, the cedent's own model output, the proposed layers and the expiring terms. The reinsurer's cat team re-runs the exposure through one or more licensed models and produces an exceedance-probability curve and a modelled loss cost for each layer. That number is the start of the price, not the price.

The experienced underwriter then adjusts it. They add a load because this cedent's insured values look ~15–25% below rebuild cost, judged from the average value per square foot and the construction mix (estimate). They add another because the cedent writes in Florida counties where litigated claims ran far above the model's assumption. They take one off because the cedent's claims department settled the last two storms fast and below the industry's loss ratio. They load for perils the model treats lightly, such as severe convective storm, wildfire on the urban edge or flood under a homeowners form. They also load for the feeling that the submission is too clean. Underwriters describe this as knowing which cedents' data flatters them. It is tacit knowledge in the plain sense: pattern recognition built over many renewals and a few large events, which the underwriter can justify case by case but cannot write down as a rule. Milliman sells catastrophe model review and adjustment, and rating agencies expect reinsurers to vet vendor models for their own view of risk. The adjustment is therefore expected, examined in aggregate and central to the business.

It is also the least recorded decision in the process. The modelled loss cost is stored and the bound price is stored. The gap between them appears as a single load factor in a pricing workbook, or as a line in a referral memo. The reasons — insured-value adequacy, claims culture, data quality, non-modelled perils, trend — are blended, so the reinsurer cannot later tell which of them carried information. When a lead underwriter retires or moves to a competitor, the reinsurer keeps the pricing history and loses the reasons behind it.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the underwriter must record the adjustment, broken into its reasons, at the moment of pricing. That moment falls in the busiest weeks of the year, when a firm-order deadline from the broker makes every minute of documentation a cost. Notes written after the season are rationalised. The submissions themselves arrive in incompatible formats, so even the facts the underwriter reacted to, such as the average insured value or the share of frame construction, are not captured consistently across cedents.

The labelling problem is that catastrophe losses are rare. A cedent may go a decade without a loss that reaches the layers, so its programme gives almost no labels, and when a large event does hit, it hits many cedents at once with correlated errors. The honest label is the cedent's own loss experience against the model across all events large and small: ground-up claims from moderate storms that never touched the reinsurance layers, the ratio of reported losses to modelled event losses after every event, and the development of those losses over time. Experts also disagree with themselves. The same underwriter loads Florida more heavily in the renewal after a bad season, and blind re-pricing of the same submission months apart gives different answers.

The deployment problem is speed and pride. The output must arrive inside the cat team's existing turnaround, before the quote goes to the broker, and faster than the underwriter's own recall of the cedent. An experienced lead underwriter will not use a system that grades their judgement unless it first saves them time and shows them the evidence for each suggested load.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An own-view-of-risk ledger first, a model second. Every priced programme records the modelled loss cost per layer from each licensed model, then each adjustment as a separate, reasoned line: insured-value adequacy, data quality, claims culture and litigation, non-modelled perils, trend and portfolio fit. The ledger is drafted automatically from the underwriter's pricing workbook, the referral memo and the submission, then confirmed in seconds. Each line is later graded against the cedent's realised losses, reported event by event through cedent notifications and loss bordereaux, with industry loss indices such as PCS and PERILS as the benchmark for how far the cedent ran from the market.

On top of the ledger, learn the underwriter's read. Pair submission features (exposure mix, value per unit, geocoding quality, claims history, state litigation climate) with the loads experienced underwriters applied and with the cedent's later experience. Then surface to a junior underwriter, or a successor, the adjustments the best reads would have suggested and the evidence behind each. The aim is not to replace the lead underwriter but to make their pattern recognition, with its track record, available in time for the quote.

## Impact If Solved
A reinsurer that can show which adjustments carry information can defend the loads that matter to brokers in a softening market and drop the ones that do not, rather than cutting all of them alike. In money terms, one point of loss ratio on ~$30B a year of property catastrophe premium ceded by US cedents is ~$300M a year across the market (estimate). For a single reinsurer writing ~$2B of property cat, it is ~$20M a year (estimate). Better-aimed loads plausibly move the loss ratio by a point or more in a bad year, because the costliest mistakes are the cedents whose experience runs well above the model. Keeping a retiring lead underwriter's documented reads also turns the industry's most expensive recurring loss of expertise into an asset the firm keeps.
