# Open, Moved, Closed or Never There

**Industry:** [[directory-and-list-publishers|Directory & List Publishers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every record a compiler sells rests on a judgement — is this business still here, at this number, in this line of work — made in under a minute by a caller or an analyst reading conflicting sources, and neither the reasoning nor its later outcome is kept in a form that could teach the next decision.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #large-language-models #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A business record in a compiled file is a bundle of assertions from different sources with different ages. The carrier listing says the number belongs to "Ace Plumbing". The state registry says the LLC is active but its annual report is overdue. The website still loads but its copyright line says 2022. Reviews stopped last spring. A newer filing at the same address belongs to a company with a different name. A compiler has to decide which of these to believe, publish one answer, and sell it — to a sales team, a mailer, a map platform — by the thousand.

When the sources disagree, the record goes to a person. At the scale of the national compilers that means a phone queue: Data Axle runs 150+ data specialists making ~60,000 calls a day, at an average call time under a minute, and routes records with outdated attributes to tele-research; its newsletter reports downgrading ~145,000 records a month since adding the step. The caller hears a ring-out, a voicemail greeting, a receptionist, a disconnected tone, or someone who answers with a different business name, and enters a status.

Experienced researchers and compilation analysts read these signals in ways they cannot fully write down. A phone answered "service department" for a plumber means a lead-generation front forwarding calls to whoever bids, not a business. A contractor that re-registers under a new LLC every two years is the same firm, not a closure and an opening. A seasonal business that rings out in February is closed for the season, not closed. A dental practice whose dentist retired often continues under a group owner at the same address. Retail numbers that ring out at ten on a Tuesday morning mean something different from office numbers that do. This is tacit knowledge in the vault's sense: pattern recognition built over thousands of calls, used every minute, and recorded as a code.

The outcome of each judgement is observed, but somewhere else and later. A customer's emails bounce; a mailer's pieces come back undeliverable; a map platform's users report a closure; the next call cycle finds the number reassigned. These arrive as credit requests, complaint tickets and refreshed source files. They are almost never joined back to the decision that published the record, the researcher who made it, or the signals they saw. So the compiler cannot say which signals predict closure, which researchers are best at which categories, or how accurate its file is for, say, five-to-nine-employee HVAC contractors in Ohio.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's reasoning must be captured during a sub-minute call inside an auto-dialer workflow measured on production. Asking the caller to explain each decision would halve throughput; the status code is all the workflow tolerates. Call audio, where it is recorded at all, is rarely aligned to the record's source history, and the analyst who resolves conflicting sources at a desk leaves no trace beyond the final field value.

The labelling challenge is that ground truth is itself a judgement. A call verifies that a phone was answered, not that a business operates; two researchers calling the same record on the same day can reach different conclusions, and the same researcher codes an ambiguous answering-service call differently in week one and week fifty. Outcome labels are delayed by months and biased towards complaints: customers report the records that bounced, not the ones that quietly worked, and a record nobody bought is never tested at all.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. Any assistance must arrive before the dialer connects and be faster than the caller's own read, or it will be ignored; any automatic decision must be right often enough that the compiler will publish without a call. Data Axle already uses a machine-learning tool, Smart-Calling, to prioritise which records to call — but choosing whom to call is a different problem from deciding what the call meant, and neither is graded against what happened next.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A verification ledger first. Every record keeps its source assertions with dates, every call outcome with researcher and timestamp, every customer bounce and credit claim, and every later correction — joined per field, not per record. From that ledger, each field gets a calibrated probability of being true and an expected time to change, and each queued record gets an estimate of how likely a call is to change what the compiler publishes. Records with high confidence publish without a call; records where the call is decisive go to the researchers best at that category; ambiguous outcomes go to a second read instead of a penalty.

On top of the ledger, learn the researchers' reads. Pair call outcomes and the short notes researchers leave with the signals present at the time and with what later proved true, and surface those patterns — "lead-gen network: one forwarding number shared across a cluster of listings" — to whoever handles the record next. The goal is to make the experienced researcher's pattern recognition available to the new hire on day one, with its track record attached.

## Impact If Solved
A compiler that can state field-level accuracy by segment can sell an accuracy figure rather than a refund policy, and price the segments it verifies best. Routing calls to records where the call actually changes the answer could plausibly cut ~30–50% of verification calls or redirect them to decayed segments (estimate) — on a 25M-call year, millions of calls. And the judgement that walks out with every departing researcher stays with the firm.
