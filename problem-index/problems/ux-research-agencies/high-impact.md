# Nobody Ever Finds Out Whether the Finding Was Right

**Industry:** [[ux-research-agencies|UX Research Agencies]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Research makes testable predictions about what users will do, hands them over in a report, and the discipline has no record of how often those predictions were correct.
**Tags:** #hypothesis-testing #bayesian-inference #confidence-intervals #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #probability-distributions #tacit-knowledge-ml #descriptive-statistics

## The Problem
A research study ends with claims. Users do not understand this term. The second step in this flow causes abandonment. This concept is compelling to small business owners and not to enterprises. Each of those is a prediction about behaviour at scale, made from a study of eight people, and each is checkable against what happens when the product ships.

Nobody checks. The agency delivers the report and the engagement closes. The client's product team acts on some recommendations, ignores others, and ships months later alongside a dozen unrelated changes. The behavioural data that would confirm or refute each claim accumulates in the client's analytics, and no one returns to the report.

The consequences run in both directions. Findings that were wrong are never corrected, so the same wrong belief is carried into the next study by the same researcher. Findings that were right are never confirmed, so the researcher has no evidence for the next stakeholder who doubts them.

The generalisation gap makes it worse. A qualitative study of eight participants reliably surfaces severe usability problems — this is the well-founded part of the method and it works. It does not support statements about prevalence, preference distribution, or what a market segment wants, and report summaries routinely contain exactly those statements because that is what stakeholders ask for. Researchers know this. The methodological caveat sits on slide forty and the summary is on slide three.

And the absence of a track record is precisely why the function is structurally insecure. Research budgets are cut first in every downturn, the stated reason is always unprovable return, and the discipline's response has been advocacy rather than evidence — because evidence would require the follow-up nobody funds.

## Why It's Unsolved
The engagement ends before the answer exists. A study concludes in week six; the product change ships in month five; the behavioural evidence matures a quarter after that. No standard commercial structure spans that gap, and a client has no obvious reason to pay for a retrospective that might conclude the research was wrong.

The attribution is also genuinely hard. A recommendation is implemented partially, alongside other changes, sometimes in a form the researcher would not recognise. Establishing that the finding was correct requires separating it from everything else that shipped, which is the same causal problem the rest of this cluster faces and is harder here because the unit is a claim rather than a design.

There is a cultural difficulty too. The discipline has spent two decades defending qualitative methods against demands for statistical proof, and the defence — that small-sample qualitative work answers different questions than quantitative work — is correct. It has also hardened into a general resistance to being measured, which makes proposing accuracy tracking sound like an attack on the method rather than a way to strengthen it.

And the base rates are unknown because nobody has ever assembled them. How often does a severe usability finding from a five-person study correspond to a measurable behavioural effect at scale? The discipline's most-cited heuristics rest on work from the 1990s, on very different products, and have been debated for years without new evidence, because generating new evidence would require exactly this corpus.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Record findings as predictions. A finding stated with a falsifiable expectation — this step will show elevated abandonment, this term will generate support contacts — is checkable later, and stating it that way costs nothing at the time. Most findings can be written this way and currently are not, because nothing downstream ever reads them.

Contract for the look-back. A short retrospective engagement one or two quarters after release, comparing predictions to observed behaviour, is a small piece of work with disproportionate value: it produces the agency's track record, it tells the client which research was worth commissioning, and it is the only mechanism by which a researcher ever learns.

Separate the claims by what the method supports. Severe usability problems, generalisation about prevalence, and preference distribution require different evidence, and a report that labels each claim with its support level — observed in six of eight participants, versus inferred, versus requires quantitative confirmation — is both more honest and more useful to the person deciding what to act on.

Build the base rates. Across many studies and clients, the relationship between qualitative finding severity and observed behavioural effect is estimable, and it would give the discipline the empirical foundation its central heuristics have lacked for thirty years. This has to be built by someone running many studies — an agency or a platform, not a single in-house team.

## Impact If Solved
This is the evidence the discipline has been arguing without for its entire existence. A research practice with a track record can defend its budget with a record rather than a methodology lecture, can tell clients which kinds of study actually changed outcomes, and can correct its own wrong beliefs. And the base rate work would settle long-running methodological arguments — about sample size, about what qualitative work can claim — that have been conducted on anecdote because nobody assembled the data.
