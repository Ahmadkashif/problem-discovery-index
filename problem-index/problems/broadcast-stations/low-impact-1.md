# The Public File and the Quarterly Issues List

**Industry:** [[broadcast-stations|Broadcast TV & Radio Stations]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Compliance calendars and document management already track deadlines and store files for any business, but a station's public inspection file runs on FCC-specific clocks and content rules, and the quarterly issues/programs list is still assembled by hand from rundowns and memory.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #bert #transformers #k-means-clustering #compliance #workflow-orchestration #automation

## The Problem
Every full-power and Class A station keeps an online public inspection file hosted by the FCC, and its contents are licence evidence. By the 10th of January, April, July and October the station must upload a quarterly issues/programs list: the programming that gave its "most significant treatment of community issues" in the previous three months. Law firms advise at least five issues per quarter, each tied to specific programmes with dates, times and durations. The FCC's base fine for a missing or late list is $10,000. The same file must also hold the political file, updated as candidate and issue orders arrive through every election window, annual EEO public file reports, children's programming reports for TV, and the retrans and must-carry election statement filed by 1 October 2026. A missing document becomes a fine or consent decree at renewal, or a petition to deny.

At most stations the issues list is put together in the last week of the quarter by a news director, program director or office manager. They scroll through newscast rundowns, public affairs show logs and PSA schedules and pick five issues that sound like the community's concerns. Radio clusters do it per signal, often with a syndicated public-affairs programme as the backbone. The political file is uploaded order by order by traffic or sales assistants, with timing errors that show up only when an outside group checks.

## What Already Exists
Generic compliance calendars and GRC platforms track due dates and owners. Document management (SharePoint, Box, Google Drive) stores files. Newsroom systems (AP ENPS, Avid iNEWS, Ross) hold every rundown. Broadcast law firms send quarterly advisories and checklists, and state broadcaster associations run alternative inspection programs. Some traffic systems export political order data for upload.

## The Customisation Gap
The generic tools know dates, not what the FCC counts. The station-specific logic needs four things. First, the FCC's public-file taxonomy and per-item timing rules: quarterly by the 10th, political items "immediately", EEO reports on the licence-renewal anniversary, election statements for a three-year cycle. Second, a link from newsroom and programming systems to a running catalogue of community issues, so each story and public-affairs segment is tagged by issue as it airs rather than reconstructed later. Third, a draft list that meets the content expectations: variety of format, duration, source and daypart, local production weighted, and issues that move with the community. Fourth, a gap check before the deadline: an issue with no programme, a signal with nothing local. The tool must also respect that the list is the licensee's certification and stays a human signature.

## Impact If Solved
For a group with dozens of stations and clusters, issues-list preparation drops from a day per signal per quarter to an hour of review (estimate). Late-file fines and renewal challenges based on thin lists become rare. The issue catalogue built along the way gives the news and programming staff a record of what the station actually covered, which is also what the community and the FCC ask about.
