# The Sell-Out Call Made by Feel

**Industry:** [[broadcast-stations|Broadcast TV & Radio Stations]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A station's inventory perishes every time a unit airs, and the people who decide what each daypart-week is worth, how much to hold back and what to preempt do it on a feel for their market that is never recorded, never graded and leaves when they do.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #bayesian-inference #survival-analysis #time-series-forecasting #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A TV station has a fixed number of commercial units in every program. A radio cluster has a fixed number of minutes per hour on each signal. All of it perishes when it airs. Every week the general sales manager, usually with an inventory or pricing manager and the traffic director, decides what each daypart in each future week is worth. They set the rate grid local account executives sell from and the floor the national rep can quote, and decide how many units to hold back for business that has not yet called: a car dealer's month-end push, a late scatter buy, a ballot-measure campaign in October. When a daypart oversells, they decide which orders to preempt and which advertisers will accept a makegood without walking.

Experienced managers make these calls on a read nobody has written down. They know, to take illustrative examples, that early news in this market sells out about three weeks before a holiday and that the last ~20% of units goes at roughly double the early rate. They know this auto group always comes in late and always pays. They know a preempted personal-injury firm's spot costs nothing, but a preempted furniture retailer's spot loses the account. They know that in an even year, issue groups will pay whatever is asked for the last week before an election, so holding too little is the expensive mistake. In 2026 AdImpact projects $11.6B in political spending, $5.6B of it on broadcast TV. The value of the hold decision is concentrated in exactly the weeks the station has least time to think about it.

The outcome of every one of these calls is recorded in full in the traffic system: rate per unit, sell-out by daypart, preemptions, makegoods, credits, unsold units filled with promos or remnant direct response. The decision is not. Nobody records what the GSM expected a daypart to sell at, what was held and why, or which preemption was chosen over which alternative. When a seasoned GSM retires or moves to another group, as consolidation now makes common, the station keeps the revenue history and loses the judgement that produced it. The national half of the same inventory is priced at the rep under the same blindness, analysed under [[media-rep-firms|Media Representative Firms]]. This note is about the owner of the inventory, who sets the grid and the holds that the rep sells within.

## Why It's Unsolved
**Data collection.** The judgement has to be captured from the expert while they make it, during the busiest weeks of the year. Rate changes happen in the traffic or sales system as an override with no reason field. Holds are often informal: a daypart blocked in someone's head or a spreadsheet, not in the system. Reconstructing past decisions means joining rate-card versions, order histories, preemption logs and avails emails that were never designed to be joined. The national demand signal sits in the rep's systems, and political demand in the political file and the trackers' reports, none of it in the station's own data.

**Labelling.** Revenue is a noisy label for decision quality. A hold that went unsold might have been a correct bet that lost: a race became uncompetitive and the issue money moved elsewhere. Strong sell-out might have come from a ratings spike rather than good pricing. The honest label is counterfactual: what the units would have earned under a different grid or hold. Experienced managers also disagree with themselves. The same GSM prices the same November week differently after a bad October, and two GSMs at sister stations in similar markets apply different rules. Any training set needs repeated judgements on a sample of the same situations to measure that inconsistency before treating it as signal.

**Deployment.** The decision is made in minutes, many times a day, inside the traffic and proposal workflow. A recommendation that arrives in a weekly report after the avails have gone out will be ignored. It has to be faster than the GSM's own recall: a suggested rate and hold level shown at the moment an order or avails request is being priced, with the reason in the manager's own terms ("last two Novembers this daypart sold out by the 20th"). It also has to survive the political window, when candidate orders must be charged the lowest unit charge for their class and every rate decision becomes a compliance fact.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A yield record first and a model second. Every rate-grid change, hold and preemption choice is captured as a short structured entry: daypart-week, expected sell-out, rate set, units held and for whom, alternative preemption candidates. Entries are drafted automatically from the traffic system's changes and confirmed in a click, not written from scratch. Each entry is graded after air against what the units actually earned and what the next-best use would plausibly have earned.

On top of the record, a demand model per station: probability of sell-out and expected clearing rate by daypart-week as a function of weeks to air, current pacing, ratings, seasonality, category demand from occurrence data and, in even years, race competitiveness. The model is calibrated against the managers' own past calls, so it can show where it agrees with them and where it does not. It surfaces the experienced GSM's recurring reads as rules a successor can see, with their track record. For preemptions, it ranks candidate orders by the expected cost of displacing each one: makegood liability, churn risk of the advertiser and rate difference.

## Impact If Solved
Yield management moved airline and hotel revenue several points (estimate) by pricing perishable inventory against forecast demand rather than habit. A station that gained even ~1–2% of core advertising revenue on a $20–60M station (estimate) would add hundreds of thousands of dollars a year at almost no cost. A group with 200 stations would see it at scale. The larger gain is continuity: the pricing and hold judgement of the best GSM in a group becomes something the group keeps and can teach, rather than something that retires.
