# The Principal's Red Pen

**Industry:** [[architecture-firms|Architecture Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** The senior architect who can flip through a construction set and point to the details that will leak, clash or come back as RFIs carries the firm's real quality system in their head, and it is never recorded, graded or handed on.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #cnns #object-detection #transformers #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
Before a construction document set is issued for permit or bid, most firms run a QA/QC review. In practice that means a principal or senior project architect with twenty or thirty years of construction administration behind them spends one to three days on a set of 150 to 400 sheets (estimate) and marks it up in red. They do not read every note. They go straight to the places where buildings fail: the roof-to-wall transition and parapet cap, the window head and sill flashing, the slab edge at a curtain wall, the rated corridor wall where a duct passes through, the stair section where the structural framing is drawn shallower than it will be built, the accessible toilet room where the door swing and grab bars fight the plumbing chase. They know, from having been on site when it went wrong, which of these the junior team drew from a standard detail that doesn't fit this condition.

That review is the most consequential quality judgement in the firm, and it is exercised under the worst conditions: the week before a deadline, by the person with the least available time, on a set produced by people who will not be in the room when the markups are picked up. Its results are scattered across a Bluebeam file, a pickup checklist and the reviewer's memory. When the building is in construction, the errors the review missed come back as RFIs, change orders and occasionally water intrusion or a professional liability claim — and nobody ties them back to the sheet, the detail or the review that should have caught them. Design errors are the largest single cause of claims against design professionals (one Canadian insurer put them at 55% of claims, a dated figure). The firm has no measure of which of its standard details generate RFIs on every project, which project architects' sets are clean, or whether the principal's red pen is better on envelope than on code.

When that principal retires — and the profession's licensed population is ageing while the path to licensure takes over twelve years — the judgement leaves with them.

## Why It's Unsolved
**Data collection.** The expert's judgement has to be captured while they perform it, and they perform it by marking up PDFs at speed. Historical redlines exist in Bluebeam markup layers and scanned paper sets, but they are free-form clouds, arrows and abbreviations ("CONF W/ STRUCT", "FLASH?", "SEE ME") that must be aligned to the sheet region and building element they refer to. The outcome data that would grade the review sits in other organisations: RFIs and change orders in the contractor's Procore or ACC project, warranty calls with the owner, claims with the insurer under privilege. Assembling even one project's record of "markup → issued drawing → construction consequence" means negotiating access to the contractor's closeout archive.

**Labelling.** Experts disagree with each other and with themselves. Two principals reviewing the same wall section flag different things, and the same principal flags more the week after a leak on another job. A redline is also not a label of risk — it mixes genuine hazards with preference ("use our standard parapet") and drafting cleanup. Labels have to separate those categories, and the outcome label (did this detail generate an RFI, a change order, a failure?) is sparse, delayed and confounded by the contractor's own behaviour: some contractors send RFIs on everything, others build and claim later.

**Deployment.** A tool that flags 600 issues on a 300-sheet set will be ignored by the second project. It has to be faster and more selective than the principal: a ranked map of the twenty places on this set most like the places that went wrong before, available inside the markup tool the principal already uses, the night before the review rather than after it. Liability culture adds a further constraint — many firms are advised not to create discoverable records of known errors, so the system has to be framed and governed as quality assurance, not as an error log.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A review assistant built from the firm's own history. It ingests past issued sets, their QA/QC markups, the RFI and change order logs from closed-out projects (with the reference to sheet and detail that most logs already carry), and the firm's standard detail library. A vision model locates building elements and detail types on each sheet; a second model scores each region by how often similar regions, drawn by similar teams for similar building types, produced markups and construction-phase questions. Before review, the principal opens a heat map of the set: the parapet detail reused from a different roof assembly, the wall type that changes rating between plan and schedule, the window detail that has generated an RFI on four of the last six projects. The principal still makes every call; the tool tells them where to look first and records what they decided, so every review becomes training data and a standard detail that keeps failing gets retired.

## Impact If Solved
If a principal's two-day review can be focused on the thirty places that matter and those places are caught before issue, each avoided RFI saves hours of CA time and each avoided change order protects both the owner's contingency and the architect's relationship. On a mid-size institutional project with several hundred RFIs (estimate), cutting design-attributable RFIs by a third would return hundreds of CA hours and a measurable reduction in errors-and-omissions exposure — and the principal's judgement would outlast their career at the firm.
