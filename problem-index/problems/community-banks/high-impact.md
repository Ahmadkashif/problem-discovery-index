# The Risk Grade Set by the Lender Who Made the Loan

**Industry:** [[community-banks|Community Banks]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A community bank grades every commercial loan on a single-digit scale set by the lender who booked it, moves the grade after the problem is visible, and never records the senior credit officer's early read of a drifting borrower in a form anyone can learn from.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #large-language-models #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #compliance #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A commercial lender at a $700M community bank carries ~150 to 300 relationships (estimate): owner-occupied buildings, a contractor's equipment line, three farms, a dozen investor properties, a car wash. Every loan has a risk grade, typically on a nine- or ten-point scale where the top grades are "pass", then "special mention", "substandard", "doubtful" and "loss". The lender assigns the grade at origination and is expected to revisit it at each annual review, when the borrower's new tax returns and financial statements are spread and the debt service coverage ratio recalculated. The grade feeds the CECL allowance, the watch list, the monthly board credit report, the bank's own capital planning and, above all, the examiner's asset-quality component rating.

The grade is the bank's most consequential recurring judgement and the one it measures least. Lenders are slow to downgrade their own loans; they know the borrower, they believe the story, and a downgrade invites scrutiny of their origination. Loan review, often an outside firm, samples a share of the book once a year. The examiner samples again every 12 to 18 months. The pattern most chief credit officers describe is the same: by the time a loan is downgraded, the borrower's deposit balances have been sliding for two quarters, the tax return was filed on extension, the line has been fully drawn since spring, and the lender had stopped visiting.

The bank's best early-warning instrument is a person. A senior credit officer with thirty years in the market reads a set of returns and "knows" that the add-backs are hiding a cash squeeze, that this guarantor's other entities are borrowing from each other, that a dairy operator's milk contract is about to reprice, or that the borrower who stopped returning calls is negotiating with another bank. Ask them how, and they list a few rules of thumb that do not reproduce their judgement. When they retire, or the bank is sold and the acquirer's credit team takes over, the read goes with them.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the expert's read happens in conversation and margin notes. The credit memo records the conclusion and a paragraph of narrative; it rarely records the specific signal that made the officer uneasy, or the borrowers who worried them and turned out fine. Capturing it means sitting the officer in front of historical files and asking for a dated grade and the reason, or capturing their comments at loan committee and annual review as they happen, at the point where their time is most expensive.

The labelling problem has three layers. A single bank has few defaults: a $700M bank might charge off a handful of commercial relationships in a good year, so the outcome label is sparse and the bank must use migration to criticized or classified, past-due and modification events as intermediate labels. Experts disagree with themselves; the same officer grades the same file more harshly after a bad quarter, so repeated blind regrading of a sample is needed to estimate the ceiling. And the observed grades are censored by the process itself: loans the officer worried about were managed more closely, so a model trained naively learns that worried-about loans perform fine.

The deployment problem is speed and politics. A warning that arrives after the annual review is useless, and one that contradicts a lender with no explanation is ignored. Lenders resist a system that scores their grades; boards and examiners will accept a model only if the bank can explain why each flag fired and show that it was validated, which brings model risk management expectations to a bank with no model risk staff.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A grade ledger first and a model second. Every grade assignment and change is recorded with a short structured rationale, drafted automatically from the spread, the memo and the officer's comments and confirmed in seconds: the primary repayment source, the key risk, the trigger that would move the grade, and the officer's confidence. Each record is then joined to what happened: migration, past-due, modification, payoff, charge-off.

On top of the ledger, an early-warning model that combines behavioural signals the core already holds (deposit balance trends in the borrower's operating account, line utilisation, overdrafts, payment timing, NSF activity, returned ACH) with spread ratios, covenant tests and the language of memos and site-visit notes. Its outputs are a ranked watch list between annual reviews, with the specific signals behind each flag, and a comparison of each lender's grades against the model and against later outcomes. Senior credit officers annotate the flags, and those annotations become the training data that carries their read forward. Pooling across banks, through a vendor whose contracts allow it, solves the sparse-default problem that no single bank can.

## Impact If Solved
Moving the detection of a deteriorating commercial credit forward by even one or two quarters gives the bank time to restructure, take additional collateral or exit before the borrower's other lenders do, which on a community bank's commercial book is worth roughly ~10–25% of annual commercial charge-offs (estimate: earlier detection lifts recoveries on the minority of migrating credits where a restructure, added collateral or exit is still possible). It also changes the examination: a bank that can show every grade with its rationale and a validated early-warning record argues from evidence rather than defending a judgement it never wrote down, and keeps its senior credit officer's read when the officer leaves.
