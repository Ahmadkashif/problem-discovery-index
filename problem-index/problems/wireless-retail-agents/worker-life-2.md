# The Walk-In Who Isn't Buying

**Industry:** [[wireless-retail-agents|Wireless Retail Agents]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Many of the people who walk into a carrier-branded dealer store want help with a bill, a broken phone or a data transfer, and the rep who helps them earns nothing for it while the door counter scores each one as a failed sale.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #time-series-forecasting #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
To a customer, a store with a carrier's logo on the door is the carrier. They walk in to dispute a bill, ask why a promotional credit didn't post, return a phone, recover a password, transfer photos to a new device, fix a phone that won't connect, or pay a bill in cash. Authorised dealers are mostly paid on new activations, upgrades and attached features, not on service, so these visits earn the store little or nothing. Prepaid stores do earn a small fee on cash bill payments. The work still lands on the same consultants who are measured on sales, and traffic counters at the door count every visitor in the conversion rate. One 2024 report on T-Mobile third-party retailers described a 27% activation-conversion minimum on all store traffic at one chain.

The service visits are long and awkward. The rep often has to log into the carrier's care tools with limited permissions, wait on hold with carrier care alongside the customer, or explain that a store can't fix a billing system it doesn't control. The FCC's long-standing CPNI rules (47 CFR 64.2010) already let a store, as the carrier's agent, disclose account information in person only after the customer shows a valid photo ID matching the account, and carriers add their own passcodes and one-time codes. The FCC's November 2023 SIM-swap and port-out order (WC Docket 21-341) requires carriers to authenticate customers before a SIM change or port-out and to notify them immediately, but a July 2024 bureau order (DA 24-649) deferred compliance with all of its rules until OMB approval and a Federal Register compliance-date notice, and no such notice had appeared by October 2026. These are necessary controls, and they add steps to every service visit and make customers who can't authenticate angry with the rep.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The consultant is caught between a customer who needs help and a scorecard that punishes giving it. Every 40-minute billing dispute is 40 minutes off the sales floor, a lower conversion rate and a smaller paycheck. Turning customers away feels wrong, and it comes back as a bad survey score, which is also on the scorecard. The emotional labour of absorbing anger about decisions the carrier made is constant and unpaid. It's a large part of why experienced reps leave.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Triage at the door: a short intake on a tablet or QR code that sorts visits into sales, service the store can resolve, and issues only the carrier can fix. Customers in the last group are routed to carrier digital or phone care with the case already written up. For in-store service, a guided assistant that pulls the carrier's current policy, promotion and troubleshooting steps, drafts the explanation and logs the visit. Traffic-to-conversion metrics computed on sales-intent visitors only, with service time measured and credited. Staffing forecasts that account for service peaks such as bill-due dates and outage days.

## Impact If Solved
If service visits take ~30–50% of consultant floor time (estimate), giving back even a third of it means several hours per rep per week returned to paid work, and conversion metrics that measure selling rather than how many people needed help with a bill.
