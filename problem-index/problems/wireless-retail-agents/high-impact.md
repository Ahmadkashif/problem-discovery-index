# The Activation That Comes Back in 180 Days

**Industry:** [[wireless-retail-agents|Wireless Retail Agents]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A veteran wireless store manager can tell at the counter which new line will cancel, go unpaid or turn out to be fraud before the chargeback window closes, and the dealer pays for every one of those misreads months later without ever recording the read that would have caught them.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #logistic-regression #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact #worker-facing

## The Problem
An authorised dealer is paid when a line activates and paid for real only if the line survives. A new postpaid line with a financed phone might earn the store a few hundred dollars in activation commission, feature payments and spiffs (~$100–$400, estimate), all of it reversible. If the customer ports out after the promotion credit posts, stops paying, never makes the first recharge on a prepaid plan, or turns out to have opened the account with a stolen identity to walk out with three new iPhones, the carrier claws the commission back on a statement 30 to 180 days later. A fraud chargeback hurts twice, because the device is gone too. GameStop's Spring Mobile carried exactly this as an accounting line: commissions recognised "net of an allowance for chargebacks from AT&T for estimated customer cancellations". At a chain of hundreds of stores the clawbacks run to thousands of dollars per store per month (one 2024 report quotes Arch Telecom store sources saying so).

Experienced store and district managers say they can feel the bad ones. The customer porting in a number that was activated elsewhere two months ago. Four lines with maximum-financed flagships and no interest in cases or protection. An ID that's fine but a story that isn't. A rush at closing time. A trade-in that's a different model from the one on the account. A cash-paying prepaid customer who'll be back to recharge, against one who won't. At Cellular Sales, a Verizon authorised retailer, it was a regional director who noticed irregular phone transfers between two stores, and that led to charges that an employee had diverted 735 phones worth nearly $600K. This is tacit knowledge in the strict sense: pattern recognition built across thousands of activations and their later fates, which the manager can't write down as a rule and the new hire on a Saturday doesn't have.

Nobody connects the read to the outcome. The point-of-sale record knows who sold what and when. The carrier's chargeback file, months later, knows which lines came back and roughly why. The two are matched for money, if at all, never for learning. So the dealer pays for the same patterns repeatedly, and the rep who opened the bad line loses the commission without ever learning what the veteran saw.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection problem is that the expert's judgement is never captured at the moment it is made. A manager who hesitates over a deal, adds a verification step, steers the customer from four financed devices to two, or quietly declines a sale leaves no trace of why. To learn the read you have to record it in the flow of a busy counter: a two-second "how confident are you this sticks?" with optional reason chips, plus the deal's observable features. That is a behaviour change in a commission-driven culture, where admitting doubt can feel like costing yourself a sale.

The labelling problem is that the outcome is late, noisy and selected. The chargeback reason codes on carrier statements are coarse ("deactivation", "non-pay") and differ by carrier and month. A line that cancels at day 170 because the customer moved is a different event from fraud, yet both read as a chargeback. Lines the manager declined never get an outcome at all, so the data is biased toward deals somebody believed in. And experts disagree with themselves: the same manager reads a deal differently on a slow Tuesday and on launch day, and after a fraud loss they turn suspicious of everyone for a week. Inter-rater and test-retest agreement has to be measured before any model is held to the expert's standard.

The deployment problem is speed and legitimacy. The flag has to appear inside the activation flow before the device leaves the store, faster than the manager can walk over, or it changes nothing. It must not decide who gets service. Credit approval belongs to the carrier, and a model that proxies for protected characteristics invites discrimination claims, so the outputs have to be actions on the deal (a step-up identity check, a lower device count, a different promotion), not a verdict on the person. And the customer data involved is the carrier's CPNI, which the dealer agreement restricts.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An activation ledger first and a model second. Every new line, upgrade and add-a-line is recorded with the deal's features (port-in source and tenure, device count and financing, plan, promotion, trade-in match, payment method, time, rep tenure, store), the rep's and manager's quick confidence call when they make one, and, when it arrives, the carrier's commission and chargeback outcome joined back by line number and IMEI. Then a time-to-chargeback model trained on the dealer's own history, separated by cause (early cancellation, non-pay, fraud, never-recharged), surfaces a calibrated risk and the reason behind it at the counter, with the veteran managers' annotations as the expert benchmark it is measured against.

The output is a recommended action, not a refusal: run the carrier's step-up ID check, cap financed devices on this deal, offer the version of the promotion that doesn't pay out on day one, or call the manager over. For district managers the same ledger shows which stores, reps and promotions generate chargebacks, weeks before the statement arrives.

## Impact If Solved
If chargebacks run at ~5–15% of activation commission (estimate) on an industry receiving ~$20.4B, cutting them by a quarter at the counter is worth hundreds of millions of dollars a year to dealers, before counting the devices fraud rings walk out with. For the rep it means fewer clawed-back paychecks and a skill that once took years at the counter, available on the first day.
