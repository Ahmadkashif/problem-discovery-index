# The Fitment Call Only the Counter Pro Can Make

**Industry:** [[auto-parts-distributors|Auto Parts Distributors & Stores]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** The day after this is solved, a six-month counter hire picks the part that fits as reliably as the store's twenty-year counter pro when the catalog returns three candidates, and the store finally keeps a record of which choice stayed on the car.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #contrastive-learning #transformers #gradient-boosting #k-nearest-neighbors #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Every busy parts store has one or two counter professionals the rest of the staff lean on. A shop calls for a water pump on a 2012 crossover; the catalog shows two, split by an engine code that the VIN decode doesn't settle. A technician texts a photo of a brake hose with a bracket the listed part doesn't have. A service writer orders "rear shocks, the heavy-duty ones" for a van whose build sheet the store can't see. A walk-in has a starter with a casting number and no vehicle. The veteran resolves each in seconds. They know that this model year changed rotor size in March production, that the "with towing package" qualifier in the catalog is unreliable for this make, that this shop always wants the OE-supplier brand on European cars and the house brand on fleet vans, that the second listing for this sensor is a known catalog error that three shops returned last quarter, and that the photo's bracket means a different sub-model than the shop said.

None of this is written down. The point-of-sale system records the part number invoiced. It does not record the words the shop used, the candidates the catalog showed, the qualifier the counter pro overrode, or whether the part came back. When a return arrives, its reason code is "customer return" or "wrong part" at best, and it is rarely joined to the lookup that produced it. The catalog itself is assembled from hundreds of supplier files coded against the Auto Care Association's VCdb, and suppliers disagree: two brands' pads for the same car can carry different qualifiers, and a supplier's file can be months behind a production change. An estimated ~10–30% of lookups at a commercial counter return more than one plausible candidate (estimate), and those are the lines where wrong parts, second runs and lost shops come from.

The people who resolve them best are the hardest to replace. In the AAPEX 2026 survey, attracting skilled talent was among the top three challenges, named by 36% of aftermarket respondents and second only to uncertainty (45%), and the chains' own scale shows the stakes: O'Reilly employed 92,923 Team Members at the start of 2026, most of them in stores, and AutoZone ~132,000. A store that loses its veteran sees wrong-part returns rise and its best shop accounts test a competitor. Newer staff compensate by phoning the hub, the supplier's tech line or the veteran at another store, adding minutes to a call where the shop is waiting. It is tacit knowledge in the classic sense: pattern recognition across thousands of vehicles, brands and shops that the expert cannot fully specify, applied to requests that are partial, spoken and often wrong about the vehicle itself.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the resolution happens on the phone and at the counter, at the busiest part of the day. Learning from it means capturing the request as it arrived (the call or text, the VIN or the year-make-model the shop gave, any photo), the candidates the catalog returned, the one the counter pro chose and why, and then the outcome: kept, returned, re-ordered as a different part. Today the request side is not stored at all, and returns are coded too loosely to serve as labels. Building the dataset means instrumenting the lookup screen, the phone and text channels and the returns desk, then joining each lookup to its invoice line and to any return that followed.

The labelling challenge is that "correct" depends on context, and experts disagree with themselves. The same counter pro will send the house brand when the premium line is out of stock and refuse to on a fleet account. A return can mean the part didn't fit, the technician misdiagnosed, the shop ordered two and used one, or the customer declined the job. Catalog qualifiers are sometimes wrong in the source file, so the "catalog answer" cannot be the label either. Honest labels need return reasons captured at the desk with a short structured code, a sample of hard lookups reviewed by two or three senior counter pros to measure how often they agree, and fitment outcomes tracked by vehicle configuration rather than by part number alone.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. The suggestion must appear on the lookup screen faster than the counter pro could ask the person next to them, within a second or two of the VIN decode, and it must say when it is unsure. A confident wrong answer sends a second hot-shot run and costs the shop's goodwill. The chains already own their catalogs and will build or buy only what fits their point-of-sale systems; independents run Epicor, MAM or Autologue screens that a new tool has to sit beside. And suppliers resist any system that makes their catalog errors visible to rivals.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A fitment ledger first, a model second. Every lookup is stored with its raw input (VIN or stated vehicle, the shop's words, any photo), the decoded configuration from NHTSA's vPIC and the store's own build data, the candidate list, the part chosen and the outcome from the returns desk. Counter pros confirm or correct suggestions in one click, and returns are coded at the desk in three taps.

On top of the ledger, a ranking model learns which candidate fits given the configuration, the qualifiers, the shop's history and the store's return record for that application. It combines embeddings of part descriptions and qualifiers, the vehicle configuration, supplier-level error rates and the shop's brand preferences, and returns a ranked shortlist with the evidence for each (qualifier match, production split, shop history, past returns on this application) and a calibrated confidence that routes easy lines straight through and hard ones to a senior person. Corrections that recur become explicit rules ("this sensor's second listing is wrong for the 2.0L"), visible to every store, and flagged back to the supplier as catalog errors.

## Impact If Solved
If the fitment-return rate on multi-candidate lookups fell from ~8% to ~3% (estimate), a store writing ~150 commercial lines a day would avoid two to four wrong-part runs daily, each costing ~$15–$40 in driver time and margin (estimate), and keep the shop accounts those runs put at risk. The larger gain is the record itself: a store chain or programme group that holds lookup-to-return data can train a new counter hire to useful accuracy in weeks instead of years, push catalog corrections back to suppliers with evidence, and give every downstream automation, from text-order intake to assortment, a labelled base to learn from.
