# The Outside Rep Defending the Shop's First Call

**Industry:** [[auto-parts-distributors|Auto Parts Distributors & Stores]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The outside sales rep gets clarity: which of their shops is quietly moving its first call elsewhere, and why, before the monthly sales report tells them it already has.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #feature-engineering #worker-facing #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Every commercial parts business has outside reps who call on repair shops: O'Reilly alone lists ~825 full-time sales staff dedicated to professional customers. A rep covers ~60–150 shops (estimate), visiting the best of them weekly with donuts and a price sheet, settling disputes over a return or a delivery, enrolling shops in rebate and training programmes, and fielding the owner's complaint that a competitor's hub had the part an hour earlier. What the rep cannot see is the slow drift: a shop that used to buy all its brakes from this store and now buys only the parts the competitor didn't have. The monthly sales report shows the drop weeks after the shop decided, and the reason (a wrong part on a comeback job, a price matrix that changed, a new service writer who prefers another supplier's app) sits in counter notes, driver complaints and the rep's memory.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The rep's pay usually carries a commission or bonus on account growth, so a lost shop is personal income lost. Reps spend evenings rebuilding call lists by hand from sales reports, and their days reacting to the loudest shop rather than the one about to leave. They take the blame for losses caused by the counter, the catalog or the delivery schedule, without evidence to show what happened. When a rep leaves, the relationship history leaves too, and the successor starts by guessing.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A weekly account brief on the rep's phone: each shop's share of spend by category against its own past, the early signs of drift (fewer brake orders, more one-off orders of parts the store didn't stock, a spike in returns or late deliveries), and the likely cause drawn from returns, lookups that found no stock and delivery logs. It suggests the visit order and the conversation ("two wrong rotors on comeback jobs this month; offer the premium line at the house price"). The rep confirms or corrects the cause, which teaches the system.

## Impact If Solved
If a rep catches a drifting shop ~4–6 weeks earlier (estimate) and keeps even one extra account worth ~$40K a year in parts (estimate), the rep's bonus and the store's margin both hold, and the rep gets back the ~3–5 hours a week now spent building call lists.
