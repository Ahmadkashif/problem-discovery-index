# The Research Bet Nobody Grades

**Industry:** [[contract-research-labs|Contract Research Labs]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A contract research lab lives on a senior investigator's call that a topic is winnable and that a technical approach will take this many hours, every such bet is later resolved in the firm's own records, and nobody joins the two, so the judgement that decides the firm's revenue is neither measured nor kept when the investigator retires.

## The Problem
Every few weeks a solicitation opens — a DoD SBIR topic list, an NSF or DOE funding opportunity, a DARPA broad agency announcement, a corporate sponsor's request for a research proposal. The chief scientist or a senior principal investigator reads the topics and decides, mostly in minutes, which ones the firm should chase. For the chosen few, the same person writes the technical approach and a basis of estimate: so many hours of a senior engineer, so many of a technician, this much equipment time, milestones at month three, six and nine. The program manager or sponsor funds the promise; the firm then has to keep it at the price it named.

The calls that matter most are the ones experienced investigators "just know" and cannot fully explain. That a topic's wording means the program office already has a performer in mind. That this program manager wants hardware on a bench at month six, not a simulation. That an approach which looks like four months on paper is really eight, because the material will not behave until the third synthesis route. That a firm-fixed-price Phase I is safe only if the hard part is already half-solved in-house. A junior PI cannot write these patterns down and usually learns them by losing proposals and overrunning tasks.

Every one of these bets resolves in records the firm already holds. The award is won or lost, often with a debrief. The hours actually charged sit in the timekeeping system against each task. The milestone report says whether the result was achieved. Whether the sponsor funded a Phase II, an option or a follow-on is in the contract file. These records live in a proposal folder, Deltek Costpoint or QuickBooks, a shared drive of reports and a contracts spreadsheet, and nobody joins them. At DoD only 18% of Phase I proposals were selected in FY2022 and 28% of Phase II (National Academies); a firm submitting dozens of proposals a year cannot say which of its PIs' bets carry information.

Two clocks make this sharper. From FY2027 each agency will cap the number of SBIR proposals a firm may submit (P.L. 119-83), so a firm that won by volume must now choose. And the senior investigators who carry the pattern recognition are the oldest people in most labs; when one retires, the firm keeps the contract history and loses the judgement that produced it.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the investigator's reasoning has to be captured at the moment of the bet, by the investigator, in the week before a solicitation closes — the worst possible week to ask for anything. A bid decision is usually a hallway conversation; a basis of estimate is a spreadsheet with numbers and no reasons. Hours are recorded, but by contract line rather than by the technical task the estimate described, so the comparison between "we said 400 hours for the synthesis" and "it took 900" has to be rebuilt by hand.

The labelling problem is that outcomes are noisy labels for the quality of the call. A good proposal loses because the topic was written for an incumbent or the budget was cut; a weak one wins in a thin field. An overrun may reflect a bad estimate or a sponsor who changed the scope. And experts disagree with themselves: the same PI estimates the same kind of task differently after a recent overrun, and two senior staff will rate a topic's winnability differently. Selection compounds it — the firm only observes the outcome of the topics it chose to bid.

The deployment problem is cultural and temporal. Senior investigators' standing rests on their judgement and many will resist a system that scores it; juniors fear that a recorded estimate is a recorded mistake. Any assistance has to arrive inside the hour a PI spends triaging a topic list, and be faster than asking the chief scientist down the hall, or it will be ignored. Defense work adds a constraint: much of the technical content is export-controlled or controlled unclassified information, so the tooling has to run inside the firm's own enclave.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A bet ledger first and a model second. Every bid decision, proposal and basis of estimate becomes a short structured record — topic, sponsor, technical approach, key risks, estimated hours by task, stated milestone and the PI's confidence — drafted automatically from the proposal itself and confirmed in seconds. Each record is later joined to its outcomes: award or loss and debrief notes, hours charged by task, milestone met or slipped, and follow-on funding.

On top of the ledger, learn the experts' reads. Models trained on the firm's own history estimate the realistic effort for a described task and the chance a milestone is met on time, and score new topics against the firm's track record with that sponsor and that kind of work. Retrieval surfaces the closest past bets with their outcomes ("the last three times we proposed a sol-gel coating to this office, two overran by half"). The goal is not to replace the chief scientist's read but to make it available, with its record, to the PI writing the next proposal.

## Impact If Solved
A firm that knows which topics it actually converts and how far its estimates run can spend a capped proposal allowance where it wins and price fixed-price work where it survives; for a 50-person SBIR firm, cutting losing proposals by a third returns several hundred hours of senior time a year (estimate), and halving the worst overruns protects margins that are rarely above single digits on research work (estimate). Keeping a retiring chief scientist's calibrated judgement turns the industry's most expensive recurring loss into something the firm owns.
