# The Part Only the Veteran Can Name

**Industry:** [[industrial-distributors|Industrial Distributors]]
**Type:** High Impact

## The Problem
Every industrial distributor has two or three people per branch whom everyone else walks over to. A maintenance planner emails "need 4 of the bearings for the Baldor on the chiller pump, same as last time." A technician brings a seized bearing to the counter with the markings rubbed off. A buyer sends a PO line reading "6205LLU C3 NTN or equal." A plant calls about a gearbox whose manufacturer was acquired twice and whose catalogue number no longer exists. The veteran resolves each one in seconds: they measure the bore and outside diameter, recognise the seal type by eye, know that "LLU" is NTN's contact seal and that this plant always takes the SKF equivalent with C3 clearance, recall that the old gearbox model was superseded by a specific ratio and frame, and know which substitute the plant's maintenance manager rejected last year and why.

None of this is written down. The ERP records the SKU that was finally invoiced. It does not record the words the customer used, the photo they sent, the three candidates the rep considered, the interchange they rejected, or whether the part fitted when it arrived. Manufacturer cross-reference tables exist, but they cover a fraction of real requests and none of the plant-specific preferences: this customer's internal stock code, this line's tolerance for a different seal, this buyer's habit of writing the motor's part number when he means the bearing inside it. An estimated ~20–40% of inbound lines at a typical MRO distributor need this kind of identification work before they can be priced (estimate), and on a breakdown call the clock runs while it happens.

The people who do it best are the ones closest to retirement, and their knowledge was built over decades of returns, wrong parts and angry phone calls. When a veteran leaves, the branch's identification speed drops, wrong-part returns rise, and some accounts drift to a competitor whose counter still has someone who knows. Newer reps compensate by calling the veteran at another branch, the manufacturer's inside sales line or a supplier's application engineer, adding hours to work that used to take seconds. It is tacit knowledge in the classic sense: pattern recognition across thousands of physical parts, brands and customers that the expert cannot fully specify, applied to inputs that are messy, partial and often physical.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's reasoning happens in their head, on the phone and at the counter, during the busiest part of their day. To learn from it, a system must capture the request as it arrived — the email text, the photo, the caliper measurements, the customer's own code — alongside the candidates considered and the final choice, without asking the veteran to fill in a form. Historical data mostly lacks the input side: the ERP holds the sale, not the question, and emails are not linked to order lines. Building the request-to-resolution dataset means instrumenting the inbox, the counter and the phone, then joining each request to the order line it produced and to any return that followed.

The labelling challenge is that "correct" has layers, and experts disagree with themselves. A part can be a technically valid interchange and still be wrong for this plant; the same veteran will accept a substitute on Tuesday when the preferred brand is out of stock and refuse it on Friday. Returns are noisy labels: a part comes back because it was wrong, because the customer over-ordered, or because the machine was scrapped. Bearing and belt markings vary by manufacturer and era, nameplates are worn, and photos are taken with a phone in a dark plant. Honest labels need a structured review of a sample of resolutions by more than one expert, an estimate of how often the experts agree, and outcome tracking for fit and returns.

The deployment challenge is speed. The tool must give the rep a ranked, explained answer faster than walking over to the veteran — a few seconds on an email line, under a minute on a photo at the counter — and it must say when it is unsure. A confident wrong answer on a breakdown call costs the account. Distributors also hesitate to put their best people's knowledge into a vendor's model, and manufacturers resist making their parts easier to replace.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A resolution ledger first and a model second. Every inbound line — email, PO, phone note, counter photo — is captured with its raw input and linked to the candidates shown, the item sold and the outcome: shipped, returned, reordered. Veterans confirm or correct the system's suggestions in one click, which turns their daily work into labels without extra effort.

On top of the ledger, a retrieval and ranking model learns to map messy inputs to the distributor's catalogue and to approved interchanges. It combines text embeddings over descriptions and part numbers, image features from photos of parts and nameplates, measured dimensions, and the customer's own history and preferences. It returns a ranked shortlist with the evidence for each candidate (dimension match, seal type, prior purchases by this plant, interchange source) and a calibrated confidence that routes easy lines straight to pricing and hard ones to a senior person. Plant-specific rules learned from corrections — "this site never takes a shielded bearing on the washdown line" — become explicit, searchable knowledge rather than one person's memory.

## Impact If Solved
If identification time on hard lines falls from ~10–30 minutes to under 2 (estimate), a rep handling ~100 lines a day recovers several hours a week, and wrong-part returns fall with it. The larger gain is retention of the trade's core skill. A distributor that keeps its veterans' judgement in a searchable, graded form can train a new rep to full productivity in months instead of years, protect breakdown accounts through retirements, and build the first request-to-resolution dataset in the industry — the asset that makes every downstream automation, from quoting to order entry, more accurate.
