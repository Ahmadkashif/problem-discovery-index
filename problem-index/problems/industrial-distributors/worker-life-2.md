# The Hot-Shot Run Across Town

**Industry:** [[industrial-distributors|Industrial Distributors]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The branch's hot-shot driver gets a day that is planned instead of shouted across the warehouse, with runs batched, the plant's drop point known, and no more waiting at a gate for someone to sign.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #time-series-forecasting #optimization-fundamentals #dynamic-programming #worker-facing #workflow-orchestration #automation

## The Problem
Besides scheduled route trucks, most industrial branches keep one or two drivers for "hot-shot" runs: a single urgent part to a plant whose line is down, a transfer from another branch, a pickup from a supplier's warehouse across the metro. The driver is dispatched by whoever answers the phone. A run comes in, they leave; twenty minutes later another plant two miles from the first calls, and the driver is sent back out after returning. At the plant, the driver hunts for the right gate and the right building, then waits for the maintenance technician who ordered the part to come and sign.

Runs cluster around the morning and the start of each shift, and late-day calls stretch the day past closing. There is little information in advance: the driver often learns the destination from a sticky note, and the inside rep who promised "within the hour" has no idea where the driver is.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
Hot-shot drivers are paid hourly and work to other people's promises. Every urgent run is someone else's emergency, and the driver absorbs the pressure of a plant losing money while sitting in traffic. Wasted trips, waits at security gates and calls asking "where are you?" make a long day feel worse, and late runs land on family time with little notice.

It is also a dead-end role in many branches. Drivers who learn the plants, the gates and the people are some of the branch's best sources of customer knowledge, but none of it is captured or valued.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A dispatch board that every inside rep and counter person books urgent runs into, instead of shouting. It groups runs that can share a trip, predicts each run's travel time and sets honest promises the rep can give the customer, and holds a note per plant on the right gate, dock, building and contact. The customer gets an arrival time and a live link; the technician gets a text when the driver arrives; proof of delivery is a photo, not a wait for a signature. The board also predicts when the next cluster of calls is likely, so the driver can pre-stage parts or plan a lunch.

## Impact If Solved
If batching and better promises cut ~15–25% of hot-shot trips and ~20–40 minutes of waiting a day (estimate), a driver gets back ~1–1.5 hours a day and finishes closer to closing time, while customers get a part when it was promised rather than when the driver happened to be free.
