# The Junior Designer Counting Shrubs at Midnight

**Industry:** [[landscape-architecture-firms|Landscape Architecture Firms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** A landscape designer with a professional degree spends the years before licensure counting plants, renumbering details and picking up redlines the night before every deadline, and gets back the design time and the study time the licence requires.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #transformers #object-detection #feature-engineering #worker-facing #automation #workflow-orchestration

## The Problem
A designer two or three years out of an accredited landscape architecture programme is the person who produces the construction document set. The principal and project landscape architect make the design decisions; the junior turns them into thirty or forty sheets: layout and dimension plans, materials plans, planting plans with labels on every symbol, plant schedules with quantities, irrigation plans, and detail sheets cross-referenced by number. Every design change ripples through all of it. Moving a walkway shifts a planting bed, which changes shrub counts, which changes the schedule and the estimate; deleting a detail renumbers every callout that pointed at it.

The work peaks before every milestone — schematic, design development, 50%, 90%, permit, bid — and the redlines arrive late because the principal reviews last. The junior picks them up overnight, re-runs the plant schedule, checks that labels match the schedule and that the estimate matches the schedule, and hopes nothing was missed. Land F/X and Vectorworks automate parts of this, but the checking — does every callout point at the right detail, does every plant on the plan appear in the schedule with the right size, does the spec section agree — is still done by eye.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
These designers took a five-year degree to design landscapes and spend most of their week on coordination. Pay is modest for a licensed profession — BLS puts the median for landscape architects at $79,870 in May 2025, and unlicensed designers earn less — and the late nights are unpaid for salaried staff. The licence requires passing four LARE sections, including grading and drainage with a 50% pass rate, and study time is what the deadline weeks consume first. Many leave for public agencies, the design-build side of [[landscaping|Landscaping]] or other fields before they sit the exam.

Mistakes are visible and land on them. A wrong count becomes a contractor's RFI with the junior's initials in the title block; a mislabelled plant becomes a substitution argument in construction.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A coordination assistant that runs continuously on the drawing set: reconciles every plant symbol against the schedule and the specification, checks every detail callout and sheet reference, flags labels that no longer match after a change, recomputes quantities and the cost estimate on each save, and turns the principal's PDF redlines into a checklist tied to the exact objects to change. The designer still makes the decisions about how the drawing communicates; the tool does the counting and cross-checking and shows what changed since the last issue.

## Impact If Solved
Taking the coordination half out of document production gives each junior designer back several evenings per milestone — on the order of a hundred hours a year (estimate) — moves that time to design and exam preparation, and removes the class of errors that put their name on an RFI.
