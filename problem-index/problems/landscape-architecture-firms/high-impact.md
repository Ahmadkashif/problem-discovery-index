# The Grading Plan Only a Principal Can Read

**Industry:** [[landscape-architecture-firms|Landscape Architecture Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A senior landscape architect can scan a grading plan and see where water will pond, where a walkway will fail an accessibility survey and where the spot elevations will not tie — and that review, the firm's main defence against its most expensive errors, is never recorded, never graded and leaves when the principal retires.

## The Problem
On every site project a landscape architect produces a grading and drainage plan: proposed contours and spot elevations across plazas, walks, lawns, planting beds and parking islands, tied to the building's finished floor elevation, the civil engineer's inlets and pipe inverts, the existing grade at the property line and the outlet of every bioretention cell. A junior designer or a mid-level project landscape architect draws it, usually in AutoCAD or Civil 3D with Land F/X, against a topographic survey that may be a year old. Before the set goes to permit or bid, a principal or senior reviewer prints it or opens it in Bluebeam and marks it up.

What that reviewer does is hard to describe and easy to recognise. They trace the high points and see that a plaza drains toward the entry doors. They notice that a curb ramp lands at a 3% cross-slope where the accessibility standard allows about 2%. They see a planting bed whose finished grade sits above the adjacent walk, so mulch will wash across it in the first storm. They spot that a bioretention cell's overflow is higher than the inlet that feeds it, so the cell will never reach its design ponding depth. They know, from having been called back to sites, that a 1% lawn swale in clay soil will stay wet all spring. Few of these checks are written into a firm's QA checklist, and the ones that are written ("verify ADA slopes") say nothing about how to find the failure.

The licensing exam reflects the difficulty. CLARB's Grading, Drainage and Stormwater Management section had a 50% national pass rate in Spring 2026, against 80% for construction documentation and administration — the lowest of the four LARE sections across the last three administrations. The errors a review misses surface later and at a higher price: RFIs and field changes when the contractor's surveyor finds elevations that do not tie, change orders for added drains, ponding at entrances discovered at the first rain after occupancy, accessibility complaints against an owner, and professional liability claims for water intrusion and trip-and-fall incidents. Meanwhile the principals who carry this pattern recognition are ageing out; ASLA's 2024 industry report found one in four firms going through an ownership transition.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's judgement exists only in markups made under deadline. Red-pen comments live as PDF annotations, scanned prints or verbal instructions to a junior; they are rarely tied to the CAD geometry they refer to, and the version of the plan the principal reviewed is often overwritten. Capturing the expert at work means recording the markup against the surface it critiques — which part of the TIN, which spot elevation, which slope — without adding seconds to a review that is already squeezed into the night before submission.

The labelling challenge is that the review and the outcome disagree in both directions. A principal flags a slope that would have been fine; a plan passes review and ponds anyway because the contractor regraded in the field or the survey was wrong. Experts also disagree with themselves: the same reviewer is stricter on a public project with a tough inspector than on a private one, and stricter after a recent claim. Honest labels need two sources — the markups, and downstream evidence such as RFIs, change orders, as-built surveys and punch lists — joined to the specific location on the plan, and that join is the expensive part.

The deployment challenge is speed and trust. A checker that flags 400 cells of a slope raster as "non-compliant" is ignored within a week; one that misses the curb ramp the principal would have caught is never opened again. The tool has to sit inside the drawing software, run in seconds, rank its findings the way a senior reviewer would, and explain each one in the reviewer's vocabulary ("ponds at door", "ramp cross-slope", "bed above walk").

## What a Solution Looks Like
Start with capture. A review layer inside the CAD or PDF markup tool that records each redline against the surface model at the moment it is made, with a short tag from a firm vocabulary, and links later RFIs, change orders, as-built survey points and punch-list photos back to the same coordinates. Within a year a firm has a dataset that does not exist anywhere today: grading surfaces, what its best reviewers flagged on them, and what actually went wrong.

On top of that, a model that treats the proposed surface as an image and a graph — elevation and slope rasters, flow accumulation, the network of high points, low points, inlets and outlets, plus ADA route geometry — and predicts where a senior reviewer would put a red mark and why. Deterministic checks (slope limits, positive drainage away from thresholds, tie-in mismatches) run first; the learned layer ranks them and adds the patterns no rule captures, such as flat swales in poorly draining soil or bed grades that will shed mulch.

## Impact If Solved
A firm whose junior designers get a principal-quality first review in minutes cuts the review cycle before every submission, catches the grading errors that become RFIs and change orders on a large share of site projects, and reduces its exposure to the drainage and accessibility claims that dominate design liability for site work. For an industry with ~23,500 landscape architects and a wave of ownership transitions, keeping a retiring principal's red pen inside the firm is worth more than any drafting speed-up.
