# The Park Designer After the Community Meeting

**Industry:** [[landscape-architecture-firms|Landscape Architecture Firms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The landscape architect leading a public park plan runs evening meetings, then spends nights sorting hundreds of comment cards, dot-voting boards and survey responses into a summary the council will accept — and gets back the evenings and a defensible read of what the public actually asked for.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #bert #transformers #k-means-clustering #dbscan #worker-facing #automation

## The Problem
Public park and open-space projects are planned in public. A city hires a landscape architecture firm to produce a park master plan or a renovation design, and the scope requires engagement: two or three community meetings, pop-up events at farmers' markets, an online survey, a map where residents drop pins, comment cards in two or three languages, and stakeholder interviews with sports leagues, neighbours and advocacy groups. The meetings are on weeknight evenings and Saturdays because that is when residents come.

After each round the project landscape architect, often with one junior designer, has to turn the material into findings: which amenities are wanted, where people disagree, what the neighbours near the proposed pickleball courts said, what the Spanish-language cards said, and how all of it maps to the design options. Responses arrive as photos of sticky notes and dot boards, scanned cards, survey exports and map pins. The summary goes into a staff report on the council's deadline, and every claim in it ("residents strongly prefer a natural playground") may be challenged in public comment.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
The engagement weeks stack evening meetings on top of a normal design workload, and the synthesis is done afterwards, usually at home. The work matters to the designer personally: they are the person standing at the front of the room, and when the plan comes back to council, a resident who feels unheard will say so on the record. A summary built from a hurried skim of 900 comments is a professional risk, and the designer knows it.

It is also lonely judgement. Coding open-ended comments consistently, deciding what counts as a theme and keeping minority-language and quieter voices visible are research skills most landscape architects were never trained in.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An engagement workspace that ingests every channel — transcribed cards and boards, survey exports, map pins, meeting notes, translated comments — and codes them into themes the designer defines and edits, with every theme linked to its source comments and every map comment clustered by location. The designer reviews and adjusts the coding rather than doing it from scratch, sees where groups disagree and where a theme comes from a handful of very active voices, and exports the summary with citations to the raw comments for the staff report.

## Impact If Solved
Turning a week of evening synthesis per engagement round into a day of review gives the lead designer back roughly two to four weeks a year on a park-heavy workload (estimate), and gives the city a summary whose claims can be traced to what residents actually wrote.
