# The Income Call Nobody Grades

**Industry:** [[mortgage-lenders|Mortgage Lenders]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A senior underwriter decides, file by file, whether a borrower's documented income will continue, and the lender that later pays for every wrong call in QC findings and buybacks never joins those outcomes back to the judgement that produced them.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #large-language-models #survival-analysis #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #compliance #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Automated underwriting has taken the credit decision away from the underwriter. Desktop Underwriter or Loan Product Advisor returns Approve/Eligible on most conforming files once the data is keyed. What the engine cannot decide is whether the data is true and durable, and that part is still the underwriter's job. Is the qualifying income $9,400 a month or $7,100? Is the overtime likely to continue? Is the Schedule C decline a one-off or the start of a slide? Is a large deposit a gift, a loan or a sign the borrower is not who the file says? Fannie Mae's Selling Guide requires the lender to analyse "the stability of the borrower's income", to measure year-to-year trends in gross income, expenses and taxable income, and to judge "the ability of the business to continue generating and distributing sufficient income". It sets factors, not a threshold. As one lender's guide puts it, there is no fixed rule on how much decline is acceptable; it is up to the judgement of the approving underwriter.

Experienced underwriters make this call quickly and cannot fully say how. They notice that a contractor's 1099 income spikes in the year before an application. They see that a borrower's bank deposits do not match the business revenue on the return. They know that a particular payroll provider's stubs are a favourite of fabricators, and that a self-employed borrower whose expense ratio jumps should get a third year of returns. A junior underwriter with the same file applies the worksheet, gets the same Form 1084 answer, and clears it. The difference shows up months later.

It shows up in QC findings, agency defect notices and buybacks. Income and employment were the largest category of critical defects in ACES's QC data, 27.24% of them in Q3 2025. A Reggora and STRATMOR study found repurchases cost lenders about $32,288 each, with appraisal and income issues together behind 57% of requests. Each of those outcomes traces to an underwriter who signed off on a figure. But QC sits in a different department, the defect notice arrives a year later through the agency portal, and nobody asks which underwriter, which income type, which doubt was raised and waved through. When the lender cuts underwriters after a refinance wave, it decides who goes on file counts, not on calls.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the underwriter's doubt is rarely written down in a usable form. The origination system stores the final qualifying income and the conditions issued, not the alternatives considered. An underwriter who suspected the deposits but cleared the file after a letter of explanation leaves no trace that they hesitated. Capturing the expert at work means recording their reasoning at the moment of review, on files where they are measured by turn time and have every reason not to slow down.

The labelling problem is that the outcome is a noisy, delayed and selected label. Most loans with a generous income call perform anyway. A QC "defect" partly reflects the QC reviewer's own judgement, and experienced underwriters disagree with QC and with themselves: the same underwriter treats a declining Schedule C differently in a boom, under turn-time pressure, than in a slow month. Repurchase demands are rare (Urban Institute measured 0.24% of 2018–23 GSE originations) and are themselves filtered by which loans the agency happened to sample or which defaulted. Loans that were declined or withdrawn never produce an outcome at all.

The deployment problem is speed and authority. An underwriter clearing three or four files a day (estimate) will ignore any second opinion that arrives after they have issued conditions, and will resent one that grades them. A model that flags half of all self-employed files is useless; it must point at the specific line on the specific return and say why, faster than the underwriter's own scan. And it must not become a new defect source: a flag that was raised and ignored is discoverable evidence in a repurchase dispute.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An income ledger first and a model second. For every file, record the underwriter's decision as structure rather than prose: each income source, the documents relied on, the calculation method, the alternatives considered, the specific doubts and the condition used to resolve each. Draft it automatically from the worksheet, the conditions and the underwriter's notes, and have them confirm it in seconds. Then join every record to what happened: prefunding and post-closing QC findings, agency defect notices and their resolution, early payment defaults, 12-month delinquency, and buybacks.

On top of the ledger, learn the senior underwriters' reads. Pair their historical files and their flags with the outcomes, and build models that surface those patterns to the next underwriter as a pointed second read: this deposit pattern, this payroll provider, this trend in expense ratio, here is how files like it performed and how your best underwriters treated them. Use the same record in reverse for repurchase defence, where an income decision made with documented reasoning is far easier to defend than a bare number.

## Impact If Solved
A lender that can say which income types, documents and underwriters carry defect risk can target QC samples, coach juniors on evidence, and set staffing on calls rather than counts. At the study's 0.49% buyback rate, a lender selling 20,000 loans a year faces ~100 repurchase requests, perhaps a third of them income-driven (estimate); cutting those by a third avoids roughly ten buybacks a year at ~$32,000 each, plus the investor pricing penalties, QC remediation and agency scrutiny that follow a rising defect rate. And the judgement of the underwriters who survive each layoff cycle stops walking out of the door with the ones who do not.
