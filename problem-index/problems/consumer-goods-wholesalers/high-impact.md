# The Container Ordered Before the Item Has Sold Once

**Industry:** [[consumer-goods-wholesalers|Consumer Goods Wholesalers]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An importer commits to a factory minimum months before an item reaches a shelf, on a merchant's feel for what will sell, and never records that judgement in a form that can be checked against what the item later sold, cost in markdowns, or recovered as closeout.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #contrastive-learning #gradient-boosting #transformers #time-series-forecasting #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
A merchant at a housewares, toy, sporting goods or gift importer spends weeks a year in factory showrooms and at Asian sourcing fairs looking at samples. For each one she decides whether to take it, at what price point, in which colours, for which accounts and how many. The factory sets a minimum, often one to five thousand units per item or colourway (estimate), and the order goes in four to nine months before the goods reach a shelf (estimate). Retailer commitments at that point are soft. A buyer liked the sample at a line review, a programme letter names a quantity the retailer can still cut, and the independent stores have not seen the item yet. The whole buy depends on her read.

Experienced merchants say they "just know". This price point clears at a discount chain but stalls at a gift shop. That colourway will sell and the other will not. A licensed character has peaked. An item is too close to last year's to earn a new slot. A factory's sample will not match its production run. A junior buyer with the same spreadsheet cannot do it. The judgement is pattern recognition built over hundreds of items and dozens of seasons, the same kind of tacit knowledge a craftsman has about materials.

The outcome of every bet is eventually recorded, but in pieces that no one joins. Sell-through sits in each retailer's portal. Markdown money and seasonal return claims arrive months later as deductions on remittances. Unsold units go to a closeout buyer at a fraction of cost on a separate ledger. Underbought items simply sell out, and the lost sales are never measured, because re-buying from Asia takes longer than the season lasts. When a senior merchant retires or leaves for a competitor, the company keeps its item master and loses the judgement that filled it.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the reasoning has to be captured at the moment of decision, in a showroom, during the busiest weeks of the merchant's year. Item attributes sit in spec sheets and factory quotes, images on phones, and the merchant's intent (which account, what retail price, how confident) in her head or an email. The items she passed on are never recorded at all, so there is no record of the bets not taken. Outcome data is scattered across retailer portals furnished under each retailer's supplier terms, EDI remittances and the closeout ledger, and joining them to the original item takes item-number mapping most importers have never done.

The labelling problem is that sell-through is a noisy grade for a buying decision. A good item fails when the retailer puts it in the wrong place, prices it high or cuts the order. A weak item succeeds because a competitor's shipment was stuck at port. The honest label conditions on distribution: rate of sale per store per week where the item was actually on shelf, net of markdown money and recovery. Even then experts disagree with themselves. The same merchant rates the same sample differently after a hot season or a bad one, and two merchants pick different colours from the same rack. The data is selected too. Only bought items have outcomes, and deep buys get more distribution than shallow ones.

The deployment problem is speed and pride. Help has to arrive on a phone in the minutes of a factory meeting, faster than the merchant can recall the comparable from three seasons ago, or it will be ignored. Merchants are paid for their eye and are wary of a system that scores it. The first version has to make them look good, by surfacing their own past wins and the comparables they half-remember, before it tells them anything about their misses.

## What a Solution Looks Like
An item ledger first and a model second. Every sample the merchant considers is recorded in a few taps: photo, factory quote, minimum, target retail, intended accounts, a buy-depth range and a conviction score, with spec sheets and quotes parsed automatically. Every bought item is later graded against its realised outcome: rate of sale where distributed, markdown money and returns claimed by retailers, closeout recovery, and stockouts that point to an underbuy.

On top of the ledger, learn the merchant's read. Embed item images and attributes with a contrastive model trained so that items which sold alike sit close together. For a new sample, retrieve its nearest comparables from the company's own history and their outcomes by channel. A gradient-boosted quantile model then predicts 26-week sell-through by channel from the item's features, the comparables, price-point position, account intent and early signals (show orders, Faire orders, a retailer's test-store results), and translates the forecast into a recommended buy range at the factory minimum. The merchant sees the comparables, the range and how her own past calls on similar items turned out, then makes the decision herself.

## Impact If Solved
For an importer selling ~$100M a year with ~$15M of new items (estimate), cutting the overbuys that end in closeout by a quarter and the markdown money on seasonal items by a fifth is worth ~$1–2M of gross margin a year (estimate), more than the company's whole analytics budget. Keeping a retiring merchant's graded record of hundreds of buys turns the industry's most expensive recurring loss, the eye that walks out of the door, into an asset the company keeps.
