# The Disabled Tester Whose Expertise Is Priced as Participation

**Industry:** [[digital-accessibility-firms|Digital Accessibility Firms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** The people who can actually say whether a site works are disabled users with years of assistive technology expertise, and the industry frequently engages them as research participants rather than as specialists.
**Tags:** #evaluation-metrics #large-language-models #gradient-boosting #confidence-intervals #hypothesis-testing #worker-facing #compliance #tacit-knowledge-ml

## The Problem
Testing with real assistive technology users is the highest-value evidence in accessibility work and the least systematised. A firm recruits screen reader users, switch access users, voice control users and others to attempt flows and report what happens, and the engagement is often structured as participant recruitment — a session fee, an hour of time, treated as research participation.

The mismatch is that these are frequently expert practitioners. Someone who has used a screen reader daily for fifteen years knows the software's behaviour, the conventions of well-built sites, and precisely why a given pattern fails, at a level that takes a sighted auditor years to approach. Paid at participant rates, briefed like a research subject, and asked to describe their experience rather than to render a professional judgement, that expertise is systematically underused and underpaid.

The practical consequences follow. Findings from these sessions are recorded as user quotes rather than as specialist findings, which makes them easier for a client to discount. The testers rarely see whether anything was fixed. And the work is intermittent and uncontracted, so people with genuinely scarce expertise cannot build a stable practice on it.

Employment in the field is better than it was — several firms employ disabled specialists directly and prominently — and a substantial share of the testing volume still runs through participant recruitment at participant economics.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is an underpayment and misclassification of expertise, and it lands on a population with documented employment disadvantage. Disabled workers face persistently lower employment rates, and an industry built on accessibility engaging their expertise at participant rates is an uncomfortable arrangement that people in it describe openly.

There is a professional development cost too. Work structured as a session provides no progression, no title, no portfolio and no route into the specialist roles that pay properly, so the expertise stays informal even where it exceeds that of the certified auditors reviewing it.

And there is the labour of the sessions themselves. Repeatedly attempting flows that are broken, documenting failures that are frustrating to encounter, and doing it for an hour at a time is genuinely tiring in a way that is not usually acknowledged in the session design or the rate.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Structure the role as specialist work. Contracted, titled, paid at specialist rates, with findings recorded as professional judgements rather than as participant quotes. Several firms do this and it should be the industry's default, not its distinguishing feature.

Capture the judgement, not just the experience. A structured finding — the pattern, the technology, the failure mode, the severity, the recommendation — from the person with the deepest knowledge of the technology is more valuable than a narrative, and the tooling should support producing it quickly, including through drafting assistance so the documentation burden does not fall hardest on the people already doing the hardest part.

Close the loop for the tester. Telling someone what was fixed as a result of their finding is basic professional respect, is almost never done, and would also produce the outcome evidence this whole industry lacks.

Build the panel as a durable practice. Regular, contracted, retained relationships rather than per-session recruitment give testers stable income and give firms accumulated familiarity with a codebase — both of which produce better work.

And design the sessions for the person. Session length, breaks, and the choice of what to test should reflect that encountering broken interfaces repeatedly is taxing.

## Impact If Solved
The people who can most reliably answer whether a site works are engaged by this industry at the lowest tier of its economics, which is both an equity problem and a quality problem. Structuring the role as specialist work with proper rates, structured findings, outcome feedback and durable engagement improves the evidence the whole field rests on and creates skilled employment in a population the industry exists to serve.
