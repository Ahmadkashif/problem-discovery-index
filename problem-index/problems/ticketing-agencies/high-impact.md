# The House Scaled by Feel

**Industry:** [[ticketing-agencies|Ticketing & Reservation Agencies]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** Every seat in a major onsale is priced, held and released on an experienced ticketing director's read of demand, the market grades that read within hours, and nobody records the read, so the best judgement in the building is never measured and leaves when the person does.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #causal-inference #bayesian-inference #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering #revenue-impact

## The Problem
Weeks before a tour goes on sale, a ticketing director at the primary ticketing company sits with the promoter's representative and the venue's box office manager and scales the house. They divide a 15,000-seat arena into price levels: floor, lower bowl sides, lower bowl ends, upper bowl. They mark the seats to hold back for the artist, the promoter, production kills, sponsors, fan-club and card presales, and the platinum or dynamically priced inventory. Then they set the release plan. That is a judgement built from the artist's last tour, presale registration counts, streaming in the market, the day of the week, the competing shows that month and, above all, memory: this market always buys the lower-bowl ends late, this artist's fans will not pay above a certain number for the upper bowl, this city's floor goes to brokers in the first ninety seconds.

On onsale morning the same people watch the queue. Within minutes they decide whether to open the holds, move a section to a higher price level, switch sections to dynamic pricing, cap tickets per account, or call the promoter to add a second show before the brokers' listings set the market. These are the most valuable decisions in the industry, and they are made by perhaps a few hundred people across Ticketmaster, AXS, SeatGeek, the large venues and the promoters (estimate).

The outcome is recorded better than in almost any other business. Every seat's sale time, price and channel is logged. For every seat that resells, StubHub, Vivid Seats and SeatGeek show within hours what the market would pay, and that spread goes to the broker. Seats priced too high sit unsold and are discounted the week of the show. The decision itself, why the ends were priced where they were, why the holds were released at 10:20, is kept in an email thread and the director's head. When the director moves to a rival or a promoter, which happens often in a small industry, the read goes too.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection challenge is that the expert's decision has to be captured at the moment it is made, and the moment is an onsale where minutes matter. A scaling sheet records the final price levels but not the alternatives considered, the confidence, or the signal that drove the call. A hold release appears in the system log as an inventory change with no reason attached. Capturing the reasoning means instrumenting the scaling session and the onsale war room without slowing either down, and the people in the room are running the most stressful hour of their month.

The labelling challenge is that the obvious label is biased and the expert disagrees with themselves. Resale prices measure the value of the seats brokers chose to buy and list, not the seats in general. Speculative listings, fee changes, the all-in pricing rule in force since May 2025 and new state caps such as Vermont's 110% limit all move the resale price without any change in fan demand. Seats that never sold are censored observations, not zeros. Directors scale similar shows differently depending on whether the last one in that market sold out or embarrassed them. Any honest label needs factor-style adjustment for market, artist and date, plus repeated scaling of historical shows by the same expert to measure their own consistency.

The deployment challenge is speed and politics. A recommendation that arrives after the queue has drained is worthless, so the model must be faster than the director's own glance at the sales map. And the final price is not purely a revenue decision. Artists set prices with fan anger in mind, the antitrust case has made "dynamic pricing" a public word, and the DOJ settlement caps fees at 15%. A model that maximises yield and ignores the artist's ceiling will be switched off after the first onsale.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A scaling and onsale ledger first and a model second. Every event gets a structured record of the scaling decision (price levels, hold counts by purpose, release plan, expected sell-through by section and the director's confidence), drafted automatically from the scaling sheet and the setup in the ticketing system and confirmed in seconds. Every onsale action is logged with a reason chosen from a short list and an optional sentence. After the show, each record is graded against section-level sell-through curves, final price realised, unsold inventory and the resale premium observed in the first week, adjusted for market and date.

On top of the ledger, learn the director's recurring reads. Fit seat-level time-to-sale and resale-premium models on thousands of past onsales. Use them to surface the patterns an experienced director acts on ("in this building the upper-bowl ends sell late; release holds after the first hour, not before") to the junior director setting up the next show and to the promoter weighing a second date. Every recommendation carries the artist's price ceiling as a constraint.

## Impact If Solved
If an onsale scaled on evidence moved even a few percent of the resale spread back to the artist, promoter and venue, that would be worth hundreds of millions of dollars a year on Ticketmaster's $37.1B of fee-bearing transactions alone (estimate). It would also cut the unsold-seat discounting that erodes the rest. Keeping a departing director's documented judgement turns the industry's most expensive tacit skill into something the firm keeps, and gives regulators and artists a record of why each price was set.
