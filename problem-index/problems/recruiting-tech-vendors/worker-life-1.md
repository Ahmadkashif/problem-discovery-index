# The Recruiter With Forty Open Requisitions

**Industry:** [[recruiting-tech-vendors|Recruiting Tech Vendors]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Hundreds of applications per role, hiring managers who will not give feedback, and a metric that measures how fast the requisition closed rather than whether the hire was right.
**Tags:** #gradient-boosting #bert #large-language-models #time-series-forecasting #confidence-intervals #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #automation

## The Problem
A corporate recruiter carries a portfolio of open requisitions, frequently far more than can be worked properly. Each involves sourcing, screening, coordinating, managing the hiring manager, negotiating offers and keeping candidates engaged through a process that takes weeks.

Application volume has outpaced any capacity to review it. A role receiving hundreds of applications gets minutes of attention per application at best, which is not review — it is a scan for disqualifiers, which is precisely the mechanism that produces the false rejections nobody measures.

The hiring manager relationship is the recurring friction. Managers reject candidates without articulable reasons, change requirements mid-process, are slow to give interview feedback, and want someone who does not exist at the budgeted level. The recruiter absorbs all of it and is measured on time to fill regardless.

And the metrics measure speed and volume — time to fill, requisitions closed, offer acceptance — none of which say whether the hire was good. Quality of hire is the metric everyone names as the one that matters and almost nobody computes, which means a recruiter who fills fast with mediocre hires outperforms one who takes longer and hires well.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is a role with high volume, high stakes for other people, and metrics that reward the opposite of care. A recruiter who wants to review applications properly cannot, and the system's response to that is automation that screens faster rather than capacity that screens better.

The powerlessness in the hiring manager relationship is the daily grievance. The recruiter is accountable for an outcome controlled by someone who outranks them, does not prioritise it, and frequently cannot articulate what they want — and the recruiter's job is to translate that into a search anyway.

The candidate-facing part is where the personal cost lands. Recruiters deliver rejections, manage candidates through processes that stall, and are the human face of a system they know treats applicants poorly. Most of them find that uncomfortable and have limited power to change it.

And the volume prevents the work that would make them good at it: understanding the role deeply, building relationships with candidates over time, and learning which of their judgements held up — the last being impossible anyway, since nobody measures it.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Make screening depth possible rather than screening speed faster. Capability-based surfacing that brings forward candidates a keyword filter would have missed, with the reasoning shown, lets a recruiter spend their limited attention on a better-selected set — which is a different intervention from automated rejection at higher volume.

Structure the hiring manager conversation. An intake process that establishes what the role actually requires, what is negotiable, and what the market offers at the budgeted level — with evidence from comparable searches — prevents most of the mid-process requirement changes that waste weeks.

Chase feedback automatically. Interview feedback that is overdue, blocking a decision and losing a candidate is a workflow problem with an obvious mechanical fix.

Measure quality of hire and make it the recruiter's metric. Tenure, performance and progression of hires, attributed back to the recruiter and the process, is computable at any organisation with a year of data and would change what the role optimises for entirely.

And close the loop on judgement. A recruiter who learns which of their advanced candidates succeeded has the only feedback that would make them better, and it is the same data the sector's validation problem requires.

## Impact If Solved
Recruiters make high-volume decisions about people's access to work under metrics that reward speed over accuracy, with no feedback about which of their judgements were right. Better surfacing makes limited attention go further than faster rejection does, structured intake removes the largest source of wasted process, and quality-of-hire measurement changes what the role is optimising for — which is the change that would improve outcomes for candidates and employers simultaneously.
