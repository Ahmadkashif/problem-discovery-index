# Onboarding and Member Matching

**Industry:** [[membership-community-platforms|Membership & Community Platforms]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Every platform has an onboarding checklist and an introductions channel, and neither does the one thing that works, which is putting a new member in front of the two people they should know.
**Tags:** #bert #word-embeddings #contrastive-learning #graph-neural-networks #k-nearest-neighbors #dimensionality-reduction #evaluation-metrics #feature-engineering

## The Problem
A new member arrives, completes a profile, is shown a welcome video and invited to introduce themselves in a channel where dozens of other introductions scroll past. They post, receive a few emoji reactions or nothing, and are then left to find their way around a structure of spaces and threads built for people who already know where things are.

What actually converts a new member into a participant is a connection to a specific person or a specific conversation where they have something to offer. Communities that do this well do it manually: a founder who personally introduces newcomers to two relevant existing members, or a buddy system, or a small cohort intake. Those practices work reliably and consume exactly the time the operator does not have, so they are abandoned as the community grows — which is when they matter most.

Meanwhile the platform holds everything needed to do the matching: what the new member said about themselves, what existing members talk about, who answers questions in which topics, and who has a history of welcoming people.

## What Already Exists
Circle, Mighty Networks and Skool all offer onboarding flows, welcome sequences, member directories and profile fields. Mighty Networks has invested specifically in member matching features. Discord communities use bots for role assignment and introductions. Some platforms run scheduled member-matching for one-to-one conversations, generally by random pairing. Gamification — points, levels, streaks — is widely deployed as a substitute for connection and produces activity rather than relationships.

## The Customisation Gap
Matching is done by profile fields or at random, and the useful signal is behavioural. Who answers questions about this topic, who replies to newcomers, who is in a subgroup that is still open, who shares a specific and unusual interest with this person — all of that is in the interaction history and none of it is in a profile dropdown.

The second gap is timing and framing. A match is only useful if it produces an actual exchange, which means it needs a reason — a specific question the new member asked that this existing member can answer, or a shared context worth mentioning. A random pairing with a prompt to chat has a low success rate for understandable reasons; a routed introduction with a concrete hook has a much better one.

Community-specific customisation matters more here than in most categories because the axis of relevance differs: a professional community matches on expertise and problem, a hobby community on subgenre and skill level, a recovery or support community on circumstance and stage — and in the last case the matching has to be handled with considerably more care than a recommendation algorithm usually applies, since a bad match is not merely unhelpful.

And the greeting capacity has to be managed. The members who welcome newcomers are a small group who can be exhausted by volume, so routing has to spread the load and notice when someone's willingness is being overused.

## Impact If Solved
The first fortnight determines retention, and the difference between a member who found two people and one who posted into silence is most of the churn in this category. Behavioural matching with a concrete hook reproduces at scale the manual practice that founders know works and stop doing as they grow, and it does it without the gamification that produces activity metrics and no relationships.
