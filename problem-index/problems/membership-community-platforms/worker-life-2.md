# The Volunteer Moderator Absorbing the Worst of It

**Industry:** [[membership-community-platforms|Membership & Community Platforms]]
**Type:** Worker Life Changing
**One-liner:** Unpaid volunteers handle the harassment reports, the disputes and occasionally a member in crisis, with a rules page, a delete button and nobody to escalate to.
**Tags:** #bert #transformers #large-language-models #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #compliance #tacit-knowledge-ml

## The Problem
Most community moderation is performed by volunteers — members who care about the place and agreed to help. They handle rule enforcement, disputes between members they know personally, spam and scams targeting the membership, and the small number of genuinely serious situations: sustained harassment, a member disclosing self-harm, a safeguarding concern involving a minor, a stalking situation moving offline.

They do this with consumer tooling and no training. There is no triage, so a spam post and a crisis disclosure arrive through the same report button. There is no policy beyond a rules page written at launch that does not anticipate the situation. There is no escalation path, so the volunteer who encounters a member in danger is making a judgement about another person's safety alone, at whatever hour it arrived, with no idea what the right action is.

The exposure is continuous. Volunteers read the content that gets reported, which is by definition the worst content in the community, and there is no rotation, no limit and no support.

## Why It Matters to the Worker
This is unpaid labour with real psychological cost and real consequences for getting it wrong, performed by people whose only compensation is belonging to the community they are protecting. The cumulative effect of reading hostile content is documented in the professional trust and safety literature, where it is managed with rotation limits, counselling access and clinical supervision. Volunteers have none of that and are frequently exposed to a higher proportion of distressing material than a professional moderator, because there are fewer of them.

The decisions carry weight the tooling does not acknowledge. Banning someone from a community that is their main social contact is a significant act. Mishandling a crisis disclosure is a worse one. Volunteers describe lying awake about both, and about disputes involving people they consider friends — the conflict of moderating a community you are a member of is constant and unaddressed by any product.

And the knowledge is unpreserved. A moderator who has handled three hundred situations knows what this community does and why, and when they burn out — which is the normal ending — that leaves with them, and the next volunteer starts from the rules page.

## What a Solution Looks Like
Triage by severity first, because it is the difference between an inconvenience and a harm. Reports should be classified on arrival and a disclosure of self-harm, a safeguarding concern or a credible threat should never sit in the same queue as a spam post. The response for those categories should not be a moderation decision at all — it should be a prepared pathway with resources, a script and a route to someone qualified, which almost no community has and every community eventually needs.

Give context and precedent with every report. The surrounding conversation, both members' histories, prior incidents involving either, and what this community decided in similar situations. That is mostly querying existing data and it converts a lonely judgement into an informed one.

Reduce exposure. Preliminary classification means a volunteer does not have to read everything to sort it, previews can be blurred by default for the categories that warrant it, and volume can be distributed rather than landing on whoever checks first.

Preserve the precedent. Decisions and their reasoning, recorded as the community's own case history, make a moderation team consistent and survive the departure of the person who accumulated the knowledge.

And acknowledge the real limits. A volunteer team should be able to say this is beyond us, and the product should provide somewhere for that to go — an escalation to the platform, to a paid service, or to the appropriate external authority, with the process documented before it is needed rather than improvised during a crisis.

## Impact If Solved
Volunteer moderators are the invisible labour force holding up most online communities, doing work that professional organisations treat as requiring clinical support, with a delete button. Severity triage, context, exposure reduction and a real escalation path address both the harm to them and the quality of the decisions — and preserving the precedent means a community's accumulated judgement survives the burnout that is currently how every volunteer's tenure ends.
