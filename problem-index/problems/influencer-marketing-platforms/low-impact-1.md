# Creator Discovery and Brand Safety Vetting

**Industry:** [[influencer-marketing-platforms|Influencer Marketing Platforms]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Every platform has a searchable creator database and an authenticity score, and none of them can tell a brand whether this specific creator is a risk for this specific brand.
**Tags:** #bert #transformers #cnns #word-embeddings #dbscan #graph-neural-networks #evaluation-metrics #compliance

## The Problem
Discovery means filtering a database by category, follower range, geography and engagement rate, then manually reviewing the shortlist. The manual review is where most of the real work is: scrolling a creator's back catalogue looking for anything that would embarrass the brand, checking whether their audience looks real, checking whether they have recently worked with a competitor, and forming a judgement about tone.

Both halves are weak. The filtering surfaces the same well-known creators to everyone, because category and size are the only dimensions available. The vetting is a human skimming a few dozen recent posts of a creator who may have ten years of content across four platforms, which finds the obvious problems and misses the ones that surface later — an old video, a podcast appearance, a reply thread. Brands have been burned by exactly this often enough that large ones now run manual agency review costing thousands per creator, which caps how many they can consider.

## What Already Exists
The platforms all ship discovery: CreatorIQ, Captiv8 and Upfluence index millions of creator profiles with filtering and lookalike features. HypeAuditor and Modash sell audience authenticity scores. Social listening tools identify creators already mentioning a brand. Agencies maintain vetted rosters, which is much of what a brand is buying when it hires one. Platform-native marketplaces on TikTok, Instagram and YouTube offer their own matching with better underlying data and narrower scope.

## The Customisation Gap
Brand safety is not a property of a creator, it is a relationship between a creator and a brand, and every tool in the market scores it as if it were the former. A political statement that is disqualifying for one advertiser is alignment for another; adjacency to alcohol matters enormously to a children's brand and not at all to a spirits brand; a competitor relationship is fatal in some categories and routine in others. The generic risk score cannot express any of this, so it is either too strict to be useful or too loose to be trusted, and the manual review happens anyway.

The second gap is coverage. Review looks at recent posts on the primary platform; risk lives in the back catalogue, in other platforms, in video and audio content nobody transcribes, and in comment replies. Doing that thoroughly by hand is not affordable at the scale a campaign needs, which means it is done thoroughly for the ten largest creators and barely for the other ninety.

The third is that authenticity scores are opaque and vendor-specific. A brand told a creator scores 78 has no way to know what that means, whether it is comparable to another vendor's 78, or which of the underlying signals drove it. For a decision that disqualifies someone's income, that opacity is a real problem and not only a product one.

## Impact If Solved
Vetting cost is the binding constraint on how many creators a brand can consider, which is why campaigns concentrate on the same known names. Brand-specific risk models with stated reasons — this creator, this content, this concern, here is the clip — make deep vetting affordable across a hundred candidates rather than ten, which directly widens the funnel into the mid-tier creators the selection model would otherwise recommend. It also gives the creator something they currently never get: a reason.
