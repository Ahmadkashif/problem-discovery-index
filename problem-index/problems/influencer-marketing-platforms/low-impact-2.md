# Campaign Workflow, Rights and Payments

**Industry:** [[influencer-marketing-platforms|Influencer Marketing Platforms]]
**Type:** Low Impact (Customisation Opportunity)
**One-liner:** Contracting, briefing, approvals, usage rights and payment for two hundred bespoke partnerships is handled by workflow software that assumes every brand runs the same process, and none of them do.
**Tags:** #large-language-models #bert #gradient-boosting #evaluation-metrics #workflow-orchestration #compliance #automation #data-integration

## The Problem
Every creator partnership is a small bespoke contract. It has deliverables, exclusivity terms, a usage rights window, whitelisting permissions, a disclosure obligation, an approval process and a payment term. A mid-sized campaign has a hundred of these; an always-on programme has thousands live at once with different terms agreed at different times by different people.

The failure modes are administrative and expensive. Usage rights expire and the brand keeps running the asset as a paid ad, which is a breach the creator eventually notices. A creator posts without the required disclosure and the brand carries the FTC exposure. Approval rounds sprawl across email and direct messages until nobody knows which version was signed off. Payments sit unpaid past terms because an invoice arrived in a format the brand's accounts payable system rejected.

## What Already Exists
This is the part of the category that works. Grin, Aspire, CreatorIQ and Mavrck all provide contracting, briefing, content collection, approval workflows, rights tracking and payment, and they have removed an enormous amount of spreadsheet work. Payment rails through Tipalti and similar handle global creator payouts. E-signature is integrated. Some platforms auto-check for disclosure hashtags.

## The Customisation Gap
The workflows encode one process and brands have many. A regulated advertiser needs legal and medical review steps with recorded sign-off; a fast-moving direct-to-consumer brand needs approvals in hours and would rather skip them; an enterprise needs procurement onboarding, vendor tax forms and purchase order matching before a creator can be paid at all. Platforms handle the middle of that distribution and force the ends to work around them, usually in spreadsheets alongside the tool.

Rights tracking is the clearest gap because it is where the money and the legal exposure both sit. The term is in the contract as prose — thirty days paid usage on Meta placements in North America, extendable by mutual agreement — and the platform stores a date field somebody typed. Extracting the actual terms from the executed agreement, holding them as structured obligations, and enforcing them against where the asset is actually running is not done by anyone, and the asset is running in ad accounts the influencer platform cannot see.

Disclosure compliance is similar. Checking for a hashtag catches the simplest violations; the FTC's guidance is about clarity and conspicuousness in the actual content, which means placement in the caption, duration on screen, whether it is in the first three lines, whether a verbal disclosure was made in a video. That is a content question and nobody is answering it.

## Impact If Solved
Administrative failure is the most common way these programmes lose money and trust: expired rights that become claims, disclosure gaps that become regulatory exposure, and late payments that cost a brand access to the creators it most wants. Terms held as structured obligations and enforced against live ad accounts turns a recurring legal risk into a monitored one, and it is the piece brands consistently name as missing from tools they otherwise like.
