# The Charge Nurse's Read of Who Will Escalate Tonight

**Industry:** [[psychiatric-hospitals|Psychiatric Hospitals]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** An experienced psychiatric charge nurse knows at the start of a shift which patient is heading for a restraint, an assault or a self-harm attempt, and quietly changes the unit to prevent it, but the hospital never records that read, never learns which reads were right, and loses it when she leaves.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #survival-analysis #transformers #causal-inference #evaluation-metrics #worker-facing #compliance

## The Problem
A 24-bed adult acute unit at shift change holds people on involuntary holds, people in their third day of alcohol withdrawal, a young man in a first psychotic episode and a woman readmitted eleven days after her last discharge. The charge nurse takes report, then walks the hall. Within twenty minutes she has decided who gets checked every five minutes instead of fifteen, who moves to the room nearest the station, who is offered a PRN dose before dinner rather than after an outburst, which technician sits with whom, and which two patients should not be in the day room together. She does not consult a score. Asked how she decides, she says she has been doing this for twelve years.

What she is reading is specific. A patient who has been pacing the same stretch of corridor since lunch. A refusal of the evening dose followed by a polite question about discharge. A phone call from a family member that ended early. A new admission who keeps standing too close to staff. A patient who was loud all day and has suddenly gone quiet. These are tacit patterns built across thousands of shifts, and they are the clinical core of a psychiatric hospital, because the unit's main job besides medication is keeping a locked room full of people in crisis from hurting themselves or each other.

When the read is wrong, the outcome is recorded in detail. Staff restrain or seclude the patient, a physician or trained nurse must see them face to face within an hour, and an adult order lapses after four hours (42 CFR 482.13). Every hour is counted and published. In 2024 the median facility reported 0.05 hours of physical restraint per 1,000 patient hours and 0.03 hours of seclusion, but the 90th percentile reported 0.63 and 0.56, and the worst facilities 24.5 and 39.5 (IPFQR, 1,410 facilities). Staff are hurt. In 2017–2020 psychiatric and substance abuse hospitals recorded more than 100 violence injuries with days away from work per 10,000 workers, against 7–17 for the rest of the hospital subsector (AHRQ, using BLS data; search summary). Patients are hurt too, and a death in restraint goes to CMS by close of business the next day.

When the read is right, nothing happens and nothing is written down. The hospital records the restraint it could not prevent. It does not record the escalation the charge nurse saw coming, the observation level she raised, the PRN she offered early, or the fact that the night passed quietly. Structured tools exist, such as the Brøset Violence Checklist and the Dynamic Appraisal of Situational Aggression (named from recall), and some units score them every shift. They capture a handful of observable behaviours and miss most of what the experienced nurse is weighing. When she leaves for a hospital job with fewer assaults, her replacement inherits a census sheet and a scale.

This is a different judgement from the bedside deterioration read in [[general-hospitals|General Hospitals]], where the question is physiological decline. Here the question is behavioural, the outcome is a coercive intervention or an injury within hours, and the main levers are the unit's layout, staffing and medication timing, all controlled by the charge nurse.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data-collection challenge is that the nurse's read is never written as a prediction. It shows up only in actions: an observation-level change, a room move, a PRN given, a technician assignment, a note that says "pt more guarded this evening". Some of what she uses is in the record: medication refusals, PRN history, the legal status of the hold, the day of the stay, the last restraint, the census and the staffing grid. Much is not. Fifteen-minute observation sheets are still on paper on many units, and pacing, tone and who sat next to whom at dinner are never charted. To capture the expert, the system has to log her decisions as data, with a one-tap worry rating at shift start, and join them to what happened in the next eight to twelve hours.

The labelling challenge is that her action changes the outcome. A patient she moved and medicated early, who then had a calm night, looks like a false alarm. A patient she did not flag who assaulted a technician at 2 a.m. may have been unforeseeable. Nurses also disagree with themselves. They are more cautious after a bad night, less so on a short-staffed weekend, and differently calibrated for patients who frighten them. Labels have to be outcome-based (restraint, seclusion, staff or patient injury, self-harm, emergency medication within a fixed window) and corrected for the interventions that preceded them, using comparisons between similar patients on similar shifts who were or were not flagged.

The deployment challenge is that the tool must be faster than the nurse, and quieter. She already knows the three patients everyone is worried about. A score that ranks all 24 patients with no reason will be ignored by the second week, and one that flags patients who are loud but harmless will be resented. It has to surface, at handover, the one or two patients she has not flagged who look like patients who later escalated, give the reason in nursing terms ("refused 17:00 dose, PRN use doubled since yesterday, hearing tomorrow"), and fit on the handover screen. It must also never become a pretext to restrain earlier, and it must hold up when a surveyor or plaintiff's lawyer reads it.

## What a Solution Looks Like
First, a ledger of the nurse's decisions. At handover the charge nurse marks her worry for each patient on a three-point scale and records the actions she takes: observation level, room, technician assignment, PRN offered, family call. Digital observation rounds replace the paper sheet, so the technician's 15-minute entries (location, behaviour, sleep) become data. Each shift is graded afterwards against what happened within 12 hours.

Second, a model trained on the ledger and the outcomes. It learns which combinations of legal status, medication adherence, PRN trajectory, length of stay, recent events, milieu (who else is on the unit), staffing and note language preceded escalation, and which interventions preceded quiet shifts for similar patients. At each handover it adds at most two patients the nurse did not flag, each with its reason and the action that worked for similar patients. A new charge nurse gets the departing nurse's pattern library with its track record. Every demonstration and early pilot runs on synthetic records, and the model supports the nurse's call rather than replacing it.

## Impact If Solved
If the 10% of facilities at or above the 90th percentile brought restraint use halfway to the median, each would cut roughly 0.3 restraint hours per 1,000 patient hours; on a 100-bed hospital at 85% occupancy, about 745,000 patient hours a year, that is ~220 fewer restraint hours (estimate). Fewer restraints mean fewer staff injuries, fewer workers' compensation claims and less turnover on units where replacing a nurse costs tens of thousands of dollars (estimate), and fewer of the events that start surveys, lawsuits and investigations.
