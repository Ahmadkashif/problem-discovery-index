# The Rate Card in the Rep's Head

**Industry:** [[media-rep-firms|Media Representative Firms]]
**Type:** High Impact
**One-liner:** A rep firm is hired and fired on its stations' share of national money, and the pricing calls that decide that share are made by senior account executives on a feel for each buyer and market that is never recorded, never graded and leaves when they do.
**Tags:** #tacit-knowledge-ml #gradient-boosting #bayesian-inference #survival-analysis #causal-inference #feature-engineering #evaluation-metrics #revenue-impact

## The Problem
An agency buyer planning a quarter of spot TV for a car brand sends an avails request to the reps for every station in thirty markets: target demo, dayparts, flight weeks, a budget and a cost-per-point goal. For each represented station the rep's account executive pulls inventory, prices it and submits a proposal. Then the negotiation starts. The buyer comes back below the station's rate. The AE decides whether to hold, come down, or offer a package that protects rate in prime by giving value in early fringe. The buyer allocates the market budget across stations, and the station's share of that buy is set.

Senior AEs make these calls on knowledge they cannot fully specify. They know that this buyer always has money left in the last two weeks of a quarter and will pay up to clear it. They know this agency office allocates on CPP alone while that one protects the news station it trusts. They know a market is tightening before the station's pacing report shows it, because three buyers asked for the same week. They know that holding rate with one holding company this month costs share for the rest of the year. Asked how they price, they say they "read the market". This is tacit knowledge in the sense the term is used for a craftsman: pattern recognition built over thousands of negotiations.

The outcome of each call is eventually observable. The order lands or it doesn't, at a known rate. Weeks later the market's pooled revenue report shows the station's share of each agency's spend, and the power ratio shows whether the station took more or less than its audience share. But the decision and the outcome are never joined. The proposal lives in the agency's buying system, the counter-offer in an email, the order in the station's traffic system and the share in a subscription report. The rep is judged on power ratio at contract renewal, and contracts run up to ten years. It cannot say which pricing behaviours lifted it and which gave money away, and when an AE moves to the other rep firm or to a station group's in-house national desk, the reads go with them.

## Why It's Unsolved
The data collection problem is that the reasoning behind a pricing call has to be captured from the AE as they make it. That happens mid-negotiation, by phone and email, often on ~20 requests a day (estimate). A rationale written after the order is rationalised. One written before is a tax with no visible return to a commission-paid seller. The structured fields that would make grading possible are the buyer's stated goal, the AE's floor, what was traded away and why. No system the AE touches asks for them.

The labelling problem is that the outcome is noisy and partly hidden. A station loses share because a competitor ran a promotion, because the buyer's client cut budget, or because its ratings fell, not only because the AE priced badly. The share label comes from a pooled report weeks later at market and agency level, not order by order. Experts also disagree with themselves: the same AE prices the same buyer differently in a soft first quarter and in a political fourth quarter, and two AEs at the same firm will price the same avails differently. Requests that were never answered, or answered late, are part of the outcome and rarely logged.

The deployment problem is speed and trust. A pricing suggestion has to arrive while the buyer is waiting, inside the AE's existing screen, and be faster than the AE's own recall, or it will be ignored. It also has to respect the firewalls between divisions that represent competing stations in the same market. A model trained on one division's negotiations cannot leak another division's rates.

## What a Solution Looks Like
A negotiation ledger first and a model second. Every avails request, proposal, counter and order is joined into one record per station per buy, drafted automatically from the agency system export, the traffic system and the email thread. It carries the few fields only the AE knows, confirmed in seconds: floor, what was offered in trade, the reason for holding or moving. Each record is graded later against the order outcome and against the station's share of that agency's spend in the market's revenue report, adjusted for the station's ratings that quarter.

On top of the ledger, learn the senior AEs' reads. A model predicts the clearing cost per point and the probability of an order at each price, for this buyer, market, daypart mix and week of the quarter. It flags where the station's rate is out of line with what clears, and surfaces the senior AE's past decisions on similar requests to a junior AE or a successor. The goal is not to replace the negotiation but to make an experienced seller's market read available, with its track record, to everyone pricing the same stations.

## Impact If Solved
A rep firm that can show which pricing behaviours lift power ratio can defend its contracts with evidence rather than relationships. Against a commission base of several hundred million dollars at the largest firm (estimate), even a point of share recovered across a station group is worth more than most of the tools the firm buys. Keeping a departing AE's documented reads turns the industry's most expensive recurring loss, the seller who walks to a rival or to an in-house desk, into something the firm keeps.
