# Greeting Card Publishers

## Profile
**Category:** Media, Telecom & Information
**NAICS 2022:** 513191 — Greeting Card Publishers
**Market Size:** ~$2.6B in 2022 revenue at US employer firms, down from ~$3.55B in 2018 (Census Service Annual Survey via FRED, reported under the 2017 code 511191); at retail, Americans buy ~6.5 billion cards a year for ~$7–8B (Greeting Card Association via USPS, 2019). Census counts only ~113 employer establishments, while American Greetings' fiscal 2011 10-K estimated ~3,000 US card publishers, most of them small or nonemployer firms
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the two large publishers run scan data, EDI and planogram systems at mass-retail scale, but which design goes into which pocket of which store, and which of a season's candidate designs gets printed at all, is still decided by editors' and line planners' judgement in review meetings and spreadsheets; small publishers run on trade shows, sales reps and paper reorder forms
**Workforce:** Artists, illustrators, lettering and photo stylists; writers and editors who develop captions; art directors and line or product managers who plan each occasion and recipient; licensing managers; category managers and account teams who sell card departments to retailers; production artists and prepress staff; and a large part-time or contract field force of in-store merchandisers

## Key Pain Themes
A card publisher's product is thousands of small bets. Each design (art plus caption plus finish) occupies one pocket on a fixture organised by occasion and recipient ("Birthday — Wife — Humor"), sells a few units a week, and either earns its place or is pulled at the next refresh. Hallmark alone develops more than 19,000 new or redesigned cards and related products a year with a creative staff of about 900. Choosing which candidates to produce is a judgement made by editors and line planners who "know" what a 50-year-old buying for a sister will pick up, and that judgement is rarely recorded or graded. The cost of a wrong pick is concrete because much of the mass channel runs on scan-based trading: the publisher owns the card until it scans at the register, is paid only for what sells, and carries shrink in one of retail's highest-shrink categories. American Greetings reported in 2011 that three of its five largest customers bought this way.

The work runs on clocks the publisher does not set. Seasonal programs are planned more than a year ahead (estimate); the UK trade body describes Christmas ranges launched to retailers in January. Valentine's Day must be set the moment Christmas comes down. Seasonal cards are sold with a right of return, so every unsold Mother's Day card comes back as a credit or a destroy. Retailers award whole card departments on multi-year supply agreements, often with upfront incentives that the publisher books as deferred costs and earns back only if minimum purchases are met, and each renewal is a line review against the rival publisher's numbers. Licensed character cards add licensor approval windows. Sound and light-up cards fall under the CPSC's button and coin battery standard (16 CFR part 1263, adopting UL 4200A-2023 by direct final rule in the Federal Register of 21 September 2023), whose preamble names greeting cards that light up or make sound among the pliable products its battery-compartment tests cover; products made or imported after 23 October 2023 must comply.

Underneath sits the art. Publishers buy freelance designs for a flat fee, typically $275–$500 with ownership passing to the publisher, or license them for a set term, use and territory against a 4–6% royalty with a $150–$300 advance (Greeting Card Association guidance). A mid-size publisher holds thousands of such agreements with different rights, and whether a design can also go on gift wrap, a bag or a digital card is often answered by finding the contract. In the store, part-time merchandisers paid a flat rate per assignment keep fixtures to planogram, swap seasons and process credits for out-of-date product.

## Current Tech Landscape
Space planning tools (Blue Yonder, NielsenIQ Spaceman) draw card department planograms; EDI providers (SPS Commerce, TrueCommerce) move orders and point-of-sale reports under scan-based trading; retail execution apps (Repsly, GoSpotCheck) collect merchandiser photos and surveys; licensing platforms such as Flowhaven and RoyaltyZone manage agreements, design approvals and royalty reporting for brand licensors; Adobe Illustrator and Esko handle artwork and prepress; PLM and ERP systems track SKUs and print runs. Each works inside its boundary. What none of them holds is the record that would let a publisher learn: each design's attributes and the editor's reasons for choosing it, joined to pocket-level sell-through by store type, and to the rights that govern where else the art can go.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is design selection: which of several candidate designs for a pocket will outsell the incumbent, in which stores, and for how long. Outcomes are recorded at almost perfect resolution, since a scan-based trading publisher knows every unit sold by pocket and store, but the reasons a design was chosen are not recorded, so selection is never graded and the eye of an experienced editor leaves with the editor. Three things block a fix. The market is highly concentrated: Hallmark and American Greetings (majority-owned by Elliott since February 2025) hold most mass-channel departments, and American Greetings put its own North American share at about 35% in 2006. The scan data is the retailer's, shared under supply agreements. And creative organisations resist having taste scored. The strongest commercial opening is a selection and pocket-allocation system sold to the two or three publishers big enough to have the data, which is a very small market. Small publishers are poor buyers: they lack scan data and budget. For an insight engine the Pass 2 sweep finds no qualifier. The category's measurement sits with retail measurement and trend-forecasting firms already scored under other industries.
