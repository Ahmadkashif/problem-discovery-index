# Travel Agencies

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561510 — Travel Agencies
**Market Size:** ~$32.3B in 2022 receipts across 7,541 employer firms and 9,285 establishments (2022 Economic Census), plus a long tail of home-based advisors: the wider travel arrangement code (5615) counted 76,090 nonemployer businesses with ~$3.2B in receipts. US agencies sold $100.4B of airline tickets settled through ARC in 2025, a record (ARC)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — bookings run through the global distribution systems (Sabre, Amadeus, Travelport), supplier portals and online booking tools, while the advisor's knowledge of the client, the record of which suppliers deliver, and the tracking of commissions owed still live in CRM notes, inboxes and spreadsheets.
**Workforce:** Leisure travel advisors, a large share of them 1099 independent contractors working under host agencies; corporate travel counsellors and reservation agents at travel management companies (TMCs); 24/7 emergency service agents; ticketing and quality-control agents; back-office accountants who report air sales to ARC and reconcile supplier commissions; and account managers who run corporate programmes. BLS counts 61,500 travel agents (2025), 72% of them in travel arrangement services, at a median of $50,160

## Key Pain Themes
A leisure advisor sells judgement and is paid for it by someone else. The client describes a honeymoon, a family reunion or a sixtieth-birthday cruise; the advisor, drawing on years of trips, fam visits and complaints, decides which resort, which ship, which cabin deck and which ground operator will make it work, and earns a commission from the supplier (~10% on hotels, ~10-16% on cruise fares, estimate) weeks or months after the client travels. The judgement that made the booking right is never written down, and the outcome — the upgrade that was or wasn't honoured, the transfer that didn't show, the client who rebooked or quietly went direct — reaches the advisor as a text message, if at all. Hours of unpaid research go into proposals for clients who then book the same hotel online.

The money runs on a slow, leaky clock. Commissions arrive from hundreds of suppliers through hotel commission clearing houses, cruise-line statements, tour-operator cheques and the advisor's host agency, which takes its split. ASTA now keeps a watch list of hotels that fail to pay 45 days after a completed stay, and recovery firms take half of what they collect on claims older than 180 days. On the air side, agencies report and settle every ticket through ARC on a weekly cycle, absorb debit memos when an exchange breaks a fare rule, and since DOT's 2024 refund rule (14 CFR 260) must refund within 7 business days on card purchases when they are the merchant of record, whether or not the airline has returned the money.

Corporate travel adds its own clocks. A TMC must keep each client's travellers inside policy, find them within minutes when a hurricane or attack hits, and rebook hundreds of stranded travellers overnight when weather cancels a hub. Airline content is fragmenting: NDC made up 21.2% of ARC-settled transactions by December 2025, so the same fare may sit in one channel and not another. The industry is consolidating at the top (Amex GBT closed its ~$540M purchase of CWT in September 2025 after the Justice Department dropped its suit) and being attacked from software (Navan listed on Nasdaq in October 2025). Leisure agencies face Seller of Travel laws in California, Florida, Washington and Hawaii that dictate trust accounts or bonds for client money.

## Current Tech Landscape
Air, hotel and car content comes through Sabre, Amadeus and Travelport, with NDC aggregators filling gaps; cruise and tour bookings are made in supplier portals. Agency mid- and back-office runs on Trams and ClientBase, with newer CRM and itinerary tools such as Travefy, Axus, Tern and TravelJoy used by home-based advisors. Host agencies (Fora, Avoya, KHM, Outside Agents and many smaller ones) and consortia (Virtuoso, Signature, Internova's Travel Leaders Network, Ensemble) supply preferred-supplier programmes, amenity access and training. Corporate programmes book through SAP Concur, Navan, Spotnana-powered TMC tools and the TMCs' own platforms, with duty-of-care feeds from International SOS and Crisis24. Each does its slice competently. The record that matters most — why this client was matched to this product, and how it went — is owned by none of them.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is fit: which product, supplier and ground arrangement will work for this particular traveller, and which suppliers actually deliver what they promise. Advisors are paid on bookings rather than outcomes, so nothing joins the recommendation to the result; the outcome evidence is scattered across supplier portals, client messages and review sites; and the people with the best judgement are often independent contractors who own their client books and take them along when they change host. Most operators are poor buyers — about 79% of establishments have fewer than five employees, and full-time hosted advisors average ~$68,310 a year by the host survey's own count, with part-timers earning far less. The commercial opening sits with the organisations that span thousands of advisors and bookings: host agencies and consortia, which already fund advisor tools to recruit and retain, and the TMCs, whose margins depend on touchless servicing and disruption handling. The insight layer around the industry is thin. No Pass 2 pocket qualifies: the richest datasets (TMC programme data, consortium sales, the GDSs' and ARC's booking records) are either client-owned or licensed by small teams, and the strongest research business, travel risk intelligence, competes on speed over public sources.
