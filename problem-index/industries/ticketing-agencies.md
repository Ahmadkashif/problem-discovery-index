# Ticketing & Reservation Agencies

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561599 — All other travel arrangement and reservation services. The official examples are ticket offices and agencies (airline, bus, cruise ship, sports, theatrical), condominium time-share exchange services, reservation services (airline, car rental, hotel, restaurant) and automobile-club road and travel services. Timeshare exchange fits the definition exactly. This build also counts primary ticketing platforms and resale marketplaces as ticket agencies, and public park and campsite reservation services by analogy to hotel reservation services: public-agency inventory booked through IT-contractor operators
**Market Size:** ~$25.0B in receipts across 1,661 US employer firms, 2,996 establishments and 76,907 employees (2022 Economic Census). The code is top-heavy: California alone reported ~$8.6B, and in 2023 ~51% of establishments had fewer than five employees while 45 had 250 or more (County Business Patterns). The largest firms dwarf the rest. Ticketmaster sold 346M fee-bearing tickets worth $37.1B in 2025 for ~$3.1B of ticketing revenue (Live Nation 10-K). StubHub reported ~$9.2B of resale gross merchandise sales and Vivid Seats ~$2.7B. RCI had 3.3M paid timeshare-exchange member families (Travel + Leisure 10-K). Recreation.gov handled ~9M reservations in FY2023 (CRS)
**Tech Maturity:** High at the transaction, low at the decision. Every seat, room and campsite is sold through software that records the sale to the second, while the price, the hold and the release decision behind each section is still made by an experienced ticketing manager watching a dashboard and recorded nowhere
**Workforce:** Ticketing directors and client-services managers at ticketing companies who build event maps and set up onsales for venues, promoters and teams; venue box office treasurers and ticket sellers, often unionised, who manage holds, comps and will call and settle each show; pricing, inventory and revenue-strategy analysts; professional resale brokers, their buyers and their fulfilment staff; trust-and-safety and fraud analysts; fan-support agents handling transfers, failed deliveries, cancellations and refunds; reservation agents at timeshare exchanges, park reservation contractors and hotel reservation services; and the engineers who run queues and bot defences

## Key Pain Themes
A ticketing agency sells a perishable, numbered seat on behalf of someone else, and the price on that seat is a judgement made before the market opens. The ticketing director and the promoter's representative "scale the house": they assign price levels to sections, then hold back seats for the artist, the promoter, production, sponsors and card presales. On the morning of the onsale they watch the queue and decide, within minutes, whether to release holds, switch sections to dynamic pricing or add a second show. The outcome is recorded perfectly. Every seat's sale time and price is logged, and the secondary market shows within hours what the seat was worth. Underpriced seats resell at a premium that goes to brokers. Overpriced seats sit unsold until the week of the show. Almost nobody records the scaling and release decisions beside those outcomes, so the industry cannot say whether its best ticketing director is better than a simple rule, and when that director leaves, their read of a market goes too.

The onsale is adversarial and now regulated from every side. Brokers run bots and account farms to beat purchase limits. The FTC's 2025 complaint against Ticketmaster cited five brokers controlling more than 246,000 tickets through thousands of accounts. Executive Order 14254 told the FTC to enforce the BOTS Act, and the FTC's fees rule, in force since May 12, 2025, requires the all-in price wherever a ticket price is shown, including on resale sites. States are adding resale caps and bans on speculative listings. Vermont's Act 109 caps resale at 110% of face value from July 1, 2026, with a sunset in 2028, and North Carolina's rules took effect on October 1, 2026. Above it all sits the antitrust case. A jury found for 33 states and D.C. against Live Nation and Ticketmaster on April 15, 2026, the DOJ's settlement caps fees at 15% and opens venues to rival ticketers, and the remedies phase runs into 2027.

The people inside carry the exceptions. A box office treasurer reconciles holds, comps and kills from a spreadsheet against the system at midnight so the promoter's settlement balances. A broker's fulfilment clerk transfers hundreds of mobile tickets across five marketplaces before each delivery deadline. When a tour is postponed, fan-support agents absorb tens of thousands of refund requests and chargebacks under card-network deadlines. On the reservation side, timeshare-exchange agents explain opaque deposit values to owners by phone, and park reservation contractors watch scripts take campsites within seconds of release.

## Current Tech Landscape
Primary ticketing is Ticketmaster's, with AXS (AEG), SeatGeek, Paciolan (160+ college athletics programmes), Etix, Eventbrite, Tixr, See Tickets and DICE competing. Arts organisations run Tessitura, Spektrix, AudienceView and OvationTix. Resale runs through StubHub, Vivid Seats, SeatGeek, TickPick, Gametime and Ticketmaster's own resale, and brokers manage inventory in point-of-sale tools such as SkyBox, Ticketmaster's TradeDesk and Automatiq. Onsales are defended with Verified Fan, rotating SafeTix barcodes, Queue-it waiting rooms and bot-management services from Akamai, HUMAN and Kasada, and payments with fraud tools from Riskified, Forter and Signifyd. Reservation services run on RCI's and Interval International's exchange platforms, on Recreation.gov (Booz Allen), Aspira's ReserveAmerica and US eDirect for parks, and on housing platforms from onPeak and Maritz for conventions. Each tool records its own transactions well. None holds the decision record that matters most: the price, hold and release choice made for each section, joined to how each seat sold and what it resold for.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the ticketing director's scaling and release read: how many seats at which price in each section, how many to hold, and when to let them go. It is a dated, checkable forecast, and the industry has the best outcome labels in commerce: every seat's sale time and, for the seats that resell, a market price within hours. Hardly anyone grades the forecast against them. What blocks the fix is partly structural and partly political. The data is split between the primary platform, which sells as agent for the venue or promoter and does not own the buyer record outright, and the resale marketplaces, which see the true price but not the hold decisions. The price is also not purely commercial: artists set prices with an eye on fans' anger, and regulators now cap fees and resale in the same market. The strongest commercial opening is a scaling-and-release decision layer sold to the promoters, teams and large venues that own the onsale decision, together with a compliance layer that applies fee and resale rules by jurisdiction for every marketplace and broker. The agencies themselves are poor buyers of research. The large ones employ hundreds of data scientists and sell tickets, not insight, and the many small ones employ none. Above them, the consultancies, insurers and data vendors either work on data their clients own or sell to a handful of firms, so none clears the Pass 2 bar.
