# Geophysical Survey Firms

## Profile
**Category:** Professional Services
**NAICS 2022:** 541360 — Geophysical Surveying and Mapping Services
**Market Size:** ~$1.99B in 2022 receipts at 968 US geophysical surveying and mapping firms with 7,249 employees (Census Bureau, Statistics of U.S. Businesses 2022), 75% of them with fewer than five employees and Texas alone holding 35% of receipts; worldwide, the seismic data and imaging leaders are a different scale — Viridien reported $1,165M of 2025 segment revenue and TGS completed its merger with PGS in July 2024 — putting global geophysical acquisition, data and processing spend at an estimated ~$8–10B (estimate)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the sensors and inversion software are sophisticated, but the decision that matters, which anomaly in a radargram, magnetic grid or seismic section is real, is still made by one interpreter's eye, and its outcome is recorded in someone else's excavation log.
**Workforce:** Field crews (seismic line crews, vibrator operators and observers; GPR, EM and magnetometer technicians walking grids; UXO technicians), processing geophysicists, interpreters, project geophysicists who sign reports (California licenses Professional Geophysicists; Texas and other states license geoscientists), permit agents who secure landowner access for seismic programs, and CAD/GIS staff who turn interpretations into client deliverables. Onshore seismic crews run 40–100 technicians each (Dawson Geophysical 10-K); a near-surface firm is often three to ten people.

## Key Pain Themes
A geophysical survey firm sells an inference about something nobody can see. It drags a GPR antenna over a road, walks an electromagnetic sensor across a former bombing range, lays out a few hundred thousand seismic channels over a ranch, and delivers a map or a volume that says where the pipe, the shell, the void, the ore body or the fault probably is. The measurement is physics; the deliverable is interpretation. The client acts on it — designs a road around a utility, excavates a target, drills a hole, bids on a lease — and the excavation or the drill bit eventually reveals whether the interpretation was right. That verification happens weeks or years later, on the client's side, and almost never returns to the interpreter. The firm's most valuable asset, the experienced interpreter's eye for which hyperbola is a pipe and which is a rock, which polarizability curve is a 37 mm projectile and which is shrapnel, is never graded against outcomes and leaves when the interpreter does.

The external clocks belong to clients and regulators. Utility designating runs to a state DOT's design schedule under the ASCE 38-22 quality levels, and its QL-B lines are checked when the QL-A test holes are dug. Munitions response runs under DoD's DAGCAP accreditation, where a demonstration fails if a single target of interest is missed, and the field reality is that roughly 95% of excavated objects turn out not to be munitions (DoD fact sheet). Onshore seismic waits on governmental permits and landowner consent point by point, with Canadian work stopping at the spring thaw; offshore data is held by BOEM under proprietary terms of 25 years for processed geophysical information. Mining clients need targets before the drill season and a qualified person's sign-off for disclosure.

The economics are a boom-bust cycle wearing a professional-services coat. Seismic follows commodity prices and capital budgets, and its customer base is concentrated: one client was 51% of Dawson Geophysical's 2025 revenue, and "the results of seismic surveys conducted for a client belong to that client." Near-surface work is steadier but fragmented, priced by the day or the acre, and repeatedly re-mobilised when a coverage gap is found after the crew has left.

## Current Tech Landscape
Seequent's Oasis montaj (Bentley) is the near-surface and mining workhorse, with UX-Analyze inside its UXO Land extension doing dipole inversion, library matching and ranked dig lists for advanced classification; SLB Petrel, S&P Global Kingdom and Halliburton DecisionSpace dominate seismic interpretation; Viridien, TGS, Shearwater and DUG run processing and imaging at scale. GPR is processed in GSSI RADAN, Sensors & Software EKKO_Project and GPR-Slice; resistivity in AGI EarthImager and Res2DInv; refraction and MASW in SeisImager and similar tools. Field capture runs on vendor data loggers plus Trimble and Esri apps; deliverables go out through Civil 3D and ArcGIS. New entrants such as Exodigo, which raised a $96M Series B in July 2025, fuse multiple sensors with AI to sell subsurface maps directly to DOTs and rail authorities. Every tool works on the measurement. The record that joins an interpretation to what the excavator, the drill or the munitions technician actually found sits in the client's files, and no tool owns it.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the interpreter's anomaly call: which features in the data are real targets, what they are, and how confident to be. It is made thousands of times per project, it is verified by excavation or drilling more often than almost any judgement in professional services, and the verification is almost never joined back to the call. Three things block a fix. Structurally, the ground truth belongs to the client (seismic results and dig logs are the client's property) and arrives after the project has closed. Commercially, a firm that measured its own miss rate would hand a liability argument to every client whose excavator hit a line. Organisationally, most operators are three-to-ten-person shops with no one whose job is measurement, and the senior interpreters who carry the pattern library were thinned by the 2015–2020 seismic downturn (estimate). The strongest openings are therefore where the ground truth is already captured by rule: UXO advanced classification, where every dig is logged against the call under DAGCAP; SUE designating, where QL-A test holes check QL-B lines; and concrete scanning, where each core confirms or refutes the marks. The near-surface operators are workable but small buyers; national platforms such as GPRS are better ones. In the insight layer no pocket qualifies; the closest, at 48, is the multi-client seismic data library, which owns its data outright and has never graded its depth images against the wells later drilled through them, but earns without adding analysts and sells to a handful of firms. The boundaries hold with [[industries/land-surveyors|Land Surveyors]], who fix boundaries on the surface; [[industries/oil-gas-field-services|Oil & Gas Field Services]], who service wells; and [[industries/utility-contractors|Utility Contractors]], whose locators mark lines for excavation tickets rather than design.
