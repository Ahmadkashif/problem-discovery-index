# Satellite Operators

## Profile
**Category:** Media, Telecom & Information
**NAICS 2022:** 517410 — Satellite Telecommunications (operators that own or lease satellite capacity for connectivity, video distribution and earth-observation data links, ground-station and teleport operators, and resellers of satellite service)
**Market Size:** ~$105.0B in global satellite services revenue in 2025, inside a $303B commercial satellite industry (SIA State of the Satellite Industry Report, May 2026); satellite broadband passed 10 million subscribers worldwide, and Starlink reported more than 12 million active customers in June 2026 (a company post, as reported). The US Census count is far smaller, ~$6.3B of 2022 revenue for employer firms in NAICS 5174, and BLS QCEW counted 1,030 private establishments and 8,766 employees in 2024, because much of the largest constellation's activity is reported elsewhere (estimate) and direct-to-home TV (DIRECTV, DISH) is classified under 517111 with [[industries/wired-broadband-providers|Wired Broadband Providers]]. One slice of the residual code 517810, the operation of satellite tracking, telemetry and radar stations, overlaps this industry only through the ground-station work described here; the rest of that code sits with [[industries/telecom-resellers|Telecom Resellers]].
**Tech Maturity:** High in the spacecraft and the network, manual in the judgement around them — every satellite streams thousands of telemetry channels to a mission control system, but deciding that a slow drift in a reaction wheel's friction means trouble, which interferer is stepping on a transponder, or whether a conjunction warning deserves a manoeuvre still rests on a few senior engineers and their memory of past events.
**Workforce:** Spacecraft operations controllers and subsystem engineers on 24/7 consoles, flight dynamics and collision-avoidance analysts, payload and RF engineers, network operations centre (NOC) staff and carrier monitoring technicians, teleport and ground-station technicians, spectrum and regulatory engineers who prepare FCC and ITU filings, capacity sales and solutions engineers, and, at earth-observation operators, collection planners, image-quality analysts and customer delivery teams

## Key Pain Themes
A satellite is a machine nobody can touch once it launches. A geostationary satellite is designed for about fifteen years and often runs longer (estimate), and over that life its reaction wheels, batteries, thrusters and solar array drives wear in ways that show up first as faint changes in telemetry. The operations engineer who has watched a fleet for a decade recognises the pattern — a bearing that has started to run warm, a battery cell drifting from its siblings through eclipse season — before any limit alarm trips. Getting that call right decides whether a transponder outage is planned or a surprise, whether an insurer pays, and when a replacement satellite is ordered. Most of that knowledge is held by people, not by the mission control system.

The second set of pains comes from sharing orbits and spectrum. Interference is routine: in a 2013 IRG/Newtec survey of ~500 respondents, 93% had suffered it at least once a year and 17% continuously, and Eutelsat attributed ~58% of cases to accidental or human error in 2012, such as misaligned VSAT dishes and cross-polarised uplinks. Payload engineers find and stop it by phone and email with other operators' NOCs. In low Earth orbit the collision-avoidance load is exploding: Starlink alone made 207,152 avoidance manoeuvres between December 2025 and May 2026. Every warning has to be screened, assessed and either dismissed or acted on before the time of closest approach, and the Commerce Department's TraCSS screening service still runs on pilot funding.

The third set is regulatory clocks. The ITU publishes its space circular every two weeks, and an operator has four months to object to any new filing that could affect its networks; miss the window and the coordination right is weakened. The FCC's new Part 100 rules, adopted on 22 July 2026, keep five-year deployment milestones for GSO and six- and nine-year ones for processing-round constellations. LEO operators must dispose of satellites within five years of mission end. Amazon's FCC milestone for half of its 3,232-satellite Leo constellation, due 30 July 2026, had to be relieved in June 2026. Earth-observation operators carry their own clock: imagery promised within hours of collection, screened for cloud and geolocation error before it ships.

## Current Tech Landscape
Mission control and fleet operations run on manufacturers' ground software and on products such as Kratos OpenSpace and quantumCMD; carrier monitoring and interference geolocation on Kratos Monics and satID; orbit determination and conjunction analysis on Ansys STK and ODTK, with screening data from the US Space Force's Space-Track, the Office of Space Commerce's TraCSS, and commercial trackers such as LeoLabs, Slingshot, ExoAnalytic and COMSPOC. Ground modems and hubs come from ST Engineering iDirect, Hughes, Gilat and Comtech; ground-station-as-a-service from KSAT, AWS Ground Station, SSC, Leaf Space and Atlas Space Operations. Filings move through the ITU's SpaceCap, SpaceCom and e-Submission tools and the FCC's licensing systems, tracked in spreadsheets. Earth-observation orders flow through each operator's portal and marketplaces such as UP42 and SkyWatch. Each tool is competent within its boundary. The fleet's anomaly history, the interference case record and the filing calendar are each owned by none of them.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the fleet engineer's reading of telemetry: the call that a slow drift is a failure on its way, made months before an alarm. The telemetry is archived in full and the outcomes — a wheel that seized, a battery that lost a cell, a transponder that went dark — are recorded in anomaly reports and insurance claims, but the engineer's early reads are not, so an operator can't say which warnings were right or pass them on when the engineer retires. Three things block a fix. Anomalies are rare per satellite and each bus type behaves differently, so labels are scarce. Manufacturers hold the fleet-wide view across operators but under contract and export control. And operators run lean consoles where a new tool must cut alarms, not add them. The strongest commercial opening is the operator's own operations floor — fleet-health, interference and conjunction tools that a multi-orbit operator such as SES or a NewSpace constellation will pay for — and the regulatory workload of the filings team. For a research-insight engine the operators are poor buyers: their analysis serves their own fleets and is not invoiced, and the firms selling insight around them are small or tied to government buyers.
