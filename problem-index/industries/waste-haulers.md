# Waste Haulers

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 562111, 562119 — Solid Waste Collection; Other Waste Collection (brush, rubble and other non-hazardous hauling)
**Market Size:** ~$68.4B in 2022 receipts across ~8,400 employer firms (Census SUSB 2022): solid waste collection took $65.5B across 6,993 firms and 228,376 employees, and other waste collection $3.0B across 1,407 firms. The 57 enterprises with 500+ employees took ~$41.6B (~64%) of solid waste collection receipts, while 5,796 firms with fewer than 20 employees shared ~$6.9B (~10%). The codes count collection only: the same companies' transfer stations and landfills are counted elsewhere, and city-run crews sit in [[industries/municipal-services|Municipal Services]].
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the public consolidators run in-cab tablets, central dispatch (Republic's RISE platform) and truck cameras (WM's Smart Truck is on thousands of vehicles), and ~79% of Republic's residential routes are automated single-driver trucks; most of the ~5,800 small haulers bill in QuickBooks or a hauler package, keep routes in the regular driver's head and dispatch roll-off boxes from a whiteboard.
**Workforce:** Route drivers (front-load, automated side-load, rear-load and roll-off), rear-load helpers, dispatchers, route supervisors, container delivery and repair crews, diesel mechanics, customer service reps, billing clerks, commercial sales reps and municipal contract managers. BLS counted 68,300 refuse and recyclable material collectors in the waste collection industry in May 2023 at a $48,260 mean wage, out of 135,430 nationally (38,470 work for local governments); many haulers class their drivers as heavy truck drivers instead.

## Key Pain Themes
A hauler sells three different services on one fleet. Residential collection empties carts at the curb under a municipal franchise or contract, or by subscription where a city leaves households to choose; Republic's residential contracts "usually range in duration from one to five years" and are won by competitive bid. Commercial collection empties 2–8 yard front-load containers behind restaurants, shops and apartment blocks one to six times a week on a monthly subscription. Roll-off service drops 10–40 yard open-top boxes at construction sites and charges per haul plus tonnage. In 2025 Republic took $5.06B (30.5% of revenue) from small containers, $3.10B (18.7%) from roll-off and $3.01B (18.1%) from residential; WM took $5.63B, $3.11B and $3.51B. Price, not volume, drives growth: Republic's average yield rose 4.1% while volume fell 0.6%, and WM's collection-and-disposal yield rose 3.8%. Commercial customers absorb that through annual increases, fuel and environmental recovery fees and overage charges, and the courts are now testing it. Waste Connections settled a $1.4M class claim over commercial rate increases and fuel surcharges (search summary), and a 2025 settlement in northern Nevada requires WM to warn a customer once a year before charging overages, email a photo within 24 hours and stop paying drivers incentives tied to overage fees.

The work is physical, dangerous and runs on what drivers remember. Refuse and recyclable material collectors died at 41.4 per 100,000 full-time workers in 2023, the fourth-highest rate of any occupation. Automated side-loaders replaced two- and three-person rear-load crews with one driver working a joystick arm ~800–1,200 times a day (estimate). The National Waste & Recycling Association counts more than 130,000 private collection trucks and expected 14,200 new driver and helper jobs by 2026. The regular driver knows which carts sit behind a gate, which customers set out late, where the low wires are and which enclosure needs the container rolled out. A substitute running the same route misses stops. Each miss becomes a go-back trip, a complaint and, past a threshold, liquidated damages: La Habra's 2024 contract charges $100 per missed collection once complaints pass 55 homes a month (search summary).

Third parties set the clocks. California's SB 1383 requires every hauler route to be reviewed for contamination every year, with notices to generators, records kept for CalRecycle and counts reported in each jurisdiction's annual report, against a statewide target of 75% less organic waste to landfill by 2025. Seven states have passed packaging EPR laws that move recycling costs onto producers through the Circular Action Alliance; Oregon producers were first invoiced on 1 July 2025 and Colorado's on 1 January 2026, and both programmes reimburse collection. Franchise RFPs run on city calendars: San Fernando, California asked for proposals by 22 September 2026 for a council award on 19 January 2027 (search summary). Consolidation sets the exit price for the small operator: the five largest public haulers spent ~$1.5B on acquisitions in Q1 2025, ~$659M in Q2 and ~$545.7M in Q3.

## Current Tech Landscape
Hauler software is consolidating. Routeware bought RubiconSmartCity and RubiconPro in 2024 and serves more than 1,500 clients across route optimisation, in-cab and billing. AMCS sells an enterprise platform with Vision AI cameras. Terex now owns Soft-Pak billing and 3rd Eye cameras alongside Heil truck bodies, after buying Dover's Environmental Solutions Group for $2.0B in October 2024 (search summary). Hauler Hero raised a $16M Series A in February 2026 for a combined CRM, billing and routing system with AI agents, and Solen bought the roll-off package Cairn in December 2025 (search summary). Truck-mounted cameras that flag contamination and overfilled containers come from AMCS, Prairie Robotics, Safe Fleet's WasteVision AI and WM's in-house Smart Truck; Compology, now owned by RoadRunner Recycling, puts cameras inside containers. Fleet video and telematics come from Lytx and Samsara, and Republic runs its own RISE dispatch and Capture pricing tools. Each system works inside its own boundary. None of them holds the stop-level record of what was actually serviced at each cart or container, joined to what the contract allows and to what the regular driver knows about that stop.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the regular driver's knowledge of a route: the real stop sequence, where each cart or container actually sits, which customers overfill, which set out late, and which hazards wait at which address. That knowledge decides whether a route finishes on time, with no misses and no injuries, and it is invisible until the driver is off sick and a substitute runs the route. Trucks already record GPS traces, arm-lift events and camera frames, but nobody joins them into a stop-level service record, and the same missing record drives the industry's billing disputes. An overage charge, a contamination notice or a missed-pickup credit is only defensible if the hauler can show what happened at that container on that day. Three things block a fix. Drivers and their unions resist cameras that feel like discipline. Courts and franchise contracts now set evidence standards for charges. And the five public consolidators, which hold ~64% of solid waste collection receipts, build their own systems. The commercial opening is the ~1,140 independent and regional haulers with 20–499 employees, which buy software from Routeware, AMCS, Terex and the venture-backed entrants, and the regulated workflows (SB 1383 route reviews, franchise reporting, EPR reimbursement claims) where a compliance budget pays. Under the Pass 2 rubric nothing around the industry qualifies. The richest datasets belong to the haulers themselves or to their customers, and the firms that sell analysis about waste collection are a handful of small consultancies, associations and software vendors.
