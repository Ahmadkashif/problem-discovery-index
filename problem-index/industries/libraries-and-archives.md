# Libraries & Archives

## Profile
**Category:** Media, Telecom & Information
**NAICS 2022:** 519210 — Libraries and Archives
**Market Size:** ~$15.7B in public library operating revenue in FY 2022 (derived from the IMLS Public Libraries Survey: 9,194 public library systems in the 50 states and DC running 17,329 outlets, about 86% of it from local government), plus an estimated ~$7–8B a year spent by academic libraries (estimate) and an unmeasured special-library and archives tier. Census counts only $2.38B of 2022 revenue at private, employer-firm libraries and archives, because government-run libraries sit outside its business surveys. ALA puts the total at over 120,000 libraries of all kinds, an estimated majority of them school libraries, which belong to the schools that run them rather than here: private-school libraries sit under [[industries/k12-private-schools|K-12 Private Schools]], and public-school libraries are not analysed by any built industry.
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — catalogues, circulation and e-content lending have been digital for decades and run on a few cloud platforms, but the decisions that shape a collection (what to buy, license, renew, keep, withdraw or process next) are made by selectors and archivists on judgement, in vendor carts and spreadsheets, and are almost never recorded with a reason.
**Workforce:** Librarians (BLS counts 142,300 librarian and library media specialist jobs, median pay $68,270 in May 2025, a count that includes school library media specialists in K-12), library technicians and assistants at circulation desks, selectors and collection development librarians, cataloguers and metadata librarians in technical services, electronic resources librarians, archivists and special collections curators, digitisation technicians, youth services and programming staff, and the directors and boards who answer to a city, county, district or provost. The median public library system has 4.0 paid FTE and 1.9 librarian FTE.

## Key Pain Themes
A library's product is a collection someone else decided to build, and the decisions that build it are its least recorded work. A public library selector orders print from wholesaler carts and licenses ebooks and audiobooks on OverDrive, whose readers borrowed over 820 million digital titles in 2025 (a total that includes school lending through OverDrive's K-12 app, Sora). Each licence is a bet: publishers sell library ebooks on time- or loan-limited terms (HarperCollins introduced a 26-loan cap in 2011) at prices well above consumer retail, so the selector decides how many copies to license against a holds queue, which expiring licences to renew, and which titles will never earn back their cost. Print has its own version: the median library has under five paid staff, and weeding — deciding which books no longer serve the community — runs on the CREW method's MUSTIE criteria and the librarian's own judgement. Since 2023 that judgement is also a public fight: ALA documented challenges to 4,235 unique titles in 2025, 92% of them started by pressure groups or officials rather than parents, so every purchase and withdrawal may need defending at a board meeting.

Academic libraries spend most of their materials budget on e-resources: journal packages ("big deals"), databases and ebook collections, licensed on multi-year contracts that come up for renewal on dates the publisher sets. The electronic resources librarian has to turn COUNTER usage reports, interlibrary loan requests, open-access availability and faculty pressure into a renew, cancel or renegotiate recommendation before the contract rolls over. Funder mandates move the ground underneath: NIH's public access policy removed the 12-month embargo from 1 July 2025, changing what a cancelled subscription actually costs readers. Behind the public desk, technical services catalogue every item into MARC records drawn mostly from OCLC's WorldCat (25.3 million new records in FY2025), and the original cataloguing of gifts, foreign-language and special collections material backs up when experienced cataloguers retire.

Archives carry the longest clocks. Special collections hold boxes no one has processed: OCLC's 2010 survey of 275 research libraries found about half of archival collections had no online presence. Processing means appraising what to keep, arranging it, describing it in a finding aid, and screening it for personal data and donor restrictions before anything is digitised and published. Government archives answer to records schedules: OMB/NARA memo M-23-07 required federal agencies to manage permanent records electronically and transfer them with metadata from 30 June 2024. Funding is the outer clock for everyone: the March 2025 order to dismantle the Institute of Museum and Library Services halted its grants and data collection until a federal court in Rhode Island barred the dismantling in November 2025, and the October 2025 closure of Baker & Taylor, a supplier to over 5,000 public libraries, forced selectors to rebuild standing orders mid-year.

## Current Tech Landscape
Library services platforms are concentrated: Clarivate owns Ex Libris (Alma, at 2,745 libraries per the 2025 Library Systems Report, with Primo discovery and Rapido resource sharing) and Innovative (Sierra and Polaris for public libraries); OCLC runs WorldCat, WorldShare Management Services (850+ libraries) and the dominant interlibrary loan network on $262.7M of FY2025 revenue; EBSCO backs the open-source FOLIO; SirsiDynix, Biblionix, and Koha and Evergreen hosts serve public and small libraries. Digital lending runs through OverDrive (Libby), hoopla, cloudLibrary and the Palace Project. Print arrives from Ingram and smaller wholesalers through ordering portals such as GOBI. Archives describe collections in ArchivesSpace and preserve files in Preservica or repository platforms. Each system is competent at its transaction: the loan, the record, the licence, the finding aid. The decision record — why a title was bought, licensed, renewed, kept, withdrawn or left unprocessed, and what happened next — belongs to none of them.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the collection decision: which titles to buy or license and in how many copies, which subscriptions to renew, which books to withdraw, which archival boxes to process first. Every one of those decisions produces an outcome the library records perfectly — loans, holds, turnaways, interlibrary loan requests, finding-aid views — and none is recorded with the reasoning and the alternatives, so no library can say whether its selectors' bets pay off or keep that judgement when they retire. Three things block fixing it. Patron privacy statutes and professional ethics limit how circulation data can be joined to people, so models must work at title and branch level. Budgets are public and annual, so a decision tool must justify itself to a board or provost in the same cycle. And collection decisions are now contested in public, which makes a recorded rationale both more valuable and more frightening to write down. The strongest commercial opening is a collection decision ledger sold through the platforms libraries already run, or to consortia and state libraries buying for many small systems at once. The libraries themselves are cautious, slow, publicly funded buyers, and the insight layer around them is a handful of vendors and a cooperative rather than a fragmented market of research firms.
