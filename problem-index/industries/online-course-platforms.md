# Online Course Platforms

## Profile
**Category:** Digital Media & Creator Economy
**Market Size:** ~$25B US online education and professional skilling, spanning marketplaces, creator platforms and university partners
**Tech Maturity:** Excellent delivery, absent measurement. Coursera, Udemy, edX, Skillshare, Teachable, Thinkific, Kajabi and Maven stream video, run quizzes, issue certificates and handle payments at scale. None of them can tell you whether a learner learned anything, and the sector's central statistic — that a large majority of enrolments are never completed — has been stable and publicly known for over a decade.
**Workforce:** Course creators and instructors, instructional designers, learner support and community moderators, platform and video engineers, marketplace and growth teams

## Key Pain Themes
The business model monetises enrolment and the mission claims to produce capability, and nothing in the stack connects the two. Completion rates on open courses sit in the single digits; on paid marketplace courses they are better and still low. Assessment is predominantly multiple choice or an unreviewed project, and a certificate certifies attendance rather than ability. The outcome that actually matters — the learner applying a skill, passing an interview, doing the job — happens outside the platform and is never observed.

The second theme is that recorded video, which was the product, has been commoditised. A competent explanatory video on almost any subject is now abundant and often free, and a generative assistant will explain a concept interactively on demand. What remains scarce is structured practice, real assessment, feedback on work, and a credential somebody believes — precisely the four things this category has invested in least.

The third is maintenance. Technical courses decay fast; a course on a framework is materially wrong within eighteen months. Platforms rank and recommend by rating and enrolment, both of which lag decay, so learners are routinely sold instruction that no longer matches the tool.

## Current Tech Landscape
Coursera and edX hold the university-partnered tier; Udemy and Skillshare the open marketplace; Teachable, Thinkific, Kajabi and Podia the creator-owned tier where the creator keeps the audience relationship. Maven and cohort-based formats went the other way — live, expensive, high completion, hard to scale. Video infrastructure runs on standard streaming stacks. Assessment tooling is basic almost everywhere, with proctoring vendors bolted on for credential-bearing courses. Learning record standards like xAPI exist and are barely used outside corporate systems. Generative tutoring features have been added across the category as assistants, not as assessors.

## Analysis
These platforms hold the most detailed record of human learning behaviour ever assembled — every pause, rewind, replay, quiz attempt, abandonment and return, across millions of learners moving through the same material. That data supports a well-posed and long-studied problem: estimating what a learner currently knows, and what they should do next. The sector has instead spent a decade optimising enrolment, recommendation and video delivery, because those are what the revenue model rewards. As generative tools erode the value of recorded explanation, the unexploited asset becomes the only defensible one: a platform that can credibly say this person can now do this thing has a product that a video library does not.
