# Convention & Visitors Bureaus

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561591 — Convention and visitors bureaus
**Market Size:** ~$2.66B in receipts across 863 US employer firms with 7,889 employees (2022 Economic Census). These are mostly nonprofits: 823 of the 940 establishments counted in 2023 (~88%) were nonprofit, ~58% had fewer than five employees, and none had 250 or more (County Business Patterns). The count leaves out government-run destination organisations, such as city tourism departments, special districts and state tourism offices, so total spending on destination promotion is larger (~$5B+, estimate). The biggest bureaus are $100M-a-year organisations: Visit Orlando, a 501(c)(6), reported $113.7M of revenue for 2024, of which $104.4M was contributions and grants (Form 990)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium. Every bureau above a few staff runs a destination CRM, a website CMS and a stack of licensed data dashboards. The two numbers the bureau is funded on, room nights booked and visitor spending generated, are still claims made at contract signing or produced by a model. Nobody checks them afterwards against what actually happened.
**Workforce:** A president or CEO who answers to a board of hoteliers and to the city that contracts for the bureau's services; convention sales managers covering national associations, corporate, sports and SMERF (social, military, educational, religious, fraternal) groups; convention services managers who support booked groups from handover to the last day on site; marketing and communications staff working with an agency of record; partnership or membership managers selling listings and co-op ads to local businesses; in larger bureaus, a research or data analyst and a finance lead who reports on hotel-tax use; visitor centre staff; and sports commission and film office staff in some cities

## Key Pain Themes
A convention and visitors bureau is paid out of hotel occupancy tax, and sometimes a tourism improvement district assessment, to produce overnight stays that it never sells and cannot directly count. More than 87% of DMOs reported receiving hotel room tax in the last public financial-profile study (2015). The bureau sells the destination to two audiences. Meeting planners are sold through a convention sales team that sends leads to partner hotels and bids for citywide conventions years ahead. Leisure travellers are reached through paid media placed by an agency. The KPIs it reports to its funders are definite room nights booked and the visitor spending its campaigns are said to generate. Both are predictions. A booked room night is the block written into a hotel contract three to seven years before the meeting (estimate). The industry's own reporting handbook admits that post-event pickup "may not be obtainable", because attendees increasingly book outside the block. Campaign returns come from survey and modelling studies; the most recent national study put the average at $85 of visitor spending per $1 of media.

The clocks are set by others. Planners send RFPs through Cvent and email with response deadlines measured in days, and the same lead often arrives through a third party, the bureau and the planner at once. Hotel contracts carry cut-off dates and attrition clauses that turn an over-promised block into a penalty for the group, and a lost repeat booking for the city. Above it all sits the funding cycle. Bureaus defend their contracts at annual city budget hearings, tourism improvement districts come up for renewal votes, and in states such as Texas every hotel-tax dollar must pass a statutory two-part test. In the 2025 DestinationNEXT survey, 42% of destination organisations said their funding was at risk of being cut or eliminated within three years.

The people inside carry the gap between claim and proof. After a meeting is booked, a convention services manager supports dozens of groups at once, re-sending the same materials and chasing the same local vendors, then works the long on-site days when each group arrives, in a role that produces no room nights for the sales report. A bureau's lone research analyst rebuilds the board report each month from six dashboards that disagree, knowing the number will be attacked at the next hearing. In a bureau of three people, which is the typical size, nobody does either job properly.

## Current Tech Landscape
Simpleview, which Granicus bought in 2024 and now sells as Granicus Destinations, is the default destination CRM and website platform: it claimed more than 1,000 destination clients and more than 90% of the top 50 North American meeting destinations. Tempest's iDSS is the main alternative CRM. Planners source through Cvent, and bureaus buy meeting history from Knowland and the MINT+ database. Hotel performance comes from STR (CoStar), vacation-rental supply from AirDNA and Key Data, visitor movement from location panels sold by Placer.ai, Zartico and Datafy, and visitor spending from card data such as Visa Spend Signals (formerly Visa Destination Insights). Economic impact is estimated with Tourism Economics' models, including the Event Impact Calculator, which more than 300 destination organisations license through Destinations International. Agencies such as MMGY Global, Miles Partnership and Madden Media place the media. Each tool answers its own question. None holds the record that would settle the bureau's funding argument: each bid and campaign decision, made at the time, joined to the stays and spending that actually followed.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the convention sales director's read of a group: will this association actually choose us, and if it does, will its attendees fill the block, or book around it, or stay home? That read decides which bids get staff time and how much incentive money gets offered. It is a dated, checkable forecast, and the outcome shows up in the housing report and the hotels' guest folios after the meeting. Bureaus report the forecast, the contracted block, as their headline result and almost never record the outcome beside it. Leisure marketing has the same shape: the campaign claim is modelled and the incremental visit is never isolated. What blocks the fix is structural, not legal. Pickup data sits with hotels and housing companies, location and card data are licensed panels the bureau cannot audit, and most bureaus are too small to employ anyone to join the two. The strongest commercial opening is a pickup-and-incrementality layer sold through the destination CRM vendors and the state tourism offices that already fund shared data licences. The bureaus themselves are poor buyers of research: they are publicly funded, mostly tiny, and buy through budget cycles set by councils. Above them, the data vendors, housing companies and consultancies hold richer data but sell software, services or studies to a market of a few hundred bureaus, and none clears the Pass 2 bar.
