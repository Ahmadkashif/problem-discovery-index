# Self-Insured Benefit & Risk Pools

## Profile
**Category:** Insurance
**NAICS 2022:** 525120, 525190 — Health and Welfare Funds; Other Insurance Funds (legal entities organised to provide health benefits or insurance exclusively for their sponsors' employees or members). The codes count only 739 and 390 private establishments with 5,174 and 2,398 employees (BLS QCEW, 2024), because Taft-Hartley fund offices are often staffed by third-party administrators and public entity pools file largely under public administration. This hub covers the activity wherever it is filed: the trustee- or board-run funds that bear the risk themselves. The administrators they hire sit in [[industries/insurance-tpa|Insurance Third-Party Administrators (TPAs)]], and their multiemployer pension cousins in [[industries/pension-funds|Pension Funds]].
**Market Size:** 1,220 multiemployer health and welfare funds covered ~4.8M members in 2023 (Milliman, from Form 5500), paying an estimated ~$70–90B a year in benefits (estimate). 67% of covered US workers sit in self-funded plans (KFF 2025). Over 500 public entity risk pools serve at least 80% of the 90,000+ US public entities (AGRiP, 2014), with an estimated ~$20–30B of member contributions (estimate). Self-insured employers paid 25.6% of the $64.1B of workers' compensation benefits in 2023 (NASI). Stop-loss carrier premium reached ~$39B in 2024 (Oliver Wyman, via Insurance Business). The largest single fund, the UAW Retiree Medical Benefits Trust, held ~$62.9B of assets at end-2024 (Form 990).
**Tech Maturity:** Low-Medium — claims run on TPA platforms and pool management systems such as Origami Risk, but rating, eligibility and reserving decisions are made in spreadsheets, board packets and the heads of a few senior staff.
**Workforce:** Boards of trustees (union and employer trustees for Taft-Hartley funds; elected officials and managers for public pools); fund administrators and pool executive directors; eligibility and contribution clerks, claims examiners and liability adjusters; underwriters, loss-control consultants and member-services staff at pools; and the outside actuaries, benefits consultants, auditors and fund counsel every board relies on.

## Key Pain Themes
A self-insured fund prices its own risk on data too thin to be credible member by member, and then waits years to learn whether it was right. A Taft-Hartley health fund collects a negotiated contribution per hour worked. That rate is fixed for the life of a bargaining agreement, typically three to five years (estimate), while Segal projects a 9.9% median medical trend for 2027, and million-dollar stop-loss claims rose 29% in a single year (Sun Life, 2025). A public entity pool sets each city's or school district's contribution from exposure and a few years of losses. Then one police use-of-force verdict can wipe out a decade of that member's premiums. Colorado's CIRSA raised law enforcement liability charges 150% for 2024 after successive records: an $8.75M settlement, a $9.5M settlement, a $14M Denver verdict and a $19M settlement. Its long-time reinsurers left the market.

Underneath sit high-volume, low-visibility tasks run by small offices. Health fund eligibility is decided monthly from hours that contractors report late and by hand. A Southern California electrical fund credits hours reported more than three months late to the month received, because its vendor contracts forbid retroactive eligibility. Pools still keep exposures and rates in spreadsheets (AGRiP's 2025 primer says so; Montana's schools pool did its underwriting entirely in them). Every large claim must be filed with the stop-loss carrier inside the contract's window, or the money is lost.

Third parties set the clocks. Health funds file RxDC drug-spend reports by June 1 and gag-clause attestations by December 31, and owe mental health parity comparative analyses on demand. Pools renew with members and reinsurers on fixed dates, usually July 1 or January 1. Collective bargaining expirations fix when a health fund's contribution can change at all.

## Current Tech Landscape
Taft-Hartley funds run on third-party administrators (Zenith American, BeneSys, Wilson-McShane and regional firms) or fund-office systems, with networks, PBMs and stop-loss bought separately. Consultants and actuaries (Segal, Horizon Actuarial, Milliman and others) project costs for the trustees. Public pools run claims, underwriting and billing on pool management systems such as Origami Risk, with outside actuaries setting rates and reserves at a stated confidence level. Retiree trusts buy Medicare group plans and exchanges. Self-funded plan analytics vendors (Springbuk, Artemis Health, Milliman MedInsight) report claims experience back to trustees. Each tool records the transaction, but none records the judgement behind the decisions that cost the most: why an experienced claims manager reserved a police claim at $250,000 rather than $2M, why a fund administrator expected a claimant to blow through the specific deductible, or why a loss-control consultant flagged one jail and not another.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the early read on which claim or claimant will become large. A pool claims manager makes it on a new civil rights complaint, a health fund's medical manager on a premature birth or a new dialysis start. It sets the reserve, the settlement posture, the stop-loss notice and next year's contribution. The judgement is recorded only as a number, and graded only when the claim closes years later. Three things block a fix. Experience is thin: one pool sees a handful of seven-figure police claims a decade. Labels are slow and noisy, because verdicts turn on venue and jury. And the people who know (senior adjusters, defence counsel, fund administrators) are few and retiring. Pools and funds are honest but slow buyers. They are board-governed, buy through procurement or trustee votes, and most run with fewer than ~20 staff (estimate). The Pass 2 sweep finds no qualifier. The firms that see across many funds either work on fund-owned, HIPAA-covered claims (repricing vendors, consultants, stop-loss underwriters, analytics vendors), sell into a market of a few buyers, or have no insight function beyond production staff (fund administrators and plan auditors).
