# Architecture Firms

## Profile
**Category:** Professional Services
**NAICS 2022:** 541310 — Architectural Services
**Market Size:** ~$104.1B in gross billings at US architecture firms in 2023 (AIA Firm Survey Report 2024), earned by more than 19,000 firms, about 75% of them with fewer than 10 employees; 116,005 licensed architects across the 55 US jurisdictions in 2024 (NCARB)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — design authoring is sophisticated and one-third of firms already use AI day to day, but quality review, permit corrections and construction administration still run on PDF markups, email and the memory of a few senior people.
**Workforce:** Licensed principals and project architects who stamp the drawings, project managers, unlicensed designers working toward licensure, job captains and BIM technicians who produce the sheets, specification writers, construction administrators, and marketing staff who assemble qualifications

## Key Pain Themes
An architecture firm sells a building that does not yet exist and delivers it as a legal instrument: a set of construction documents that a contractor prices, a building department approves and a court may later read. The fee is fixed in advance — a lump sum or a percentage of construction cost (~6–12% on most commercial and institutional work, estimate) — and earned through the AIA B101 phases, with construction documents taking the largest share (~40%, estimate). Average 2023 profit was 13.2% of net billings. Every hour of rework comes out of that margin, and every error that survives into the issued set comes back as an RFI, a change order or a professional liability claim years after the team has moved on.

The binding control over that risk is a person. Before a set goes to permit or bid, a senior principal marks it up in red for a day or two: the parapet that will leak, the stair that loses headroom once the framing is drawn at its real depth, the wall section that disagrees with the elevation. That review is the best predictor of how construction will go, and none of it is recorded in a form anyone can learn from. Meanwhile the external clocks keep moving: correction letters that send a set back for another four to twelve weeks (estimate), public bid dates, state review of schools and hospitals, three-year code cycles, and from July 2024 California's embodied carbon requirement for large nonresidential buildings.

Construction administration is where the firm pays for what it drew: hundreds of submittals and RFIs on a mid-size building (estimate), each with a contractual response time of ~10–14 days (estimate), each answer a liability if it reads as approving the contractor's means and methods. The same staff are often producing the next project's documents, under routine unpaid overtime — in a 2024 UK survey 90% of architecture employees worked overtime and two-thirds weren't paid for it — on a licensure path that averaged 12.9 years in 2024.

## Current Tech Landscape
Revit dominates mid-size and large firms, with Archicad, Vectorworks and SketchUp in smaller practices and Rhino for complex form. Bluebeam Revu is the de facto markup and QA/QC surface. Submittals and RFIs move through Newforma, Procore or Autodesk Construction Cloud — usually the contractor's platform. Project accounting runs on Deltek Vantagepoint or Ajera, BQE CORE or Monograph; specifications on AIA MasterSpec in Deltek Specpoint or SpecLink; code research on ICC Digital Codes and UpCodes. AI tools are arriving at the edges: Autodesk Forma and TestFit for feasibility, Veras for rendering, and drawing checkers such as Helonic, Structured AI and InspectMind that scan PDF sets for coordination and code errors. Each works within its boundary. The record that links a drawing decision — a detail, a code interpretation, a specified product — to what happened in construction and occupancy belongs to no one: the redlines stay in a Bluebeam file, the RFIs in the contractor's system, and the claims in the insurer's.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the senior architect's sense of which details and coordination points will cause trouble in construction. It is exercised under deadline by a handful of people per firm and never graded, because the outcome — RFIs, change orders, water in the wall, a claim — surfaces months or years later in records the architect does not hold. Three things block a fix. Structurally, the feedback loop crosses organisations: the contractor owns the RFI log, the owner the warranty calls, the insurer the claims. Legally, liability culture discourages firms from writing down their own errors in discoverable form. Organisationally, three firms in four have fewer than ten people and no one whose job is quality. The commercial opening is with large firms (50-plus staff, whose share of billings rose 40% between 2015 and 2023) and with the specialists paid to review architects' work; small firms are poor buyers when the industry average is ~$143,000 of net billings per employee. In the insight layer no pocket clears the Pass 2 bar; building and fire code consultants come closest at 49, with the code report as the invoice and an unused archive of what each jurisdiction accepted, held back by a clock that belongs to the owner's permit schedule rather than to a third party. The boundary with [[industries/engineering-consultants|Engineering Consultants]] (who stamp structural and MEP work under the architect) and [[industries/general-contractors|General Contractors]] (who build what the architect draws) holds throughout.
