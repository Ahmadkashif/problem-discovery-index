# Testing Laboratories

## Profile
**Category:** Professional Services
**NAICS 2022:** 541380 — Testing Laboratories and Services
**Market Size:** ~$27.5B in US employer-firm revenue in 2022 (Census Service Annual Survey); 7,970 employer establishments (2022 Economic Census, as reported) and 164,490 employees (BLS OEWS, May 2023). The global testing, inspection and certification market is put at more than €200B, about half outsourced (Bureau Veritas estimate, as reported)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the instruments are digital and the large networks run enterprise LIMS, but sample intake, field data, second-person data review, report assembly and the accreditation quality system still run on paper forms, spreadsheets and the senior reviewer's eye.
**Workforce:** Inspectors, testers and samplers (the largest occupation at 21,540), chemical technicians, chemists, engineering and environmental science technicians, mechanical and electrical engineers in product safety and EMC labs, microbiologists, calibration technicians, construction materials field technicians, sample receiving staff, quality managers, project managers and client services

## Key Pain Themes
A testing laboratory sells a number with a signature on it. A sample or product arrives, is tested against a published method or standard under ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation, and leaves as a result on a certificate that someone else acts on: a utility reports it to a state regulator, a food plant releases a held lot, an engineer of record accepts a concrete pour, a retailer lists a toy, an importer files it with customs. The instrument time is rarely the constraint. The constraint is the human work around the instrument — logging a cooler of samples from a handwritten chain-of-custody form, deciding whether an automatic peak integration is right, reviewing a finished batch before it can be signed, assembling the report in the client's format, and keeping the quality system current for the next assessment. Most of the lab's real expertise is concentrated in a few senior reviewers whose judgement on whether a result can be trusted is recorded only as an audit-trail event, never as a reason.

Every segment runs against a clock someone else sets. Environmental methods carry absolute holding times: a sample not prepared inside its window is invalid and usually cannot be recollected. A food plant holding product pending a pathogen negative loses money by the hour. A contractor waits on seven- and 28-day cylinder breaks before stressing a slab. A device launch waits on a chamber booking and a retest. On top of these sit the regulatory calendars: EPA's PFAS drinking water rule requires initial monitoring by April 2027 at limits of 4 parts per trillion; FDA's LAAF programme has required accredited labs for mycotoxin testing of certain imported foods since December 2024; CPSC has required importers to eFile certificate data naming the testing lab since July 2026; and the FCC has stopped recognising test labs owned or controlled by foreign adversaries, pulling EMC and radio testing toward labs it still trusts. Accreditation itself is a clock: proficiency tests twice a year per field in TNI-based programmes, and reassessment on a fixed cycle.

Routine environmental and food testing is bought on price per sample and turnaround, consolidation by Eurofins, SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, UL Solutions, Element, Pace and Montrose squeezes smaller labs, and the bench is staffed by technicians earning a mean of about $49,000 (chemical technicians, OEWS). Turnover there makes the senior reviewer's judgement the scarcest asset in the building.

## Current Tech Landscape
Large networks run LabWare, STARLIMS, LabVantage or Thermo SampleManager; small labs increasingly buy cloud LIMS such as QBench and CloudLIMS at roughly $170–425 per user per month. Chromatography runs in Waters Empower, Thermo Chromeleon and Agilent OpenLab or MassHunter; EMC labs run test automation such as Rohde & Schwarz EMC32; environmental labs deliver electronic data deliverables into EarthSoft EQuIS and dozens of state and client formats; construction materials firms use field and lab packages such as Spectra QEST alongside paper; quality systems sit in MasterControl, Qualio or SharePoint. Each tool is competent inside its boundary. The record that matters most — why a reviewer accepted, reintegrated or rejected a result, joined to what later happened to it in proficiency testing, client complaints and amendments — spans all of them and is owned by none.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the release decision: the moment a senior analyst or reviewer looks at a batch — chromatograms, QC recoveries, a spectrum, a plate — and decides the number can go on a certificate. That decision governs turnaround, defensibility and the lab's exposure to data-integrity findings, and it is captured only as a signature and an audit-trail entry. Three things block fixing it. Accreditation and regulators reward conservatism, so any automation that touches integration or release must be validated and must never alter raw data. Results belong to clients, which keeps labs from pooling their outcome data across accounts. And most of the roughly 5,700 firms (estimate) are small and buy software on price, while the large networks treat review tooling as an internal project. The strongest commercial opening is in the large and mid-size networks where turnaround is the contest — review-by-exception and release tooling sold on hours saved per batch — not in the long tail. The layer around the labs yields one qualifier, and it sells interpretation rather than measurement: product compliance regulatory intelligence vendors (51), whose strongest opening is turning a regulatory change into the list of products that need retesting before the effective date. Proficiency testing providers are the best clean near-miss at 49; forensic failure analysis scores 51 but is held back here by attorney work product.
