# Investigation & Background Screening Firms

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561611 — Investigation and Personal Background Check Services
**Market Size:** ~$6.9B in receipts across 3,767 employer firms in 2022, of which the 32 firms with 500+ employees took ~$3.19B (~46%) and 2,806 firms with under five employees took ~$658M (Census SUSB 2022); the Service Annual Survey puts 2022 employer revenue at ~$7.85B. First Advantage alone reported $1.57B of 2025 revenue after absorbing Sterling, and HireRight $722M in 2023, its last public year. Screeners filed under other codes (payroll resellers, tenant screeners, some platforms) and thousands of nonemployer investigators sit outside the figure, so the code understates the activity.
**Tech Maturity:** Medium and split — the national screening firms run API-driven platforms that clear most candidates without a person touching the order, while the court searches, verifications and adjudications those platforms wait on, and nearly all field investigation, still run on court runners, phone calls, case notes and an investigator's memory of a subject.
**Workforce:** Court researchers and court runners, employment and education verification specialists, adjudication and FCRA compliance analysts, and client-service teams at the screening firms; licensed private investigators doing surveillance, claims and field investigation, desk investigators working databases and social media, corporate due diligence analysts with language skills, and litigation investigators locating and interviewing witnesses. BLS counted ~40,700 employees in 5,780 private establishments in 2024 at an average pay of ~$63,600, and 39,500 private detectives and investigators across all industries in 2025, only 18% of them in administrative and support services.

## Key Pain Themes
Two businesses share the code, and both sell a finding about a person. The screening firm answers whether a candidate has a reportable criminal record, a verified job history and a valid licence. Most of that answer is assembled from court indexes that were built for clerks, not for identity matching: a record carries a name and, where the court still shows it, a date of birth. When California's Court of Appeal ruled in *All of Us or None v. Hamrick* (2021) that screeners could not search court indexes by date of birth, and Los Angeles Superior Court later removed month and year of birth altogether, some checks there took weeks or months. Each match is the firm's liability: FCRA §613 requires either notice to the candidate or "strict procedures" ensuring public-record information is complete and up to date, and FCRA suits of all kinds ran at roughly 670–930 filings a month in 2025.

After the report come the employer's clocks. A pre-adverse action notice with a copy of the report, a waiting period (customarily five business days), then a final notice; in California, an individualized assessment and at least five business days for the candidate to respond, five more if they dispute the record; in unincorporated Los Angeles County, a further ordinance since September 2024. NELP counts 37 states and over 150 cities and counties with ban-the-box rules, 15 states and 22 localities covering private employers. The CFPB's 2024 opinion on sealed records and missing dispositions, and its 2021 opinion on name-only matching, were withdrawn in May 2025, but state law and plaintiffs' lawyers kept the same standards alive.

The investigation business works under different clocks. An insurer that believes a claim fraudulent must report it to California's Fraud Division within 60 days; a workers' compensation surveillance assignment is ordered ahead of an independent medical exam or a hearing; a litigation investigator must find and interview a witness before discovery closes; a due diligence report must land before a deal signs or a board appoints. The experienced surveillance investigator knows which claimants move in the morning and which never leave the house on a weekday; the experienced researcher knows which county's index drops dispositions. Neither judgement is written down.

## Current Tech Landscape
The national screeners — First Advantage (with Sterling since October 2024), HireRight (private since June 2024), Checkr, Accurate, Cisive and several hundred smaller agencies, many running on platforms such as Tazworks, Accio Data or ClearStar — pull county court records through direct integrations and court-runner networks, buy incarceration and booking data (Equifax paid $1.825B for Appriss Insights in 2021), verify employment through The Work Number and degrees through the National Student Clearinghouse, and plug into applicant tracking systems for adverse action letters. Investigators work in case management tools such as Trackops and CaseFleet, search licensed people-data products (TLOxp, Accurint, CLEAR, idiCORE, whose maker red violet reported 10,022 customers at end-2025), capture social media with OSINT tools, and deliver reports and video to insurers, TPAs and law firms by portal and email. Corporate due diligence firms add registries, litigation searches abroad and screening data such as World-Check. Each tool is competent inside its boundary. The record that links a finding to whether it was right — whether the matched record belonged to the candidate, whether the surveillance video changed the claim — is owned by none of them.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is whether a finding is right and whether it mattered. Screening firms measure turnaround because employers renew on it, and they learn about accuracy only through disputes and lawsuits; investigation firms measure hours billed and video obtained, and rarely learn whether the evidence changed a claim or a case. The obstacles are legal (FCRA accuracy and adverse-action duties, state fair-chance laws, licensing and permissible-purpose rules on people data), structural (the assignment data belongs to the insurer, the law firm or the employer, and litigation work is commissioned through counsel) and organisational (2,806 of the 3,767 employer firms have fewer than five employees, and the field investigator's read of a subject never reaches a database). The strongest commercial opening is accuracy and workflow tooling sold to the large screeners and to [[industries/property-casualty-insurers|insurers']] special investigation units, which buy surveillance and set the billing caps. As a buyer for a research-insight engine the industry is thin: its best-scored pocket, [[niches/hr-consultants/background-screening-providers/profile|Background Screening Providers]], is already indexed under another industry, and the new sweep adds no qualifier: the investigation firms' large teams are field, desk and case staff — production, not insight functions — and the corporate investigators who do run analytical teams work on client-confidential engagements, often through counsel.
