# Money Service Businesses

## Profile
**Category:** Financial Services
**NAICS 2022:** 522390 — Other Activities Related to Credit Intermediation (the check cashing, money order and money transmission activity in the code; its third-party loan-servicing activity is not covered by any built or queued industry)
**Market Size:** ~$86B a year in household transfers sent abroad from the US (BEA, 2024), most of it through licensed money transmitters; Western Union reported ~$4.05B of global revenue and Remitly $1.6B on $74.9B of send volume in 2025; US MSB fee and FX revenue including check cashing is ~$10–14B (estimate); FinCEN counted 26,276 registered MSBs in March 2023
**Tech Maturity:** Medium, and split in two — app-first transmitters such as Remitly, Wise and Xoom run modern risk and pricing stacks, while the agent-network side still runs on a terminal at a grocery counter, small MSBs run their BSA programme on one vendor tool and spreadsheets, and licence maintenance across 49 states lives in NMLS, a calendar and outside counsel.
**Workforce:** Clerks and owners at independent agent locations (grocery stores, travel agencies, phone shops) that often sell two or three brands from one counter; tellers at check-cashing stores; agent-oversight officers and field compliance auditors; BSA/AML analysts and financial intelligence investigators; licensing and regulatory reporting staff; treasury, FX and settlement staff; corridor, pricing and payout-partner managers; customer service

## Key Pain Themes
A money service business sells a licensed promise: this cash will arrive in Lahore, Oaxaca or Mogadishu by tomorrow, or this payroll check will become cash now. Most of that promise is delivered through other people's stores. Western Union reports that about 360,000 locations transacted in the past year, roughly 90% of them outside the US, and every large transmitter depends on independent agents who are legally the transmitter's responsibility but are seen only through transaction logs, a periodic site visit and complaints. The central judgement in the industry is which agents are careless, captured or complicit. Western Union's 2017 resolution — a $586M forfeiture, 26 of its agents convicted, and prosecutors' finding that its own suspension guidelines would have triggered action against more than 2,000 agents between 2004 and 2013 — is the industry's reference case for what it costs to make that judgement late. Experienced agent-oversight investigators make it by reading an agent's pattern and "just knowing"; almost none of that reading is recorded in a form a model could learn from.

The work runs on clocks set by regulators who do not coordinate with each other. A transmitter holds a licence in almost every state, each with its own net worth, surety bond and permissible-investment rules, although 35 states had adopted the Money Transmission Modernization Act in full or in part by August 2026. The NMLS MSB Call Report is due within 45 days of each quarter end. FinCEN registration renews every two years. Suspicious activity reports on transactions of $2,000 or more are due within 30 calendar days of initial detection under 31 CFR 1022.320, currency transaction reports above $10,000 are mandatory, and FinCEN's 2025 Southwest Border Geographic Targeting Order briefly pushed the CTR threshold to $200 in 30 ZIP codes before courts enjoined it. Since 1 January 2026 transmitters also collect a 1% federal excise tax on cash-funded transfers, deposit it semi-monthly and report it quarterly on Form 720. Multistate examination teams arrive on their own schedule and ask for the evidence behind all of it.

Commercially, price is public and margins hide in FX. The World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide put the global average cost of sending $200 at 6.49% in Q1 2025, against a G20 target of 3%, and app-based senders compete by showing the landed amount on comparison sites corridor by corridor. Underneath sits a survival risk that small operators feel first: Treasury's 2023 De-risking Strategy named small and medium MSBs among the customers banks most often drop. A corridor specialist serving US senders to Pakistan — a corridor worth about $3.72B in Pakistan's FY2025 inflows — runs its whole business on one or two bank accounts and a treasury desk that prefunds payout partners every morning.

## Current Tech Landscape
The large transmitters run proprietary send platforms, agent terminals and monitoring; smaller MSBs license white-label send platforms and buy compliance tooling. Alessa sells MSB-specific transaction monitoring with CTR and SAR population, Unit21 combines monitoring and case management, and Hummingbird provides case management and SAR filing on top of other monitoring. Sanctions screening data comes from Dow Jones, LexisNexis and LSEG World-Check, identity checks from vendors covered under [[industries/identity-verification-vendors|Identity Verification Vendors]], and card-funding fraud scores from the vendors under [[industries/payment-fraud-vendors|Payment Fraud Vendors]]. Licence maintenance runs on NMLS, spreadsheets and licensing counsel. Pricing intelligence comes from FXC Intelligence, which tracks more than 25,000 corridors and about 700 providers and counts Western Union, MoneyGram, Remitly and Intermex as clients, and from the World Bank's price database. Each tool is competent inside its boundary. The agent file — monitoring alerts, field visit notes, complaint and fraud reports, sweep returns and the investigator's conclusion about one agent location over years — is the record that decides the transmitter's biggest risk, and no tool owns it.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is agent risk: whether a location's pattern of transfers reflects a busy store in an immigrant neighbourhood, a careless clerk, a store captured by a fraud ring, or an owner who is complicit. Experienced investigators make that call from transaction logs, field visits and instinct, and the outcome — a conviction, a terminated agent, an enforcement action years later — is rarely joined back to the signals they read. Three things block fixing it. SAR confidentiality forbids telling an agent, or most counterparties, that a report was filed, so the label set cannot leave the transmitter. Agents sell several brands from one counter, so no single transmitter sees an agent's whole activity. And regulators are fragmented across FinCEN, the IRS's BSA examiners, the CFPB for larger participants and every state, each demanding its own evidence. The strongest commercial opening is in the handful of large transmitters — Western Union, MoneyGram, Ria, Intermex and Remitly — who can buy agent-risk, scam-interdiction and corridor-pricing tooling and have the data to train it. The thousands of small MSBs are poor buyers one at a time, but they are a real market through the sponsor banks and white-label platforms they depend on. As a home for a research-insight engine the industry is weak: the transmitters' own insight is filed with FinCEN, not invoiced, and the clean, insight-rich data around them — sanctions lists, regulatory intelligence — has already been indexed under other industries.
