# Credit Card Issuers

## Profile
**Category:** Financial Services
**NAICS 2022:** 522210 — Credit Card Issuing
**Market Size:** ~$1.26T in US card balances at Q2 2026 (New York Fed), on which consumers were assessed $160B of interest in 2024 (CFPB); firms classified to 522210 reported $142.1B of receipts in 2022 (Census SUSB), a floor because most universal-bank card lending is booked under commercial banking
**Tech Maturity:** High at the decision, manual at the edges — the largest issuers run some of the most sophisticated credit and fraud modelling in any industry, while disputes, hardship conversations, first-party fraud investigation, co-brand economics and regulatory notices still lean on queues, spreadsheets and the judgement of long-tenured staff.
**Workforce:** Credit risk and decision-science analysts, fraud strategists and investigators, dispute and chargeback analysts, collections and hardship agents, rewards and partnership finance teams, model risk and compliance officers, and large customer service operations; 65,357 people worked in 522210 establishments in 2024 (BLS QCEW), with many more inside bank card divisions

## Key Pain Themes
A card issuer is a lender that makes millions of small, repeated credit decisions — approve, set a line, raise it, cut it, authorise a transaction over limit, offer a hardship plan — and observes the outcome of each one month by month. The outcome data is excellent; the labels are not. When an account charges off, someone decides whether it was a credit loss or a fraud, and first-party fraud — the cardholder who builds a clean record and then maxes the line and walks away — is routinely booked by collections as ordinary credit loss. The credit models then learn from fraud labelled as default, the fraud models never see it, and the investigators who can recognise a bust-out in the making carry that pattern recognition in their heads. Synthetic identities alone represented an estimated $3.3B of lender exposure across cards and other consumer credit at end-2024 (TransUnion).

Around the decision sit external clocks that do not move. Reg Z gives an issuer two complete billing cycles, and never more than 90 days, to resolve a billing error, and cardholders disputed $9.8B of charges in 2024 (CFPB), each of which must be classified, evidenced and either charged back to the merchant inside the network's window or written off. Reg B requires specific, accurate reasons within 30 days for every decline and line decrease, including those made by machine-learning models. The CARD Act sets 45-day notices for rate increases and a six-monthly re-review of every increase made. Quarter-ends set the loss allowance, and the largest issuers file account-level card data to the Federal Reserve for stress testing.

Then there is rewards economics. Rewards cards carry 92% of general-purpose purchase volume, and the cost of a point depends on how and when it is redeemed, which transfer partner it goes to and how much is never redeemed at all. Co-brand partnerships concentrate this: Delta booked $8.2B of remuneration from American Express in 2025, and when a partner rebids — Apple Card is moving from Goldman Sachs to JPMorgan Chase over roughly two years from January 2026 — the incumbent and the bidder are each pricing a portfolio's lifetime value on different data.

## Current Tech Landscape
Card processing runs on FIS Total Issuing Solutions (the former TSYS, acquired January 2026), Fiserv Optis, and newer platforms such as Galileo, Marqeta, i2c and Pismo; underwriting and line strategies run in decision engines such as FICO Platform, Experian PowerCurve and Provenir, on FICO and VantageScore scores and bureau attributes. Authorisation fraud is scored by FICO Falcon, Visa's tools (now including Featurespace) and in-house models; disputes are worked in case-management tools such as Quavo and the networks' own portals; collections run on FICO Debt Manager and similar platforms; rewards run on vendor or in-house loyalty engines. Each system is competent within its boundary. The record that ties an account's decisions to their outcomes — why the line was set, why the dispute was paid, whether the charge-off was fraud, what the rewards actually cost — spans all of them and is owned by none.

## Analysis
Card issuing is among the most measured businesses in consumer finance: every account is a running experiment with monthly outcomes, and the largest issuers have built their franchises on testing. The central unmeasured judgement is not the credit decision but the label beneath it — whether a loss was default or fraud, whether a dispute was genuine or first-party misuse, whether a hardship plan cured an account or delayed its charge-off, whether a line increase earned its loss. Those labels are assigned by investigators, dispute analysts and collectors working under clocks, and the patterns the best of them recognise are rarely captured. What blocks fixing it is partly structural (fraud, credit and collections report to different executives and book losses in different places), partly legal (every model change passes model risk governance and fair-lending review, and every decision needs a reason a regulator accepts) and partly commercial (the largest issuers build in-house and treat their data as their moat). The issuer itself is a poor buyer for a research-insight engine under the Pass 2 rubric: its insight is monetised as interest and interchange, not invoiced, and cardholder data sits under GLBA and FCRA. The strongest commercial pockets sit beside it — consortium benchmarking, network consulting and fraud consortia — and none clears the bar on its own; the opening for operators is label capture inside the issuer's own fraud, disputes and collections workflows.
