# Payment Processors

## Profile
**Category:** Fintech
**Market Size:** ~$120B US merchant acquiring and processing revenue, concentrated but with persistent share movement toward platform acquirers
**Tech Maturity:** Extremely high on the rails, surprisingly low on the decisions — Stripe, Adyen, Block, Braintree, Checkout.com, Worldpay and Fiserv operate globally distributed authorisation infrastructure at four or five nines, then make the decisions that determine merchant revenue (whether to retry a decline, when, through which route) with static configuration files.
**Workforce:** Payments engineers, integration and solutions engineers, merchant underwriting and risk analysts, settlement and reconciliation operations, interchange and network relationship managers, dispute specialists, developer support

## Key Pain Themes
The single number that decides whether a merchant stays is the authorisation rate, and the processor controls only part of it. An issuer declines for reasons it does not disclose — insufficient funds, suspected fraud, an expired credential, a velocity rule, or its own risk model — and returns a code from a small vocabulary that is used inconsistently across thousands of issuers. The processor can retry, route differently, use a network token, refresh the credential through account updater, or stop. Every one of those is a prediction, and the result of that prediction lands in a settlement file two days later in a different system.

Around it sit the structural burdens: merchant onboarding and underwriting, where every acquirer independently rebuilds KYB, sanctions screening and risk scoring against the same registries; reconciliation, where money movement across networks, currencies, fee schedules and settlement timetables has to be matched and rarely matches cleanly; and a support function whose job is debugging integrations written by somebody else's engineers.

## Current Tech Landscape
Authorisation runs over the card networks with an increasing share via network tokens and, for larger merchants, direct issuer connections. Smart routing and decline recovery are standard product claims across the platform acquirers and largely rule-driven underneath. Fraud screening is either in-house (Stripe Radar, Adyen's risk engine) or bought (Forter, Signifyd, Sift). Onboarding uses Middesk, Persona, Alloy and sanctions vendors. Reconciliation is a mixture of vendor tooling and very large amounts of internal code. The observability is excellent at the infrastructure layer and thin at the decision layer, which is the inverse of where the money is.

## Analysis
A large processor observes something no issuer and no merchant can: the same cardholder, across thousands of merchants, being approved and declined by hundreds of issuers, with the eventual settlement outcome of every attempt. That is the empirical basis for questions the industry answers with folklore — which decline codes from which issuer are worth retrying, on what schedule, through which route, for which merchant category. The information is generated by the network as a by-product and is used mainly for billing. The competitive frontier in acquiring has moved from price to authorisation performance, and authorisation performance is a prediction problem that the industry currently treats as a configuration problem.
