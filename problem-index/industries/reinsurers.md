# Reinsurers

## Profile
**Category:** Insurance
**NAICS 2022:** 524130 — Reinsurance Carriers
**Market Size:** ~$785B of global reinsurer capital at end-2025, a record: $649B traditional and $136B alternative (third-party) capital; reinsurers' average combined ratio was 88.5% in 2025 (Aon, April 2026). The catastrophe bond market reached a record almost $65.6B outstanding at end-H1 2026 (Artemis). In the US, NAICS 524130 counted 1,192 private establishments and 30,896 employees in 2024 at an average pay of $171,573 (BLS QCEW), a count that includes life and accident-and-health reinsurers
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — catastrophe modelling is among the most quantitative work in finance and every property treaty is priced through licensed vendor models, while submissions still arrive as broker spreadsheets and PDFs weeks before the renewal, wordings are compared by reading, and the underwriter's adjustments to the model output live in pricing workbooks and memory.
**Workforce:** Treaty and facultative underwriters, catastrophe modellers and exposure analysts, pricing and reserving actuaries, casualty loss-trend analysts, claims and technical accounting staff, contract wording specialists, retrocession buyers, capital and rating agency teams, and the portfolio managers and analysts of reinsurer-sponsored sidecars and insurance-linked securities vehicles

## Key Pain Themes
NAICS 524130 also covers life and accident-and-health reinsurers, such as RGA and the US life arms of Munich Re and SCOR. Their work is outside this build. The vault covers it only from the carrier side, through [[niches/life-insurance-carriers/life-reinsurer-underwriting-research/profile|🔍 Life Reinsurers' Underwriting Research & Mortality Studies]] and [[niches/life-insurance-carriers/inforce-block-acquirers/profile|🔍 In-Force Block Acquirers & Closed-Block Consolidators]]. This hub covers the property-casualty side: treaty, facultative, retrocession and insurance-linked capital. A reinsurer prices a promise to pay its clients' worst years. Most of the price is set in a few weeks. US and European property catastrophe programmes renew on 1 January, Japan on 1 April, Florida on 1 June and much of Australia and the rest of the US on 1 July. In the run-up, cedents' brokers send submissions containing location-level exposure, prior losses and the cedent's own modelled results. The reinsurer's cat team re-runs that exposure through one or more licensed models (Verisk, Moody's RMS, KCC) and then the experienced underwriter does what the model cannot. They load the result for the cedent's data quality, insured values they think are understated, claims-handling culture, non-modelled perils and the litigation climate in the cedent's states. Experienced cat underwriters say they know which cedents' data flatters them. That adjustment, the "own view of risk", moves price more than any other input. It is recorded as a load in a workbook, rarely with its reason, and it is graded only when a large event hits that cedent, which may be never.

The market around that judgement has swung hard. The Guy Carpenter US property catastrophe rate-on-line index fell 12% at 1 January 2026 and 16% for the year after the mid-year renewals, the largest annual fall since 2014, as capital hit a record. Softening puts every load under pressure from brokers. Casualty runs the other way. Swiss Re estimated that social inflation raised US liability claims by 57% over a decade, and casualty treaty pricing now depends on trend and development assumptions that reinsurers fit from cedents' ceded triangles and large-loss listings. Those assumptions are tested years later, when adverse development arrives.

After an event the clock moves to claims and capital. Reinsurers estimate their own loss within days for boards, rating agencies and investors. The January 2025 Los Angeles wildfires drew modelled industry estimates from $20B–$30B (Moody's RMS) to $28B–$35B (Verisk) in the first two weeks. Industry loss indices (Verisk's PCS, PERILS) then settle industry-loss-warranty and cat bond triggers on their own timetables. Collateralised reinsurance and sidecar capital stays trapped until losses develop. Statements of account, cash calls, reinstatement premiums and collateral (Reg 114 trusts, letters of credit) are reconciled cedent by cedent. Since the early-1990s Schedule F revisions, US statutory accounting has charged a cedent's surplus 20% of paid-loss reinsurance recoverables more than 90 days overdue.

## Current Tech Landscape
Catastrophe pricing runs on Verisk Touchstone Re, Moody's RMS Intelligent Risk Platform and KCC, with single-peril specialists (JBA and Fathom for flood, Reask for tropical cyclone, KatRisk) and open frameworks built on the Oasis Loss Modelling Framework and its Open Exposure Data format. Reinsurance broking analytics from Aon, Guy Carpenter, Gallagher Re and Howden Re prepare submissions and market views. Supercede (formerly Riskbook) and others clean and package submission data; Tremor runs programmatic placements; and treaty administration and technical accounting run on systems such as Sapiens' reinsurance platform or in-house builds. Industry loss estimates come from PCS and PERILS, ILS market data from Artemis and the trade press, and capital models from vendor engines plus rating agency templates (AM Best's BCAR, S&P). Each tool is competent within its boundary. None of them records the underwriter's adjustments to the model output, with reasons, joined to what each cedent's programme later lost.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the experienced underwriter's adjustment of the model to the cedent: the loads for data quality, insured-value adequacy, claims culture, non-modelled perils and trend that turn a vendor exceedance-probability curve into a price. Reinsurers measure model output and realised losses meticulously, but rarely record the adjustment as a separate, reasoned decision, so they cannot tell which underwriters' reads carry information or pass them on when a lead underwriter retires. Three things block a fix. Large catastrophe losses are rare and cluster, so any single cedent offers almost no labels. The submissions belong to cedents and arrive under placement confidentiality. And the renewal season leaves no time to document anything. The reinsurer is a strong buyer of pricing, exposure-data and event-response tooling, because the renewal dates and post-event reporting put a date on the spend. Under the Pass 2 rubric it is a poor buyer of a research-insight engine, because its insight is priced into premium rather than invoiced. The firms that invoice for reinsurance insight are the catastrophe modellers, already indexed under another industry, and a set of near-misses: industry loss indices, single-peril modellers, cat bond modelling agents and broker analytics. No reinsurance pocket clears the threshold (see the niche overview). The opening that remains is inside the reinsurer: a graded ledger of own-view-of-risk decisions, and fast, clean submission and event-loss data.
