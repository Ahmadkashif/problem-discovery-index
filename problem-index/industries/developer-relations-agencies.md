# Developer Relations Agencies

## Profile
**Category:** Digital Professional Services
**Market Size:** ~$2B US in developer relations services, community programmes, developer marketing and advocacy-as-a-service, alongside a much larger in-house DevRel function it supports and partly competes with
**Tech Maturity:** Community and content tooling is adequate; measurement is the field's defining unsolved problem, acknowledged openly by its own practitioners. Every conference talk about DevRel metrics concludes that attribution is hard, and the function's budget is cut first in every downturn for exactly that reason.
**Workforce:** Developer advocates and evangelists, community managers, technical content producers, developer experience researchers, programme and event managers

## Key Pain Themes
Developer relations works because developers adopt tools they have heard explained by someone credible, tried in a workshop, or been helped with in a community — and none of those moments carry an attribution parameter. The adoption shows up months later as a self-serve signup or as a name on an enterprise deal that sales sourced, and the advocacy that caused it is invisible.

So the function reports activity: talks given, articles published, community members, event attendance, stars and forks. Everyone involved knows these are inputs rather than outcomes, and the field has argued about better metrics for a decade without settling on any, because the honest answer requires connecting a diffuse influence to a delayed conversion nobody can trace.

The second theme is that advocacy is delivered by individuals whose credibility is personal and portable. The value accrues partly to the employer and substantially to the advocate, which makes the relationship unusual and the succession problem severe — when an advocate leaves, the community relationship frequently leaves with them.

The third is that the work is structurally exhausting: travel, public performance, continuous availability in community channels, and a support load that arrives because developers ask the person who seems approachable rather than the support queue.

## Current Tech Landscape
Community platforms are Discord, Slack, Discourse and GitHub Discussions. Content runs through company blogs, YouTube and conference circuits. Community CRM tools — Common Room, Orbit before its wind-down, Savannah — attempt to connect community activity to product usage and represent the most serious attempt at the measurement problem. Product analytics and self-serve signup data sit on the company side. Developer experience research borrows methods from UX research. Event management is generic tooling. Attribution of developer adoption remains largely unsolved by any product.

## Analysis
Developer relations is a function whose central claim — that credible technical advocacy drives adoption — is almost certainly true and has never been demonstrated in a form that survives a budget review. The evidence exists in pieces: community participation is identifiable, content consumption is measurable, event attendance is recorded, and product usage is tracked, all at the level of individuals or accounts who could in principle be linked. Nobody links them, partly because the join is genuinely difficult across pseudonymous developer identities and partly because doing it properly raises privacy questions the field is right to be careful about. The version that works is measurement at the aggregate and cohort level rather than at the individual, which sacrifices precision for defensibility and is considerably better than the activity counting the field does now.
