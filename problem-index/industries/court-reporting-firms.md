# Court Reporting Firms

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561492 — Court Reporting and Stenotype Services
**Market Size:** ~$3.07B in 2022 receipts at 2,989 US employer firms (3,161 establishments, 12,874 employees, ~$743M payroll; 2022 Economic Census). County Business Patterns counts 3,195 employer establishments in 2023, 89% of them with fewer than five employees. Payroll is under a quarter of receipts because most reporters work as independent contractors, and nonemployer freelancers likely add ~$0.8–1.2B (estimate). California took ~$610M. Official reporters employed by courts sit outside this code.
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — realtime CAT software is mature and national agencies run online scheduling, remote depositions and transcript repositories, but the typical firm is two or three reporters booked by phone and email, transcripts edited overnight by the reporter and a freelance scopist, and a career-long personal steno dictionary as the firm's real asset.
**Workforce:** Stenographic reporters licensed as CSRs in roughly half the states or holding NCRA's RPR; voice writers (California had licensed 219 by May 2025); digital reporters certified by AAERT; scopists and proofreaders; agency schedulers and production coordinators; and CART providers and realtime captioners serving deaf and hard-of-hearing users. BLS counts 19,900 court reporters and simultaneous captioners in 2025 (33% self-employed, 19% in business support services, the rest in state and local government), median pay $72,420.

## Key Pain Themes
The product is a certified verbatim record made by an officer of the court. The noticing party names the recording method (FRCP 30(b)(3)(A)), the officer certifies in writing that the witness was sworn (30(f)(1)), and the deponent gets 30 days to review the transcript (30(e)(1)). Transcripts are sold by the page against clocks someone else set: a rough draft tonight for tomorrow's examination, an expedited copy before an expert-disclosure deadline, daily copy through trial. Federal page prices are capped by turnaround, from $4.40 for 30-day delivery to $8.70 for hourly, plus $3.70 a page for one realtime feed. Freelance same-day surcharges reach 110% in AAA/ICDR's published schedule. Margin lives in copies sold to the other parties and in add-ons, which is why neutrality rules require services offered to one side to be offered to all. Advocates count 16–17 states that restrict insurers' volume contracts with reporting firms.

The labour supply is shrinking, and the fight over what replaces it is political. NCRA's 2013 Ducker study counted ~32,000 stenographic reporters, median age 51. AAERT, the digital reporters' association, now puts the count as low as ~23,000 and estimates that school enrollment fell from 3,083 in 2015 to 790 in 2024. Texas's certified shorthand reporters fell 14% in twenty years while trial benches grew by more than 14%. California went the other way: enrollment at its recognised programs is up 286% since 2017, driven by voice writing, and its skills exam pass mark dropped from 97.5% to 95% in January 2025. Both sides' numbers come from parties with a stake. California's bill to license digital reporters died in February 2026, Texas commissioned a study of digital recording due in October 2026, and NCRA backs a federal bill to oversee AI in courts.

Consolidation and new money sit around both. Veritext, owned by Leonard Green since 2018 and drawing on 11,500+ independent reporters as of 2018, kept buying local firms in 2024–25, as did Lexitas, Magna Legal Services, Esquire and U.S. Legal Support. Steno raised a $49M Series C in March 2026 on a model that lets plaintiffs' lawyers defer reporting fees until the case resolves.

## Current Tech Landscape
Reporters write into CAT software: Stenograph's Case CATalyst (whose Check It tool flags likely errors with Stenograph's Phoenix recogniser), Advantage Software's Eclipse and DigitalCAT, streaming realtime text to counsel's screens. Remote depositions run on Zoom and agency platforms. National agencies offer booking portals, repositories and AI summaries, while small firms book by phone and spreadsheet. Digital reporters annotate multi-channel recordings and send the audio to transcriptionist pools. Legal videographers synchronise video, covered under [[niches/video-production-smb/legal-video-specialists/profile|Legal Video Specialists]]. Each tool owns its step. Nobody owns the record of accuracy itself: what the reporter wrote stroke by stroke, what the software translated, what the reporter and scopist changed, which passages were read back or interrupted, which errata the witness filed, and whether any page was later challenged.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the reporter's split-second call on what was said: stop the room and ask, or write through and recover the passage later, then certify the result under licence. CAT files record every stroke and edit, so this judgement is unusually well logged, yet nobody pools or grades it. Three things block a fix. Legally, the record belongs to the proceeding and the transcript to the parties who ordered it, neutrality rules forbid selling one side what the other cannot buy, and certification is personal liability. Structurally, 89% of establishments have fewer than five employees and the stroke files sit on contractors' laptops. Politically, any accuracy measurement becomes ammunition in the stenographic-versus-digital licensing fight. The strongest commercial opening is selling capture-agnostic accuracy evidence and production tooling to the national agencies and CAT vendors. The operators are poor buyers for a research-insight engine: they sell certified text, not insight. The Pass 2 sweep finds no qualifying pocket. The nearest are expert-testimony databanks (42) and court data analytics already indexed under [[industries/personal-injury-law|Personal Injury Law]].
