# Electric Distribution Utilities

## Profile
**Category:** Utilities & Energy
**NAICS 2022:** 221122 — Electric Power Distribution
**Market Size:** ~$514B of US retail electricity revenue in 2024 (EIA Electric Power Annual, all sectors; includes the generation cost that distribution utilities collect and pass through); investor-owned utilities alone plan more than $1.1T of capital spending in 2025–2029 (EEI)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the meters, outage management and SCADA systems are digital and generate enormous data, but distribution planning, interconnection screening, rate case preparation and storm logistics still run on study reports, spreadsheets, phone calls and the judgement of a few senior engineers.
**Workforce:** Distribution planning and protection engineers, interconnection and new-service designers, distribution system operators in the control room (about 9,040 power distributors and dispatchers nationally, BLS May 2023), lineworkers (about 57,100 of the ~120,170 US power-line installers work for utilities), meter technicians and AMI analysts, utility foresters, customer service representatives and credit staff, rate and regulatory analysts, and storm logistics coordinators

## Key Pain Themes
After two decades of flat demand, load is growing again. Data centres used about 4.4% of US electricity in 2023 and are projected at 6.7%–12% by 2028 (LBNL for DOE), and heat pumps, EV chargers and new factories add requests on top. Every new large load and every rooftop solar, battery or EV-charger application lands on a distribution planning group sized for a quiet era. A senior planner can look at a feeder and say within minutes whether it can take another 5 MW. The utility's formal answer takes a load-flow study that can run weeks to months. Developers file speculative requests at several utilities at once, and the utility cannot tell the real ones from the placeholders. Hosting capacity maps exist for some feeders in some states (58 utilities and agencies in 26 states by May 2024, per DOE), but they are refreshed rarely and rarely match what the full study later finds.

All of this is paid for through a rate case. A utility proves its revenue requirement before a state commission: test-year costs, plant in service, cost of capital, a cost-of-service study and rate design. Intervenors then serve hundreds or thousands of data requests, and witnesses defend the filing under cross-examination on a procedural schedule the commission sets. Requests hit a record in 2025: nearly $31B of electric and gas increases, more than double 2024 (PowerLines). With affordability now political, every capital dollar has to be shown to be prudent. Unpaid bills, payment arrangements and disconnections fall on the customer service floor, where representatives explain a higher bill to people who can't pay it.

The third clock is the weather. US customers averaged 11 hours without power in 2024, and 80% of that came from major events such as Hurricanes Beryl, Helene and Milton (EIA). Restoration depends on staging the right number of crews before landfall through regional mutual-assistance groups, then documenting every crew-hour well enough to recover the cost. In the West, wildfire has turned vegetation management and line hardening into regulated plans with their own reviewers. California's Office of Energy Infrastructure Safety approves or denies each utility's 2026–2028 base plan after public comment.

## Current Tech Landscape
Distribution planners model feeders in Eaton CYME, DNV Synergi Electric, Siemens PSS SINCAL or EPRI's OpenDSS. Operators run outage management and ADMS platforms from GE Vernova, Oracle, Schneider Electric and Hitachi Energy. Meter data flows from Itron, Landis+Gyr and Aclara networks into meter data management systems, and billing runs on Oracle Customer Care & Billing, SAP IS-U or co-op systems such as NISC. GIS (Esri ArcGIS with ArcFM or Utility Network) holds the connectivity model, and it is often wrong about which transformer serves which meter. A newer layer is selling AI into these gaps. Gridsight, Kevala and envelio sell grid capacity and connection analytics, GridUnity and PowerClerk interconnection workflows, AiDash and Overstory vegetation risk from satellites, Buzz Solutions and Noteworthy AI asset inspection, Neara network digital twins, EnergyHub and Bidgely DER and meter analytics. E Source and the large consultancies sell analytics engagements. Each tool owns a slice. No tool owns the record that joins a planner's quick judgement to the study that followed and to what was actually built, energised or failed. The equipment itself (transformers, wire, meters) arrives from manufacturers and through [[industries/electrical-distributors|Electrical Distributors]], where lead times rather than software set the pace.

## Analysis
The central unmeasured judgement in a distribution utility is the planner's read of grid capacity: which feeder, transformer bank and substation can take a new load or generator, and what it will really cost to make room. Thousands of study reports hold that judgement's outcomes, but they sit as PDFs, never joined to the feeder data or to what was later built and energised. Three things block a fix. Network models are utility property and often protected as critical infrastructure information, so pooled cross-utility models are hard. Commissions reward documented process over speed, so a utility has little reason to answer faster than its tariff requires. And the people who could label the data are the overloaded planners themselves. The commercial opening is real but sits beside the operators. Large IOUs buy slowly, through procurement and rate-case justification, while most of the ~2,900 co-ops and munis (estimate, from APPA's shares) have no planning department and buy through their generation-and-transmission cooperatives and associations. Vendor demand is the faster route: Gridsight, E Source and Buzz Solutions are hiring forward deployed engineers to put this kind of tooling into utilities now. Public data supports a narrower demonstration than the planner's read. Hosting-capacity maps give feeder-level limits where utilities publish them: 58 utilities and agencies in 26 states by May 2024, rarely refreshed. EIA-861 and FERC Form 1 add utility-level customers, sales, DER and plant, and EIA-860 adds generators of 1 MW and up. No public source holds study outcomes, so a demo is hosting-map ingestion plus territory context. Under the Pass 2 rubric the utilities themselves are poor buyers for a research engine: their analysis is not invoiced, and the pockets around them either work on utility-owned data or sell into markets of a few firms.
