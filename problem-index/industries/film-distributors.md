# Film Distributors

## Profile
**Category:** Media, Telecom & Information
**NAICS 2022:** 512120, 512199 — Motion Picture and Video Distribution; Other Motion Picture and Video Industries
**Market Size:** ~$1.4B in 512120 receipts (2022 Economic Census) plus ~$426M for 512199 (2022 Service Annual Survey). The code undercounts the activity, because the majors' distribution arms are classified with production: the Census reports $12.6B of domestic film-rights licensing revenue for production and distribution together (2022). US/Canada box office was ~$8.9B in 2025 (AMC FY2025 10-K, on Comscore's industry figure); Box Office Mojo's calendar table shows $8.66B across 670 releases, because it covers fewer of the reported grosses than Comscore's industry total
**Tech Maturity:** Low to Medium. The larger distributors run rights systems such as Rightsline. Most firms keep avails in spreadsheets, settle film rent by email and decide acquisitions in a festival hotel room
**Workforce:** Acquisitions executives, theatrical sales and booking staff, business affairs and contracts managers, rights and avails coordinators, servicing and delivery coordinators, marketing and publicity, royalty and participations accountants, and international sales agents. On the 512199 side: footage researchers and licensing staff at stock and archival libraries, non-theatrical booking agents, and film laboratory and restoration technicians (that last slice is analysed under postproduction studios as [[niches/postproduction-studios/film-scanning-and-restoration/profile|Film Scanning, Restoration & Library Remastering]])

512120 covers the firms that buy the right to exploit a finished film and license it out by territory, medium and window. 512199 sits beside it as the same business at other scales. Stock-footage and film libraries license clips from collections they own, by the second, the use and the term. Booking agencies license public performance in schools, libraries and other non-theatrical venues, which is a distribution window the theatrical system skips. Film laboratories and restoration houses make the masters and theatrical prints that distribution ships ("reproduction of motion picture films for theatrical distribution" is a 512199 index entry). Their scanning and restoration work is a finishing craft and is analysed with [[industries/postproduction-studios|Postproduction Studios]] rather than here. The industry is small and fragmented: County Business Patterns counted 493 establishments and 2,525 employees under 512120 in 2023, about five people per establishment.

## Key Pain Themes
The central decision is the bid. An acquisitions executive watches a film once, usually at Sundance, Berlin, Cannes or Toronto with a paying audience in the room. Within a day or two of a bidding war they decide what the film will earn across theatrical, premium VOD, subscription, ad-supported, television and international windows, and what to pay against that. The answer rests on comparable titles and on what experienced buyers call loving the film. The outcome arrives over 18 to 36 months, spread across licensees and statements, and is never joined back to the reasoning behind the bid. The bid is getting harder to price. A strong independent film once sold 20 to 35 international territories and now typically closes ~10 to 14, with advances down 30 to 70% from the mid-2010s (FilmTake estimate). Theatrical windows have shrunk to 17 to 45 days, and indie streaming licences can be as low as $1,000 to $10,000 a title (Litwak).

Every licence adds a row to a rights grid: territory, medium, window, term, exclusivity, language, holdbacks and reversion date. A library of a few hundred titles carries thousands of such grants, written in prose across long-form agreements, deal memos and amendments. Questions like "is this title free for ad-supported streaming in Benelux from March?" are answered by a coordinator reading contracts. Mistakes license the same right twice or let a reversion pass unnoticed. Libraries are getting bigger and cheaper: Cineverse reports rights to over 71,000 titles and episodes, an appraisal put its digital library at ~$40M, and its book value is ~$2.5M.

Money moves through three manual ledgers. Film rent is negotiated "film-by-film and theatre-by-theatre" (AMC's 10-K) and settled engagement by engagement after each run. Statements from platforms, sub-distributors and theatres are then rolled into quarterly participation statements for producers, with fees, recoupable expenses and cross-collateralisation. Producers distrust them: a 2015 Writers Guild East survey reported that ~60% of indie filmmakers had trouble getting paid (estimate, from press summaries). Each licensee also demands its own delivery package (masters, captions, cue sheets, chain of title, an E&O certificate) before it pays. External clocks govern all of it: the festival and market calendar (Sundance, the European Film Market, Cannes, Toronto, AFM), the Academy's seven-day qualifying run and its Best Picture expanded run in ten of the top 50 markets within 45 days, and the window expiries, reversion dates and statement deadlines written into contracts.

## Current Tech Landscape
At the enterprise end, rights and royalties software has consolidated. Rightsline bought FilmTrack (nearly 200 entertainment customers) in 2024 and RSG Media later the same year, and its strength is multi-territory, multi-window avails queries over structured grants. Most independent distributors run Excel, a contract folder and an accounting package. Box office is reported through Comscore. Pre-release tracking and exit polling come from NRG, Screen Engine/ASI's PostTrak and Greenlight Analytics, which bought The Quorum in 2025. Parrot Analytics and Luminate sell streaming demand and title-valuation estimates. Collection account managers such as Fintage House and Freeway administer the money waterfall on financed films. Archives index their footage with keyword metadata typed by staff. Each tool is competent within its boundary. No tool owns the record that joins the acquisition bid to the rights granted, the revenue each window actually produced and the statement the producer was sent.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the price of a film's rights: what a title will earn by territory and window, decided by a few experienced buyers on one viewing and confirmed or refuted years later in statements nobody lines up against the bid. Three things block fixing it. Teams are tiny: distributors describe themselves as "a lean machine of three", and the 512120 code averages about five employees per establishment. Feedback is slow and confounded by marketing spend, release dating and the films the firm didn't buy. The data needed to grade a bid is scattered across contracts, licensee statements and settlement emails, and much of it is confidential to counterparties. For an AI or research-workflow vendor, the operators are poor direct buyers. The near-term opening is the mechanical layer that every distributor shares: rights extraction and avails, settlement and statement reconciliation, licensee re-servicing and archive search. It sells to the few mid-sized library holders and through the rights-software layer. The bid ledger is the larger prize but needs years of disciplined capture. The insight pockets around the industry (tracking firms, demand vendors, participation auditors) hold the outcome data the operators lack, but none clears the Pass 2 threshold.
