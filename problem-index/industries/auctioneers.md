# Auctioneers

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561990 — All Other Support Services (the independent auctioneer slice: firms that auction estates, equipment, real estate and consignments for others on commission). The real estate broker-auctioneer slice, classed with offices of real estate agents and brokers, is included because the same firms run land and property auctions under a broker licence
**Market Size:** ~$4–6B a year in commissions, buyer's premiums and fees on ~$30–50B of gross sales through U.S. independent auction firms (estimate); the whole of NAICS 561990, of which auctioneers are one slice, had 10,864 firms and $31.0B of receipts in 2022 (Census SUSB)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — online bidding became near-universal after 2020 and clerking software is cheap, but the decisions that make or lose money (what a lot will bring, whether to set a reserve, how to lot a house, what to say about condition) run on the auctioneer's memory and a quick search of sold prices.
**Workforce:** Owner-auctioneers (licensed in roughly half the states), ringmen and bid spotters, cataloguers and photographers, clerks and cashiers, set-up and load-out crews, category specialists (antiques, firearms, coins, machinery), real estate brokers who run land and property auctions, and personal property or machinery appraisers

## Key Pain Themes
Every consignment starts with one question at a kitchen table, in a machine shed or in a bankruptcy trustee's office: what will it bring? The auctioneer answers with a number, usually within minutes, from memory of what similar things sold for and a feel for who will be bidding. That number decides whether the auctioneer wins the consignment against a competitor promising more, whether the sale runs absolute or with reserve, whether a guarantee or advance is offered, and how much is spent on marketing. It is graded by the hammer a few weeks later, lot by lot, and almost no firm records the estimate in a form that can be compared with the result. The firms that overpromise win consignments and then lose sellers; the firms that underpromise lose the consignment to them.

Between the handshake and the sale sits the cataloguing grind. An estate house can hold 300 to 1,500 lots (estimate), each to be sorted, lotted, photographed, described and categorised for an online platform whose search and category rules differ from the next one's, usually against a hard date set by someone else: the real estate closing on the house, the probate court's timeline, the lender's liquidation deadline, the bankruptcy court's sale order, or the spring planting that ends the farm auction season. Condition language is free text, written fast, and is where most post-sale disputes start.

After the close comes money. Proceeds must be collected from bidders, some of whom never pay or never collect, then settled to each consignor net of commission, buyer's premium, sales tax and charges, through a trust or escrow account the state regulates. Florida, for example, requires a written agreement with the owner before the sale, deposit of unsettled proceeds into an escrow account within two working days and monthly reconciliation. Licensing boards, recovery funds and bonds exist because consignor money has gone missing before.

## Current Tech Landscape
Bidding moved online through Proxibid, BidSpotter and LiveAuctioneers (all under Auction Technology Group, which reported $12.1B of hammer value across its marketplaces in FY25), HiBid (built on Auction Flex clerking software), Invaluable, and Sandhills' AuctionTime and Equipmentfacts for machinery. The equipment market is led by RB Global (Ritchie Bros. and IronPlanet, $5.7B of construction and transport GTV in 2025), Purple Wave and BigIron; real estate by Auction.com and CoStar's Ten-X; government surplus by Liquidity Services' GovDeals. Clerking and settlement run on Auction Flex, Wavebid, AuctionMethod, Maxanet or Bidpath. Comparable prices come from WorthPoint, the Artnet Price Database, LiveAuctioneers' free price results and eBay sold listings; equipment values from EquipmentWatch, Sandhills and RB Global's Rouse. Each tool does its job. None holds the record that matters: what the auctioneer said a lot would bring, with what condition description, and what it actually brought or failed to bring.

## Analysis
The auctioneer's central judgement is a price forecast with a fast, unambiguous label: the estimate given to win a consignment is graded by the hammer within weeks, and passed or unsold lots are graded too. That makes the industry's most valuable data asset unusually easy to build and almost never built, because the estimate is spoken at the kitchen table, scribbled on a contract or kept in the owner's head, while the hammer lives in the clerking system and on the bidding platform. Academic work on the major art houses found their estimates for expensive works biased upward for decades (Mei and Moses, 2005); at independent firms nobody has measured at all. The blocks are structural: firms of a few people with no analyst, an estimate that is a sales pitch as much as a forecast, and comparables scattered across platforms the auctioneer does not control. The operators are reasonable buyers for cataloguing and settlement tools priced per sale, and poor buyers for an insight engine. The research-grade work around them sits with the platforms that hold the hammer archives, the international houses' specialist departments, and the asset-based lending appraisers that both value collateral and liquidate it. The last of these is the strongest pocket, and it trips a confidentiality kill switch on the borrower data it values.
