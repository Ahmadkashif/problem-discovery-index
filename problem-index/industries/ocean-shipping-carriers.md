# Ocean Shipping Carriers

## Profile
**Category:** Transportation & Logistics
**NAICS 2022:** 483111 — Deep Sea Freight Transportation
**Market Size:** 241 US-based firms with $9.9B of receipts in 2022, 11 of them holding ~74% (Census SUSB); the global trade they serve moved 12,720 million tons by sea in 2024 (UNCTAD), and the largest carrier alone, Maersk, reported $54.0B of revenue for 2025
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the top container lines run large in-house platforms, booking portals and DCSA-standard APIs, while shipping instructions, demurrage disputes, chartering, cargo claims and the ship's own reporting still move as email, PDF and spreadsheet.
**Workforce:** Shore staff in trade and pricing desks, customer service and documentation teams (often in offshore shared-service centres), network, stowage and bunker planners, chartering and operations managers, claims handlers, port captains and marine superintendents; at sea, masters, deck and engine officers and ratings. US employment in the code was 9,876 in 2024 (BLS QCEW), concentrated in Florida, California, Texas, Virginia and New York.

## Key Pain Themes
A liner sails on a published weekly schedule whether it is full or not, so every sailing is a perishable inventory decision. Each week a trade desk decides how much space to sell at contract rates and how much to hold for spot, how far to overbook against bookings that will not turn up, and which cargo to roll when the ship is oversold. Maersk once put its global no-show rate at about 20% of bookings and 35–40% on some trades (2011 statement). The decision is made by experienced trade managers reading booking pace, customer habits and competitor blank sailings, and it is almost never recorded with its reasons. Its outcome is recorded perfectly, in loaded TEU and revenue per slot, but the two are never joined.

Around that decision sits a paper flow under hard external clocks. Shippers and forwarders send booking requests and shipping instructions as emails and PDFs. Documentation agents key them into the carrier's system to draft a bill of lading before the SI cut-off. Carriers have pledged to issue 50% of bills of lading electronically within five years and 100% by 2030 (DCSA), from roughly 1.2% in 2021 (search summary). In US trades the Federal Maritime Commission's billing rule (46 CFR 541, effective 28 May 2024) voids any demurrage or detention invoice issued more than 30 days after the charge stops accruing, or one missing a required data element, and gives the billed party 30 days to dispute. The EU ETS now prices shipping emissions: verified reports are due by 31 March and allowances by 30 September, covering 70% of 2025 emissions and 100% from 2026. FuelEU Maritime added a GHG-intensity limit in 2025 (search summary). Adoption of the IMO Net-Zero Framework was postponed in October 2025 (search summary), and this build found no report of the October 2026 outcome.

The network itself is unstable. Global schedule reliability fell to 49.9% in August 2026, the lowest since September 2022, with late vessels averaging 6.81 days behind (Sea-Intelligence). After two years of Cape of Good Hope diversions, 63% of Far East–Europe capacity was back on Red Sea routings by mid-October 2026 (Alphaliner). Each re-routing reshuffles port rotations, free time, bunker plans and every customer's arrival date at once. Industry profit fell from about $35.4B of EBIT in 2024 to $15.4B in 2025 (Sea-Intelligence, search summary), and Maersk's 2026 guidance assumes overcapacity.

## Current Tech Landscape
The big carriers build their own booking, pricing and operations systems and expose DCSA-standard track-and-trace, booking and eBL interfaces; booking portals such as INTTRA (E2open) and CargoSmart aggregate requests from forwarders. Tramp operators run chartering and voyage management on Veson IMOS (Veson bought Shipfix and VesselsValue in 2023), alongside Signal Ocean, Sedna and Marcura for email, laytime and voyage costs. Vessel performance and emissions reporting come from ZeroNorth, StormGeo, Kongsberg and the class societies' platforms, and rate benchmarks from Xeneta, Freightos and Drewry. Container visibility comes from project44, Portcast and Vizion. Each tool covers its slice well. The record that no tool owns is the decision record: why a booking was accepted, priced, overbooked or rolled, why a ship was sped up or slowed down, and why a disputed charge was waived, joined to what happened next.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the weekly allocation call: which cargo to accept, at what price, and how far to overbook a sailing that leaves on Tuesday regardless. Outcomes are fully observed within days and decisions are not recorded at all, so a carrier cannot tell which desks, customers or overbooking habits earn money. Three things block the fix. The judgement is organisational, held by regional trade managers who resist being scored. The data is structural, scattered across booking, pricing and operations systems that were never joined. And the market is a small club: ten carriers hold most container capacity, so a vendor has few buyers and each builds in-house. The paper side is the opposite. Shipping instructions, bills of lading, demurrage invoices and emissions reports are high-volume, rule-bound documents with hard regulatory clocks (FMC's 30-day windows, the EU's 31 March and 30 September dates). That is where outside vendors are hiring. In San Francisco, Pallet has an agent-delivery engineer role open and Reform and Flexport have forward deployed engineer roles. project44 is hiring a senior FDE in Chicago, Portcast an ML engineer in Singapore, and Sedna delivery engineers in London and Athens. A shipping-instruction-to-draft-B/L agent is the clearest two-week demo. Under the Pass 2 rubric the carriers are poor research buyers: their insight is spent on their own ships, not invoiced. The commercial insight pockets sit beside them, in research houses, valuers and rate publishers, and none clears the bar here.
