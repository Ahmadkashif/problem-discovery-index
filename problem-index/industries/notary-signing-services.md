# Notary & Signing Services

## Profile
**Category:** Professional Services
**NAICS 2022:** 541120 — Offices of Notaries (nominally 541120; covers the notary-public and signing-agent services Census files under All Other Legal Services, which [[industries/legal-support-services|Legal Support Services]] carves out to this hub; see Workforce)
**Market Size:** ~$2-3B a year in US notary and signing fees (estimate, derived from ~5.4M mortgage loans in 2025 and ~5.8M forecast for 2026 by the MBA, plus seller, estate, general and remote acts); about 4.4 million commissioned notaries (NNA 2022 Notary Census), most of whom notarise incidentally for an employer
**Tech Maturity:** Low-Medium — appointments arrive through Snapdocs and signing-service portals and remote online notarization is legal in most states, but the act itself is still a person inspecting a plastic ID card, watching a signature and writing a line in a bound paper journal.
**Workforce:** Independent mobile notaries and loan signing agents paid per signing (most of them sole proprietors, many part-time), signing-service coordinators who take orders from title, escrow and lenders and dispatch agents, notaries staffing remote online notarization platforms, notary offices and shipping-store notaries handling walk-in acts, and apostille facilitators; civil-law notaries in Louisiana and Puerto Rico, who draft as well as execute acts, are the closest fit to the Census definition of 541120, which puts notaries who only certify signatures under 541199 instead

## Key Pain Themes
The notary is the only person in a real estate or estate transaction whose job is to decide that the signer is who they say they are, and that decision is made in about a minute by looking at a driver's licence across a kitchen table. It is increasingly the point of attack. ALTA's 2026 survey found ~59% of title firms saw at least one seller impersonation attempt in 2025, up from 28% a year earlier, and sellers asking for a remote signing or their own notary were the second most common red flag. When an impostor gets through, the deed records, the proceeds are wired and the real owner discovers it months later; the claim lands on the title insurer and the suit lands on the notary, whose journal entry is often the only record of what was checked. Experienced signing agents describe a feel for the wrong signer: the ID that is too new, the signer who does not know the property, the "nephew" who answers every question. Nobody has captured it.

Around that judgement sits a production job with hard clocks. A loan signing agent accepts an order for a fixed ~$75-150 fee (estimate), prints a ~100-150 page package on dual trays, drives to the borrower, walks them through it without giving legal advice, collects every signature, initial and date, scans the pages back within the hour and drops the package at the courier before the cutoff. A missed initial on a deed of trust can force a redraw and put the rate lock at risk; the closing date, the TRID disclosure timing and the three-day rescission window on refinances are all set by someone else. The agent absorbs the travel, the printing and the blame.

The record-keeping is set by fifty different statutes. California alone requires a sequential journal kept locked under the notary's exclusive control, the signer's ID details or credible witnesses, and a right thumbprint for any deed, deed of trust or power of attorney; other states require no journal at all. Remote online notarization adds identity proofing by knowledge-based authentication and credential analysis, session recordings retained for years, and a destination county or court that may or may not accept the act.

## Current Tech Landscape
Snapdocs is the dominant order marketplace for loan signings, free to agents and paid by title companies and lenders, alongside directories such as Notary Rotary, 123notary and NotaryCafe. The National Notary Association sells training, the voluntary Notary Signing Agent certification and the annual background screening that title companies require under Signing Professionals Workgroup standards. Remote sessions run on Proof (formerly Notarize), NotaryCam, BlueNotary, OneNotary, NotaryLive and DocuSign Notary, with identity proofing to the MISMO standard. Journals are mostly paper; e-journal apps such as Notary eJournal and NotaryAssist exist for states that allow them. Signing services run on spreadsheets, Snapdocs and email. Title search, escrow and the closing itself are analysed under [[industries/title-and-settlement-offices|Title & Settlement Offices]], including the title office's own seller-verification and file-risk checks. The adjacent software category is analysed under [[industries/esignature-document-workflow|E-Signature & Document Workflow]] and the identity technology under [[industries/identity-verification-vendors|Identity Verification Vendors]]. The record no tool owns is the evidence of the act: who appeared, what identity evidence was examined and judged, and how that entry ties to the instrument that was later recorded or challenged.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the identity decision: a commissioned notary looks at a person and a credential and decides, under personal liability, that they match and that the signer is acting knowingly and freely. Nobody measures how often that decision is wrong, because the outcome surfaces months or years later in a title claim, a probate contest or a criminal case that is never joined back to the journal line. Three things block a fix. Structurally, the notary is a sole proprietor with a fee cap of $10-15 an act in states such as Florida and California, so the person holding the judgement cannot fund a tool. Legally, each state prescribes the identity methods, journal fields and retention rules, and some still mandate knowledge-based authentication for remote acts. Organisationally, the parties who bear the loss — title insurers, lenders and the defrauded owner — sit outside the industry and treat the notary as interchangeable. The commercial opening is therefore not the notaries themselves, who are poor buyers, but the title companies, signing services and platforms that route the work and pay the claims: an identity-evidence record captured at the table, graded against later outcomes, is worth more to them than to the agent who creates it.
