# Wired Broadband Providers

## Profile
**Category:** Media, Telecom & Information
**NAICS 2022:** 517111 — Wired telecommunications carriers (cable operators, fiber and copper telcos, broadband ISPs and, by exception, direct-to-home satellite TV distributors)
**Market Size:** ~$322.7B in 2022 receipts across 3,403 firms and 28,993 establishments (2022 Economic Census), of which cable and program distribution was ~$195.3B (a figure that also holds the direct-to-home satellite TV distributors DIRECTV and DISH, which NAICS places in this code and which this build names but does not analyse), wired telcos ~$117.5B and stand-alone broadband ISPs ~$9.9B; receipts include video, voice and business services as well as residential broadband, which reaches an estimated ~105–110M wired households
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the large cable operators run DOCSIS networks that report detailed health telemetry from every modem, while the long tail of rural telcos, co-ops and small fiber ISPs keeps its network records in Google Earth, spreadsheets and the memory of two or three senior technicians.
**Workforce:** Installers and maintenance technicians, line technicians and fiber splicers, network operations centre engineers, outside-plant engineers and records staff, customer care and retention agents, regulatory and compliance managers, and construction managers overseeing contractor crews. County Business Patterns counts 593,911 employees in 2023, with 16,510 of 28,618 establishments under five people and 37 over 1,000.

## Key Pain Themes
Most of what goes wrong in a wired network is shared. A loose connector at a tap, water in a splice closure or a cracked hardline span degrades service for every home downstream, but the customer experiences it as "my internet is slow", calls, and gets a technician sent to their house. A senior maintenance technician can look at a modem's pre-equalization taps, a full-band spectrum capture or an OTDR trace and say which span or connector is failing; a junior technician replaces the customer's modem and closes the ticket. Regulators already measure the result: Windstream reports repeat trouble reports to Texas against a 22% corrective-action benchmark, and cable operators file trouble-report rates per 100 lines in California. The fault read that would prevent the repeat is never recorded in a form anyone can learn from.

Around the network sit hard external clocks. Every facilities-based provider files its availability, location by location against the FCC's Broadband Serviceable Location Fabric, twice a year (the June 30, 2026 data was due September 1, 2026, the same day as Fabric challenges). Providers that took subsidies carry location-level build obligations: RDOF required 40% of committed locations by December 31, 2024 and 60% by December 31, 2025, and Lumen walked away from more than 41,000 locations in 2025. BEAD subgrantees, now approved in all 56 states and territories, have four years from their subgrant to serve every funded location. Pole attachment rules changed again on May 8, 2026, and copper retirement rules on March 26, 2026.

The commercial clock is churn. Fixed wireless from T-Mobile and Verizon took roughly all of the industry's net additions in 2023 (Leichtman Research Group), fiber overbuilders are entering cable footprints, and the Affordable Connectivity Program's end in 2024 pushed payment-constrained households off the books; Charter alone lost 119,000 Internet customers in Q4 2025. Every promotional rate that rolls off is a decision about whether to discount, and most operators make it without knowing what the household can actually buy at its address.

## Current Tech Landscape
Access equipment and subscriber platforms come from Calix (about 1,600 service-provider customers), Nokia, Adtran, Harmonic and Vecima; proactive network maintenance analytics from in-house MSO teams and vendors such as ZCorum built on CableLabs methods; billing and OSS from CSG and Amdocs at the top and NISC, Sonar, gaiia, Powercode and Azotel in the long tail; network records from Vetro FiberMap, 3-GIS, OSPInsight and Esri; field service from ServiceNow and Salesforce; Fabric licences from CostQuest; and speed-test data from Ookla. Each is competent inside its boundary. The record that joins a symptom, the diagnosis, the repair and whether the symptom went away, across telemetry, ticketing and the plant map, belongs to none of them.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is where a fault is. Operators measure truck rolls, repeat rates and minutes of outage with precision, and they hold more telemetry per customer than almost any other utility, but the diagnosis that connects a symptom to a fix lives in senior technicians' heads and in resolution codes picked from a dropdown at the end of a shift. Fixing that is blocked organisationally (maintenance, installation and care are separate departments, and installation is often contracted out), by data (plant maps are wrong, and resolution codes don't say what was really repaired) and by turnover, because the technicians who can read pre-equalization taps are retiring. The large cable operators build this in-house. The commercial opening is in the long tail of roughly 1,600–3,000 smaller operators (estimate), reached through the equipment, billing and network-records vendors they already buy from, with a second, regulation-driven opening in location-level compliance: BDC filings, subsidy milestones and BEAD evidence on fixed federal dates. As a buyer of a research-insight engine the operator fails the Pass 2 gate. Its insight is spent on its own network, and the firms that sell analysis about the industry fall short for different reasons: subscriber trackers and speed-test vendors are markets of two or three firms, cost-study consultants work on each carrier's confidential books, and feasibility consultants write bespoke studies whose biggest window, BEAD, has closed. None clears the threshold.
