# Broadcast TV & Radio Stations

## Profile
**Category:** Media, Telecom & Information
**NAICS 2022:** 516120, 516110 — Television Broadcasting Stations; Radio Broadcasting Stations
**Market Size:** ~$50B in 2025 (estimate): S&P Global put total US TV station revenue, advertising plus retransmission fees, at ~$40.4B in 2024 and ~$37.6B in 2025, and BIA with the RAB put 2025 local radio revenue, over-the-air plus digital, at ~$12.3B. The FCC counted 1,390 commercial full-power TV stations, 387 educational TV stations, 397 Class A and 1,772 low-power TV stations, 4,300 AM and 11,366 FM stations (6,560 commercial, 4,806 educational) at 30 June 2026.
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — playout, traffic and billing are fully digital and increasingly hubbed across a group, but inventory pricing, retransmission economics, public-file compliance and transmitter care still run on the judgement of a few senior people, spreadsheets and paper logs.
**Workforce:** ~121,850 people (BLS OEWS, May 2023): producers and directors, announcers and DJs, local account executives and sales managers, reporters, broadcast technicians and chief engineers, traffic directors and continuity staff, master-control operators, program directors and music directors, and general managers. Radio (516110) sits beside TV because the business is the same at its core: an FCC licence to a frequency in one market, a public inspection file, a transmitter that must stay on the air within its licensed parameters, and an audience sold to local and national advertisers by the spot against Nielsen ratings. Radio differs in three ways: it has no retransmission fees, its ratings come from Nielsen Audio's 48 meter (PPM) markets and 200+ diary markets rather than TV's Big Data + Panel, and its programming is music scheduled or voice-tracked rather than bought from syndicators and networks.

## Key Pain Themes
A station sells a perishable product. Every 30-second unit that airs unsold, or is given away as a makegood, is gone. The general sales manager and the inventory or traffic manager decide every day what each daypart in each week is worth, how much to hold back for late local direct business, scatter and, in even years, political money, and which orders to preempt when a daypart is oversold. The 2026 midterms are projected at $11.6B in political spending, $5.6B of it on broadcast TV (AdImpact), so that call is worth most in exactly the weeks the station has least time to make it. The read is done by feel. Unit rates, sell-out and preemptions are recorded to the penny in the traffic system, but the decision behind them is not, so no station can say whether its holds were right, and the GSM's judgement leaves with the GSM. The national side of the same inventory is sold by reps, analysed under [[media-rep-firms|Media Representative Firms]]. This industry is the inventory owner.

For TV, the second revenue line is distribution. BIA put local TV retransmission fees at ~$14.3B in 2024, heading to ~$15.6B by 2027. That is roughly 35% of TV station revenue (estimate, against S&P Global's ~$40.4B for 2024), and a large share is passed on to the networks as reverse compensation (estimate). Commercial stations had to elect must-carry or retransmission consent by 1 October 2026 for the cycle running from 2027 to 2029. Groups then negotiate with cable, satellite and virtual distributors under a blackout threat, and collect monthly against subscriber counts the distributors report themselves. Few stations audit those counts system by system.

Under both sits a compliance and engineering floor with clocks the station does not set. Quarterly issues/programs lists are due in the online public inspection file by the 10th of January, April, July and October, with a $10,000 base fine for late or missing lists. The political file must be kept current through every election window. The 2025 rescission of ~$1.1B in Corporation for Public Broadcasting funding took grant money from more than 1,500 public stations. And the engineers who keep transmitters legal are retiring faster than they are replaced: one industry projection has 6,200 retirements against 5,100 needed hires over a decade. A chief engineer now often covers several stations and transmitter sites alone.

## Current Tech Landscape
Traffic, billing and ad management run on WideOrbit and Imagine Communications' Landmark Sales in TV, both now owned by Constellation Software's Lumine Group (WideOrbit since 2023, Imagine since mid-2026). Radio runs mostly on Marketron, WideOrbit and RCS. Playout and automation come from Imagine, Grass Valley, ENCO, WideOrbit and RCS Zetta, and music scheduling from RCS Selector and MusicMaster. Newsrooms use AP ENPS, Avid iNEWS or Ross. Ratings come from Nielsen (Local TV, and Nielsen Audio for radio), with Comscore and VideoAmp as TV alternatives. Prospecting draws on Vivvix and MediaRadar, and market share on Miller Kaplan's pooled revenue reports. The FCC's own online public file system holds compliance documents. Transmitter sites report through Burk, Davicom or the transmitter maker's remote-control and monitoring systems. Each tool is competent within its boundary. None owns the record that matters most to the station: the pricing and hold decision on each unit of inventory, joined to what that unit finally earned.

## Analysis
The central unmeasured judgement is inventory yield: what each daypart-week is worth, how much to hold for buyers who have not yet called, and what to preempt when demand arrives. The outcome is perfectly recorded and the decision is not, which is the same shape the vault finds in hedge funds' theses and media reps' pricing. Three things block a fix. Organisationally, rate decisions sit with a GSM paid on revenue who has no reason to have them graded. Structurally, the data is split between the traffic system (orders, rates, preemptions), the rep's systems (national demand) and the political file. Commercially, a market where Lumine now owns both leading TV traffic systems leaves little room for a stand-alone pricing layer that needs deep access to the log. The strongest openings sit with the station groups. Nexstar alone held more than 200 stations before the FCC approved its TEGNA combination in March 2026 by waiving the national ownership cap, so a group's central revenue, distribution and engineering teams can buy once for hundreds of logs, retrans contracts and transmitter sites. They can buy a yield model per market, a subscriber-report audit across every distribution agreement, public-file automation and remote transmitter monitoring that stretches each engineer further. As buyers of a research-insight engine, the stations are poor: their analysis serves their own selling and programming and is never invoiced. Pass 2 finds no new qualifier. The insight businesses that matter most to stations, political ad tracking, audience measurement and music performance licensing, are already profiled under other industries.
