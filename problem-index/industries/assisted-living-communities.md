# Assisted Living Communities

## Profile
**Category:** Healthcare & Clinical
**NAICS 2022:** 623312, 623311 — Assisted Living Facilities for the Elderly (state-licensed residential care with help with daily activities and medications, including memory care) and Continuing Care Retirement Communities (life plan campuses combining independent living, assisted living, memory care and often a nursing unit, usually under an entrance-fee contract)
**Market Size:** $78.4B of 2022 employer-firm revenue across NAICS 6233, of which assisted living facilities took $37.3B and CCRCs ~$41.1B (Census Service Annual Survey via FRED; CCRC share derived). NCAL counts ~41,465 assisted living communities with nearly 1.4M licensed beds and an average of 33, 57.3% of them chain-affiliated. About 1,016,400 people lived in residential care communities on a given day in 2022, ~17% of them Medicaid beneficiaries (NCHS). The 2025 national median price was $6,200 a month (CareScout)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — chains run a CRM, an EHR or eMAR and a billing system (Aline, Yardi, PointClickCare, August Health), but move-in assessments still start on paper, care levels are re-scored by a nurse every few months, incidents go to the state by fax or portal, and two in five communities are independents averaging a few dozen beds with almost no office staff.
**Workforce:** 923,770 employed in NAICS 623300 (BLS OEWS, May 2023): home health and personal care aides (245,370, mean $16.87/hr; resident assistants and caregivers), nursing assistants (146,190), food servers, cooks and housekeepers (~158,000 combined), LPNs (41,760) and RNs (30,300) working as wellness nurses and directors, recreation workers (24,270), medical and health services managers (12,760, mostly executive directors), plus medication technicians, sales counselors and maintenance staff

## Key Pain Themes
Assisted living is mostly private pay, so it sells itself twice: once to a family at move-in, and again every month after. The family usually arrives after a crisis, often a fall or a hospital stay, and often through a referral agency. A Place for Mom lists ~14,000 communities and is paid, by its own description, the resident's "first month's rent and care" for each placement (Senate Aging Committee letter, 2024). The bill a resident pays has two parts. Base rent is set by a street rate the operator moves with occupancy, now 89.1% for assisted living in NIC MAP's 31 primary markets (Q3 2026). On top sits a level-of-care fee set by a nurse's assessment. Operators make money when the care they bill matches the care their aides actually give, and lose it in the months between reassessments as a resident declines. Large operators bill ~$6,000–6,700 per occupied unit a month (Welltower SHO RevPOR $6,059, Brookdale $6,670, Q2 2026).

The residents are frailer than the name suggests. In 2022, 53% were 85 or older, 44% had Alzheimer's or another dementia, and 62% needed help with three or more daily activities (NCHS). Memory care is where the risks concentrate. The Washington Post counted more than 2,000 residents who had wandered away from senior living since 2018, 98 of whom died (2023). Unwitnessed falls are routine. Staffing is the largest cost, 56.2% of operating budgets, and total turnover ran at 35.4% in 2026, with caregivers covering ~10–15 residents in assisted living and ~5–8 in memory care during the day (Ziegler CFO Hotline). The judgement that matters most belongs to the wellness nurse. At the pre-move-in assessment she reads, from a visit and a family's account, how much care this person will really need and whether the community can keep them safe.

The clocks are mostly state clocks. There is no federal certification, survey or star rating for assisted living. Each state licenses, inspects and sets its own incident-reporting deadlines, and 18 states, counting DC, changed their rules in 2025 alone (NCAL). Medicaid pays for services, never room and board, for ~200,000 residents, through home and community-based waivers and state plan benefits; 41 of 47 states responding to KFF's survey cover such services in assisted living (KFF, 2025). Each waiver resident brings a service plan, a reassessment date and a monthly claim. Life plan communities add an entrance-fee contract whose refund promises must be covered by actuarial reserves, which some states review every three to five years. Capital adds a clock of its own. Welltower and Ventas own senior housing under RIDEA structures and report operating results quarterly: Welltower's SHO same-store NOI grew 20.5% in Q2 2026, and Ventas raised its 2026 senior housing investment target to over $4.5B.

## Current Tech Landscape
Aline, formed from Enquire, Glennis and Sherpa, sells a CRM, clinical, dining and billing suite and publishes sales benchmarks from its customers' data. Yardi Senior Living, PointClickCare Senior Living and August Health compete for the EHR, eMAR and move-in record. Sunbound runs billing and collections for assisted living and skilled nursing. Falls and room sensing come from SafelyYou (AI video and post-fall review), Inspiren (AUGi sensors and e-call, which raised $70M in September 2026) and Teton.ai. Referral agencies (A Place for Mom, Caring.com) and NIC MAP occupancy data sit outside the building. The engineering employers confirmed this week on their own boards are Inspiren, SafelyYou, Sunbound and Aline (see the source log). What no tool owns is the record joining the nurse's assessment, the care aides actually logged, the fee billed, and what followed: a fall, a move to memory care, a citation or a move-out.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the wellness nurse's read of true care need: at move-in, and again each time a resident slips. It sets the price, the staffing, and the decision to keep a resident or move them on. Almost none of it is recorded in a way anyone can learn from. Care logs are entered at the end of a shift, reassessments run on a calendar rather than on change, and nurse turnover (40.8% for RNs in Ziegler's 2026 survey) takes the best judges away. Structure, not technology, blocks the fix. Each state writes its own rules, so a multi-state product is fifty compliance products. Families, not payers, judge quality, so outcome data is thin. And the 42.7% of communities that are independent, in an industry whose average community has 33 licensed beds, buy little software. Operators are decent buyers only for tools that pay back in occupancy, billed care or avoided citations within a year. The strongest openings are two: vendors already funded and hiring engineers into the care record (Inspiren, SafelyYou, Sunbound, Aline), and products built on public state inspection reports and NIC MAP-style market data sold to chains, REITs and insurers. The Pass 2 sweep finds no qualifying insight pocket. The analytical labour around the industry is small, works on clients' resident and operating records, or sits inside REITs and operators that invoice nothing, much as in [[industries/skilled-nursing-facilities|Skilled Nursing Facilities]].
