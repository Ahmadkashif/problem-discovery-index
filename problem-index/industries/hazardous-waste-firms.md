# Hazardous Waste Firms

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 562211, 562112 — Hazardous Waste Treatment and Disposal (incinerators and combustors, treatment plants, hazardous landfills, hazardous medical and radioactive waste treatment); Hazardous Waste Collection (local hazardous hauling and transfer stations)
**Market Size:** ~$12.4B in 2022 receipts (Census SUSB 2022): treatment and disposal took $9.30B across 393 employer firms, 928 establishments and 29,927 employees, and collection $3.08B across 320 firms, 482 establishments and 9,273 employees. The 13 enterprises with 5,000+ employees took $5.00B (~54%) of treatment and disposal receipts, while 288 firms under 20 employees shared $440M (~5%). The codes understate the consolidators: Clean Harbors alone reported $6.03B of 2025 revenue ($5.19B in Environmental Services), much of it field, industrial and parts-washer work classified elsewhere, and Republic puts the US and Canada "broader environmental solutions industry" at $37B.
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the consolidators run central profile-approval systems, receiving labs and incinerator feed controls, and EPA's e-Manifest system has taken ~13.5 million manifests since 2018, but EPA-led cleanups use fully electronic manifests at 20 times the national average, which says how much of the rest still starts on paper.
**Workforce:** CDL hazmat drivers (tankers, vans, roll-offs), field and lab-pack chemists, waste approval (profile) chemists, receiving-lab technicians, incinerator, stabilisation and landfill operators, hazardous materials technicians and emergency response crews, permit and EHS managers, manifest and billing clerks, and technical sales reps. OEWS counts 16,670 heavy truck drivers, 5,540 hazardous materials removal workers and 600 chemists in waste treatment and disposal (May 2023, landfills included).

## Key Pain Themes
Every hazardous waste load starts as a document. Before a drum or tanker moves, the generator (a plant, lab, hospital or store) must describe the waste on a profile: the process that made it, safety data sheets for its ingredients, analytical results where generator knowledge is not enough, the RCRA waste codes, and the DOT shipping description. A waste approval chemist at the receiving facility decides whether that profile fits the facility's permit and Waste Analysis Plan and which treatment train it goes to. When the load arrives, the receiving lab samples it and fingerprints it against the profile; one EPA training example has the lab sampling a tenth of the containers on every shipment and every bulk load (search summary). A mismatch becomes a discrepancy, a rejected or re-routed load, a re-profile and sometimes an enforcement problem for both sides. Handlers ship ~2 million manifested loads a year (EPA), so the gap between what the paperwork says and what is in the drum is the industry's daily risk.

Capacity sets the price. Commercial incineration is scarce and concentrated: Clean Harbors runs ten commercial incinerators with 631,721 tons of practical capacity and ran all but the new Kimball unit at 89% utilization in 2025, while Veolia's $3B purchase of Clean Earth, closed in June 2026 (search summary), effectively doubled its US hazardous waste revenue. A broker put the 2023 incineration backlog at 50,000–60,000 tons (search summary). Republic, which bought US Ecology in 2022 (search summary), operates six hazardous landfills, 24 TSDFs and 15 deep injection wells. PFAS is new volume with unsettled rules. EPA's 2026 interim guidance lists permitted hazardous waste combustors, RCRA Subtitle C landfills and Class I injection wells as the lower-release options but sets no requirements, and EPA said it would keep and defend the 2024 CERCLA designation of PFOA and PFOS; it is under court challenge (search summary). Generators therefore buy outlets as much as service, and the outlet they choose decides their long-term CERCLA exposure.

Third parties set the clocks. Large quantity generators file a Biennial Report by 1 March of each even-numbered year, and small quantity generators re-notified by 1 September 2025 and do so again in 2029 (search summary). The third e-Manifest rule moved exception, discrepancy and unmanifested waste reports into the system, required small and large generators to register, and moved export manifests into it from 1 December 2025. EPA charges $5 for an electronic manifest and $25 for a scanned paper one from October 2025, and it plans rules to end paper. Retail and healthcare add their own exposure: Subpart P for hazardous waste pharmaceuticals is in force in 37 states (search summary), and Walmart paid $7.5M in October 2024 after California counties ran more than 70 waste audits of its stores. GAO counts more than 1,000 facilities that treat, store or dispose of hazardous waste, each carrying closure and post-closure financial assurance that Clean Harbors says typically runs 30 years after closure.

## Current Tech Landscape
TSDFs and transporters run specialist ERPs for profiles, orders, receiving and billing (Terralink's TDX and TDXConnect, WASTELINQ, Wastebits and EnviroWare (search summary)), and the consolidators build their own portals on top. Receiving labs keep results in LIMS. Generators track waste in EHS platforms (Enablon, Cority, Sphera, VelocityEHS) or spreadsheets, and retailers classify SKUs for disposal through chemical data vendors such as 3E and UL's WERCS. EPA's e-Manifest system, a module of RCRAInfo, holds the national record of shipments, and manifest data is public 90 days after receipt (search summary). Fleet telematics come from the same vendors other carriers use. Each tool works inside its boundary. None of them joins the profile, the fingerprint result, the discrepancy and the treatment outcome into one history per waste stream and per generator, which is the record an approval chemist carries in her head.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the approval chemist's read of a waste profile: whether this generator's description of this stream can be trusted, which treatment it really needs, and which loads will arrive as something else. Experienced approval and receiving staff know which plants under-describe, which process changes shift a stream and which profile wording predicts trouble, but the record that would test that knowledge is split between the profile system, the LIMS, the e-Manifest discrepancy report and the incinerator's feed log. Three things block a fix. Generator data arrives under service terms and the generator, not the TSDF, certifies the profile. Permits and Waste Analysis Plans fix the acceptance procedure, so any model changes how chemists rank work, not the rules. And the market is concentrated: Clean Harbors and Veolia own most of the commercial hazardous waste incinerators, Clean Harbors, Republic and WM most of the hazardous landfills, and all of them build their own systems; WM's Healthcare Solutions runs medical-waste incinerators, a separate market. The commercial opening is on the generator side and in the regulated documents everyone shares, such as profiles drafted from safety data sheets, Biennial Reports reconciled to e-Manifest and store-level classification under Subpart P and state retail enforcement, plus the ~650 collectors and treaters with fewer than 500 employees (356 and 303 in the two codes, SUSB 2022) that buy software. Public e-Manifest and RCRAInfo data make a free starting dataset for generator-status and flow analytics. Under the Pass 2 rubric nothing around the industry qualifies: the richest data belongs to generators or to consolidators that do not sell analysis, and the firms that do sell analysis are already profiled elsewhere or too small.
