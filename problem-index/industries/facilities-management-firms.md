# Facilities Management Firms

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561210 — Facilities Support Services
**Market Size:** ~$31.2B in US employer-firm revenue in 2022 (Census Service Annual Survey via FRED), from 9,019 establishments with 283,836 employees (County Business Patterns 2023, as reported). The code understates integrated facility management: CBRE's Building Operations & Experience segment alone booked $23.2B of revenue in 2025, $12.5B of it pass-through costs, and much of that sits under real estate and janitorial codes (estimate). The code also covers contract operation of jails, which belongs to correctional facilities, not here
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the large integrated providers run enterprise CMMS and national service desks, but the work at each site still runs on the building engineer's memory, PM checklists ticked on a phone, subcontractors' PDF reports and a monthly KPI pack assembled in Excel.
**Workforce:** Multi-craft maintenance technicians (general maintenance and repair workers are the largest trade occupation in the code, 12,990 in OEWS May 2023), building and stationary engineers and the chief engineer, HVAC mechanics and electricians, self-delivered janitorial and grounds crews, facility managers and account directors, service-desk coordinators, subcontract and vendor managers, EHS and compliance coordinators, and on the bid side solution designers and pricing analysts; on federal base contracts, program managers, quality control staff and the people who answer CPARS evaluations

## Key Pain Themes
A facilities management firm sells a building that works, at a price fixed before anyone knows what the building costs to run. An integrated facility management (IFM) bid for a corporate portfolio or a military installation is priced from the client's asset list, the incumbent's spend, a site walk and the provider's rate cards, then locked for three to five years. The real maintenance load surfaces only in transition, when the inherited asset list turns out to be wrong and the reactive work-order volume does not match the RFP's history. From then on the account team decides, service by service and region by region, what its own technicians will do and what goes to subcontractors. Those decisions are made by habit, and the margin rides on them: ABM's Business & Industry segment earned a 7.7% operating margin in FY2025, and more than half of CBRE's 2025 Building Operations & Experience revenue was pass-through cost. Every subcontractor invoice has to be checked against a work order, a rate card and a not-to-exceed before it is rebilled with markup.

The knowledge that keeps a building running sits with a few people. The chief engineer knows which chiller surges on a hot afternoon, which air handler's belt to watch and which "no heat" calls are really a thermostat a tenant has covered with a box. The CMMS records the work order as "completed" with no cause, and PM checklists are closed under KPI pressure whether or not the work was done, which the trade calls pencil whipping. When an account changes hands the site staff are often rebadged to the new provider. The knowledge moves with the person, never with a system.

External clocks run underneath all of it. SLA response windows of a few hours govern every priority call. Statutory inspections of sprinklers, fire alarms, elevators, boilers and generators recur on code-set calendars, and the deficiencies they find have to close into repairs. Hospitals face unannounced Joint Commission surveys in which Life Safety and Environment of Care standards are the most-cited chapters. EPA's refrigerant leak-repair rule has applied to appliances holding 15 lb or more of HFC since 1 January 2026, and New York's Local Law 97 emissions reports are due every 1 May. Federal base-operations contractors are rated in CPARS and have 14 days to answer an evaluation before source selection officials can see it, and they must reprice to every new Service Contract Act wage determination within 30 days. GAO estimated a $285B DOD maintenance backlog in FY2025 and found that the department does not consistently track work orders.

## Current Tech Landscape
Work orders and PM schedules run in IBM Maximo, Corrigo (owned by JLL), ServiceChannel, Eptura's Archibus, Planon, FM:Systems, MRI and Facilio, and the large providers run national service desks on top of them. Building automation systems such as Johnson Controls Metasys, Siemens Desigo, Honeywell and Tridium Niagara hold the plant's trend data, and fault-detection analytics (SkySpark, Clockworks, CopperTree) read it for the minority of buildings that pay for it. Capital planning runs on condition assessments in Gordian VFA, Brightly or a consultant's spreadsheet. Statutory inspections are tracked in tools like Inspect Point and BuildingReports or in a compliance calendar spreadsheet, energy reporting runs through ENERGY STAR Portfolio Manager, and subcontractor invoices pass through Coupa, SAP Ariba or the CMMS's own invoice module. Each tool is competent within its boundary. No tool joins the three things that decide an IFM account: the price the contract was sold at, the work actually done and who did it, and the condition of the asset afterwards.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is what a building actually costs to run, and how much of that work the provider should do with its own people. Every account answers it twice, once at the bid and again in every self-delivery or subcontract decision. Neither answer is recorded in a form that can be checked against what happened. Three things block the fix. The data is organisational: work orders sit in one CMMS per account, often the client's. It is contractual: clients own their asset data, and accounts change hands at rebid. It is behavioural: KPIs reward closing tickets, not recording causes, so the CMMS describes the contract rather than the building. The commercial opening for workflow software is real, in service-desk triage, invoice validation, inspection close-out and technician-facing building memory, and the large providers are capable buyers. For a research-insight engine the operators are poor buyers: their analysis prices their own bids and is never invoiced, and the Pass 2 sweep finds the operators failing Q1. No new pocket in this value chain clears the threshold. The nearest are condition-assessment consultancies, stopped because owners hold the data, and building performance standard compliance consultancies, whose audit and outcome data the city publishes for anyone to use. The indexed insight layer here remains the cost-data publishers already qualified under [[industries/cleaning-companies|Cleaning Companies]].
