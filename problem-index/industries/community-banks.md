# Community Banks

## Profile
**Category:** Financial Services
**NAICS 2022:** 522110, 522180 — Commercial Banking; Savings Institutions and Other Depository Credit Intermediation (the thrift half only: NAICS 2022 also files credit unions under this code, and they sit in [[industries/credit-unions|Credit Unions]])
**Market Size:** ~$2.8T in assets at ~3,950 community banks at Q3 2025 (FDIC data via a correspondent bank's summary), earning $29.9B in 2025 (FDIC); 3,818 community banks out of 4,238 FDIC-insured institutions at Q2 2026 (FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium-Low — deposits and the ledger run on a core rented from one of three processors (Fiserv, Jack Henry and FIS served more than 70% of banks in the Kansas City Fed's survey), while commercial credit judgement, exam preparation and conversion planning still run on spreadsheets, shared drives and the memory of a few senior officers.
**Workforce:** Commercial and agricultural lenders who own borrower relationships, credit analysts who spread financials and write memos, loan processors and closers, a chief credit officer, deposit operations staff, tellers and universal bankers, a BSA officer with ~one to five analysts, a compliance officer, a CFO who also runs asset-liability management and the Call Report, loan review (often outsourced), an IT and vendor manager, and a board of local directors

## Key Pain Themes
A community bank exists because its lenders know things about local borrowers that a scorecard does not. Community banks held about 15% of bank loans but roughly 30% of commercial real estate loans, 36% of small business loans and 70% of agricultural loans in the FDIC's last community banking study. Each of those loans carries a risk grade, set by the lender at origination and revised at annual review, and the grade drives everything downstream: the CECL allowance, the watch list, board reporting, and the examiner's asset-quality rating. Grades move late. The lender who made the loan is the person least inclined to downgrade it, loan review samples a fraction of the book once a year, and the examiner who finds the problem first writes it up. The senior credit officer who "can tell from the tax return" which borrower is drifting carries that read in their head, and it leaves when they retire or when the bank is sold. Thrifts, the savings-institution half of NAICS 522180, do a different version of the work: their books lean on one-to-four family mortgages held in portfolio, so their central judgements are interest rate risk on long fixed-rate assets and deposit runoff rather than commercial grades. This hub's analysis centres on commercial-bank charters; the thrift work is carried by the deposit-pricing niche and, for origination, by [[industries/mortgage-lenders|Mortgage Lenders]].

Around the lending sit external clocks the bank cannot move. The Call Report is due 30 days after quarter-end. A suspicious activity report is due 30 days after the bank decides activity is suspicious, never more than 60. Examiners arrive every 12 months, or every 18 for well-rated banks under $3B, preceded by a request letter listing ~a hundred or more items (estimate) to be produced within weeks. A bank whose non-owner-occupied CRE reaches 300% of capital after 50% growth over 36 months draws supervisory scrutiny of its concentration risk management. The revised Section 1071 small business data rule now applies from January 1, 2028, but only to banks originating at least 1,000 covered loans a year, which leaves most community banks out.

The operating base is rented and churns through mergers. Bank M&A in 2025 was the busiest since 2021, with 181 announced deals in S&P's half-year counts, and almost every deal ends in a core conversion: products, fee schedules, general ledger codes and customer notices mapped from one processor's data model to another's over a single weekend. Jack Henry alone booked $33.9M of deconversion revenue in fiscal 2025, mostly from clients being acquired. Compliance work falls hardest on the smallest banks; CSBS surveys find small banks spending about 11–15.5% of payroll on compliance tasks against 6–10% at the largest.

## Current Tech Landscape
Cores come from Fiserv (Premier, DNA, Signature), Jack Henry (SilverLake, CIF 20/20, Core Director) and FIS (Horizon, IBS), with digital banking from Jack Henry Banno, Q2 and Alkami layered on top. Commercial lending runs through nCino, Abrigo (the former Sageworks), Baker Hill or the core vendor's loan module, with spreading in Abrigo or Moody's tools and much of the global cash flow still in Excel. Abrigo, Moody's and several smaller vendors sell CECL allowance software; ZM Financial, Darling Consulting Group, Plansmith and the correspondent banks sell asset-liability models; Verafin (Nasdaq), Abrigo BAM+, Jack Henry and Fiserv sell BSA/AML monitoring. Deposit and loan pricing benchmarks come from Curinos and S&P's RateWatch. Each tool is competent within its boundary. The record that no tool owns is the credit judgement itself: why each loan got the grade it got, what the lender knew that the spread did not show, and what happened to the borrower afterwards.

## Analysis
The community bank's central unmeasured judgement is the risk grade on a commercial loan: an expert's read of a local borrower, recorded as a single digit with the reasoning stripped out, revised by the same person who made the loan, and graded only when a loan reviewer or examiner happens to sample it. The bank holds both halves of the label, every grade change and every later charge-off, past-due and payoff, in its core and its loan system, and has never joined them. What blocks a fix is organisational (lenders resist being scored on their own grades), structural (most banks have a few hundred commercial relationships and too few defaults to learn from alone) and legal (borrower and guarantor files are customer data under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, so a vendor cannot pool them freely). The operator is a real but modest buyer: a $500M bank signs software contracts in the tens of thousands of dollars, buys through its core vendor where it can, and asks first whether the examiner will accept the output. The strongest commercial opening sits with whoever already pools data across banks under contracts that permit it. The services firms that review loans and test BSA programmes for hundreds of banks see the cross-bank record but are production shops working on client-owned files, and the software vendors that hold it carry a customer-data kill switch; that is the central finding of the Pass 2 sweep.
