# Aerospace Parts Suppliers

## Profile
**Category:** Manufacturing & Industrial
**NAICS 2022:** 336412, 336413 — Aircraft Engine and Engine Parts Manufacturing; Other Aircraft Parts and Auxiliary Equipment Manufacturing
**Market Size:** ~$77.0B in 2022 receipts across 1,022 firm counts and 1,346 establishments (Census SUSB 2022: 336412 had 296 firms and $43.6B; 336413 had 726 firms and $33.5B); 109 enterprise counts with 500+ employees took ~$68.2B (~89%), and 913 firm counts under 500 employees shared ~$8.8B. The codes undercount the supply base: much of the detail-part machining, heat treating, plating, casting and forging behind an aircraft is done by shops coded as machine shops, foundries and forges, which the vault covers under other industries.
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the large engine and structures houses run PLM, MES and digital radiography, while the median tier-2 or tier-3 supplier quotes from PDFs, balloons drawings by hand, chases outside processors by phone and keeps its quality system in spreadsheets and binders that an AS9100 auditor reads once a year.
**Workforce:** CNC machinists and programmers, manufacturing and quality engineers, NAS 410-certified NDT inspectors (penetrant, radiography, ultrasonic), CMM operators and final inspectors, estimators, production control and expediters, buyers, and the quality manager who signs first article reports and answers customer corrective-action requests. BLS QCEW counted ~194,800 private employees in the two codes in 2024 (91,324 in engines at ~$121,600 average pay, concentrated in Connecticut and Ohio; 103,494 in other parts at ~$93,100, led by California, Washington, Texas and Kansas).

## Key Pain Themes
An aerospace parts supplier sells a part plus the proof that the part is what the drawing says. Every new part number, design change, change of sub-tier source or two-year production lapse (recalled from AS9102) triggers a first article inspection: every dimension, note and requirement on the drawing ballooned, measured and recorded on AS9102 Forms 1–3, with the material and special-process certifications behind it. Revision C of AS9102 (released 23 June 2023, per vendor summaries of the standard) made the reason for a full or partial FAI mandatory on every report. A mid-size supplier may produce dozens a month, and a single typo can get the report rejected and the invoice held. GroundControl, a YC-backed FAI start-up, says 70+ US facilities generate almost 1,000 reports a month on its platform, which shows both the volume and how recently software reached it.

The second theme is delivery. IATA put the commercial aircraft backlog above 17,000 in December 2025, almost 60% of the active fleet against a historical 30–40%, and does not expect the gap to close before 2031–2034; at its June 2026 AGM the backlog was reported above 18,000, with supply-chain failures costing airlines $11B+ in 2025 (search summary). Boeing's 737 programme began moving from 42 to 47 a month in the first half of 2026 with the FAA's concurrence, started low-rate production on a new line in July and is planning further increases (Boeing's Q2 2026 10-Q). Airbus delivered 114 aircraft in the first quarter of 2026 against 136 a year earlier, and its CEO named the shortage of Pratt & Whitney engines. The constraint runs downhill: engines wait on castings and forgings, structures wait on detail parts, and detail parts wait on outside heat treaters, platers and NDT houses. Nadcap lists 4,500+ accredited suppliers across 24 critical processes for 60+ subscribing OEMs (PRI's own page). Howmet's Engine Products segment earned a 33.3% EBITDA margin in 2025 on castings and rings, and its 10-K says price increases exceeded cost pass-through. That is what a bottleneck looks like.

The third theme is evidence of authenticity. In 2024 Spirit AeroSystems found titanium in Boeing and Airbus structures that had arrived with falsified certificates through a distributor (search summary of AP and FAA statements). Defence work adds DFARS flowdowns, ITAR-controlled drawings and CMMC, whose Phase 2 certification rollout the Department of War suspended on 13 July 2026, leaving Phase 1 self-assessments and DFARS 252.204-7012 in force while some primes kept flowing certification down. Smaller suppliers also bid government spare parts on DLA's DIBBS against thin technical data packages, approved-source lists and first article test rules. DLA Aviation manages 1.8M+ national stock numbers for 2,341 weapon systems (search summary of a DLA flyer).

## Current Tech Landscape
ERPs and shop systems (Epicor Kinetic, Plex, ProShop, JobBOSS², SAP at the large houses) run jobs and travellers. FAI software from Net-Inspect, DISCUS, High QA, InspectionXpert and newer GroundControl balloons drawings and builds AS9102 forms, while CMM software from Hexagon and Zeiss produces the measurements. Paperless Parts and CADDi handle quoting and drawing search, and First Resonance's ION runs quality and traceability for newer hardware makers. Exiger, Interos and others map sub-tier supply risk, PRI's eAuditNet holds Nadcap accreditation, and SalesPatriot and a cluster of bid tools work government solicitations. Each tool does its own job. The record nobody owns is the call made on the part itself: what the inspector saw in the penetrant booth or on the radiograph, why it was accepted or scrapped, and whether that call held up later.

## Analysis
The central unmeasured judgement in aerospace parts supply is disposition: the inspector's accept, reject or re-process call on an indication in a casting, forging or rotating part, and the engineer's call on what a nonconformance means. It decides both safety and capacity in the most constrained part of the chain, and it is recorded as a checkbox on a traveller, never as the image and the reasoning. Three things block fixing it. Any change to an inspection method needs customer and Nadcap acceptance, so automation can only assist the certified inspector, not replace them. Defence part images carry ITAR. And the outcome label (the crack found later, the field return) is rare and slow. As a buyer of insight the operator layer fails the Pass 2 gate: suppliers run thin indirect staffs and their customers own most of the requirements. The near-term commercial opening sits in the paperwork that gates every shipment (first articles, certificates, quotes and bids), where vendors such as SalesPatriot, First Resonance and Exiger are hiring forward deployed engineers now. Whoever captures disposition decisions as a by-product of that paperwork will hold the dataset the industry lacks.
