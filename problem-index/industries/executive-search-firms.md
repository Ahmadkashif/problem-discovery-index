# Executive Search Firms

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561312 — Executive Search Services
**Market Size:** ~$12.6B in US receipts in 2022 (Economic Census, as reported by a third-party summary) across 5,897 establishments employing 38,564 people (County Business Patterns 2023), 93% of them with fewer than 20 employees; AESC describes a ~$20B global industry. The top tier is concentrated: Korn Ferry's executive search fee revenue was $846.2M in fiscal 2025 on 6,300+ new engagements, and Heidrick & Struggles (taken private by Advent and Corvex in December 2025 at ~$1.3B) reported $923.7M of total net revenue in the first nine months of 2025
**Tech Maturity:** Medium-Low. Every mandate sits in a search CRM (Thrive TRM, Clockwork, Invenias, Ezekia, Cluen Encore, FileFinder), and generative AI now drafts candidate write-ups and role descriptions, but the assessment of a candidate and the record of why they were chosen live in a partner's notes and memory, and no system joins either to what happened after the hire.
**Workforce:** Partners and consultants who win and lead mandates; principals and engagement managers who run them; associates and researchers who map markets, build longlists, make first approaches and write candidate reports; knowledge and research-centre staff at the large firms; assessment specialists and I-O psychologists in leadership advisory arms; and coordinators who schedule interviews across time zones

## Key Pain Themes
A retained search is a bet that a firm can find, assess and land the right person for a senior role, and the client pays for the process whether or not the bet comes good: Heidrick's filings describe a retainer of about one-third of the estimated first-year compensation and an average fee of $146,000 per search in 2024, and practitioner guides describe the fee as usually billed in thirds — at signing, at a milestone and at placement. The product is a judgement — this candidate will succeed here, and will actually move — formed through interviews, referencing and, increasingly, psychometric assessment. That judgement is never graded. Firms track whether a placement survives the guarantee period, usually six to twelve months, and then stop looking, though the Center for Creative Leadership puts outright failure in the first 18 months at between 38% and more than half of new executives, and the departure of a listed company's principal and named executive officers is filed on a Form 8-K within four business days.

Under the judgement sits a research workflow repeated on every mandate. A researcher builds a target company list, reconstructs who holds the role at each target, levels titles that mean different things in different companies, and produces a longlist of ~100 or more names that is narrowed to a shortlist of ~5–8 (practitioner norms, estimate). Each name has to be cleared against off-limits agreements (Heidrick's 10-K describes refraining from recruiting a client's employees for up to a year after a search starts), against the firm's other clients' corporate families, and, for European candidates, against GDPR Article 14, which requires a privacy notice within one month of compiling data the candidate did not provide. Much of the map is rebuilt from scratch for each search, and is stale within weeks.

The clocks are set by the client and the candidate, not a regulator: a CEO's announced departure, a board's next proxy, a private-equity sponsor's hundred-day plan, and the counteroffer that arrives the week a candidate resigns. Board work adds a disclosure trail — proxies must describe any third party paid to identify or evaluate director nominees — and Spencer Stuart counted only 364 new independent S&P 500 directors in its 2026 Board Index, the fewest since 2016, which raises the stakes on every board slate.

## Current Tech Landscape
Search CRMs hold the mandate, candidate records and client reporting and do it competently; Invenias adds business-development pipeline reporting and Cluen Encore adds GDPR triggers. Sourcing runs on LinkedIn Recruiter and newer AI sourcing tools such as Pin, SeekOut and hireEZ; board and executive data come from BoardEx (Altrata), Equilar's BoardEdge and ExecAtlas, and proxy pay data; psychometric assessment comes from SHL, Hogan and Korn Ferry's own instruments. Generative AI use moved from 37.1% of firms at the end of 2023 (Clockwork survey) to most firms using ChatGPT by early 2026, mainly for candidate write-ups. No tool owns the record that matters: the assessment made at the time of hire, the evidence behind it, and the executive's tenure and performance afterwards.

## Analysis
Executive search sells a prediction and never checks it. The central unmeasured judgement is the consultant's read of a candidate — leadership, fit with this CEO or sponsor, motivation to move, the derailer behind a polished career story — and the outcome that would grade it is public, dated and free for most senior placements. What blocks the loop is structural rather than technical: the fee is earned at acceptance, the guarantee ends inside a year, partners are rewarded for origination rather than placement durability, and a graded record would expose the partners whose instincts are weakest. Clients are also wary of anything that looks like data about their executives leaving the engagement. The strongest commercial opening is inside the large firms, whose research and knowledge centres hold decades of interviews and placements and rebuild the same market maps every quarter; the 5,000-plus boutiques are poor buyers of anything but cheap research capacity. Around the industry, the insight businesses that look closest — leadership assessment, board reviews, fit-and-proper supervision — work on client-owned or confidential data, and the clean data businesses (executive profiles, proxy pay data) sell to a handful of buyers from public sources.
