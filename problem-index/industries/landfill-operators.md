# Landfill Operators

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 562212, 562219 — Solid Waste Landfill; Other Nonhazardous Waste Treatment and Disposal (compost dumps, lawn waste and non-landfill nonhazardous treatment)
**Market Size:** ~$14.5B in 2022 receipts across ~1,240 employer firms (Census SUSB 2022, a sum of code counts). Solid waste landfills took $12.76B across 707 firms, 1,592 establishments and 25,635 employees, and other nonhazardous treatment and disposal $1.70B across 535 firms. That second code includes compost dumps and lawn-waste facilities; their receipts are counted here, but composting work is left to the queued compost-producers industry, and this build covers landfills and non-landfill liquid and solid waste treatment. The 8 enterprises with 5,000+ employees took ~$8.73B (~68%) of landfill receipts, while 570 landfill firms with fewer than 20 employees shared ~$839M. SUSB counts employer firms only: about 65% of the 1,412 active landfills EREF counted in 2025 are publicly owned and sit outside it, so total US disposal revenue is nearer ~$18B (estimate). The same companies' collection fleets are counted in [[industries/waste-haulers|Waste Haulers]], hazardous waste landfills in [[industries/hazardous-waste-firms|Hazardous Waste Firms]], and the wider work of city and county governments in [[industries/municipal-services|Municipal Services]].
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the consolidators fly drone surveys, run central airspace and closure accounting, and pipe landfill gas to RNG plants under RIN and LCFS programmes, but wells are still tuned by a technician with a handheld analyser about once a month, leachate is logged on haul tickets, and most county landfills run on a scale-house PC and a consultant's annual report.
**Workforce:** Landfill managers, compactor, dozer and scraper operators, spotters at the working face, scale-house attendants, landfill gas wellfield technicians, leachate and wastewater operators, environmental compliance managers, site engineers, mechanics, and the outside engineers who design cells, estimate closure costs and file permits.

## Key Pain Themes
A landfill sells airspace. Every ton that crosses the scale pays a tipping fee and consumes cubic yards of permitted volume that took years of engineering and politics to win, and the business is the gap between the price of a ton and the volume it occupies. The 2024 EREF survey of 351 landfills put the national average tipping fee at $62.28 a ton, up 10%, with private landfills charging ~34% more than public ones. WM, which owns or operates 253 solid waste landfills, took 129M depletable tons in 2025 against 5.2B tons of remaining permitted and expansion capacity, and won expansion permits at 13 landfills in a year. Republic runs 207 active landfills, carries 124 closed ones, and says third-party tipping fees are ~12% of its revenue. Price and density are both managed at the margin: rate tables at the scale house, and how hard the compactor operator works each lift on the working face.

The waste keeps working after it is buried. Decomposing waste makes landfill gas, ~50% methane (estimate), and landfills account for over 14% of US methane emissions. Under the federal NSPS and emission guidelines, a technician must check every well monthly for pressure and temperature, walk the surface every quarter on a 30-metre grid, re-monitor any reading 500 ppm above background within 10 days and drill a new well within 120 days if it keeps failing. Aerial surveys found point-source plumes at 52% of 200+ landfills and emissions averaging 1.4 times what operators reported. The same gas is revenue: 542 energy projects ran at 488 landfills in September 2024, and RNG plants sell the methane with D3 RINs, which fell to ~$2.45 in 2025 from ~$3.13 (search summary). Rainwater through the waste becomes leachate that must be pumped off the liner and trucked or piped to a treatment plant, and plants are now refusing it or demanding PFAS pretreatment while EPA works on leachate limits.

The liabilities outlive the revenue. Federal rules require 30 years of post-closure care, with cost estimates re-priced for inflation every year and backed by bonds, trusts, letters of credit or a financial test. WM's closure and post-closure obligations alone are $3.3B. When the waste misbehaves the bill grows: Waste Connections' Chiquita Canyon Landfill in California has received ~410 odour notices of violation since a subsurface heating event began in 2023, faces an abatement order with 107 conditions, and stopped taking waste at the end of 2024. Permit renewals, expansion hearings and host-community agreements set the clocks for growth, and annual regulatory reports set the clocks for compliance.

## Current Tech Landscape
Scale houses run Paradigm's Compass, Carolina Software's WasteWORKS, AMCS or a hauler billing package, which ticket loads and apply rate codes but leave special-waste approvals and host-fee reports to spreadsheets. Gas teams read wells with QED's Landtec GEM analysers and keep the readings in vendor databases; Loci Controls automates wellhead valves and added AI summaries to its WellWatcher platform in August 2026. Drone surveys from Propeller, Firmatek and DroneDeploy measure airspace and density, and Sniffer Robotics' drone has been accepted by EPA since December 2022 as an alternative to walking surface surveys. Groundwater and leachate results flow through EarthSoft EQuIS, Locus and Sanitas statistics. GHGSat, Carbon Mapper and aerial survey firms measure plumes from above. Each tool sees one slice of the site. None holds the record that matters most: how the waste mass at each cell is behaving — gas, heat, settlement, liquids and density together — and what the people who run the site did about it.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the experienced operator's read of the waste mass: which well is pulling air, which cell is heating, where the working face is losing density, and how much leachate a storm will put on the liner. That read decides how much methane reaches the RNG plant or escapes, how many cubic yards each ton consumes, and whether a site ends up like Chiquita Canyon. It lives in the heads of senior wellfield technicians, landfill managers and compactor operators, and the data that would capture it is split across handheld analysers, drone surveys, scale tickets and consultants' reports. Three things block a fix. Readings that document an exceedance are compliance records, so operators are wary of systems that create more of them. The eight largest enterprises, holding ~68% of private landfill receipts, build their own engineering and accounting functions. And the ~920 public landfills buy through procurement on thin budgets. The commercial opening is operator-side: the RNG developers and consolidators whose gas revenue rises with every point of collection efficiency, and the mid-sized private and public landfills that need airspace, leachate and compliance tools. Under the Pass 2 rubric nothing around the industry qualifies. The landfills hold the richest data themselves, much of the outside data is public regulatory data, and the firms that sell analysis about landfills are engineering consultancies working site by site and a few small vendors and price agencies.
