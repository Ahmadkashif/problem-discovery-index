# Postproduction Studios

## Profile
**Category:** Media, Telecom & Information
**NAICS 2022:** 512191 — Teleproduction and Other Postproduction Services
**Market Size:** ~$4.88B in US employer-firm revenue in 2022 (Census Service Annual Survey, taxable employer firms; down from ~$5.16B in 2016 after a ~$4.15B low in 2020), with the 2022 Economic Census putting the industry at ~$6.4B, across ~3,144 establishments and ~22,670 employees (County Business Patterns 2023). Freelance editors, colorists and sound editors who file as sole proprietors add an estimated ~$1–2B more (estimate)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the craft runs on mature, demanding software (Avid Media Composer, DaVinci Resolve, Baselight, Pro Tools, Nuke) and automated QC, but the business around the shot runs on Excel bid templates, emailed notes, PDF delivery schedules and the supervisor's memory of what a shot really cost last time.
**Workforce:** Editors and assistant editors (BLS counts 39,400 film and video editors in 2025, 31% of them in the motion picture and video industries and 35% self-employed), colorists and online/conform editors, VFX supervisors, producers and coordinators, compositors, roto and paint artists, sound editors, re-recording mixers, QC operators, captioners and subtitlers, film scanning and restoration technicians, and post producers who move a show through all of them. Film laboratories and restoration houses are filed under NAICS 512199 (Other Motion Picture and Video Industries); that film-lab slice of 512199 is analysed here as [[niches/postproduction-studios/film-scanning-and-restoration/profile|Film Scanning, Restoration & Library Remastering]], while the rest of the code sits with [[industries/film-distributors|Film Distributors]]

## Key Pain Themes
A postproduction studio is hired after the shoot to turn footage into a finished, deliverable programme: cut it, colour it, add the visual effects, edit and mix the sound, and package it to a distributor's specification. It is paid per project, and on most visual effects work it is paid a fixed price per shot set at bid time. The bid is the decision that decides whether the job makes money, and it rests on judgement nobody records: a VFX supervisor looks at a plate with motion blur through hair, a handheld camera and smoke, and knows the roto alone will take days. The actual hours land in Autodesk Flow Production Tracking (formerly ShotGrid) or ftrack, shot by shot, and are never joined back to the estimate. The economics are unforgiving. Fixed bids leave no allowance for revisions, competitive bidding pushes prices to break-even, and the industry has watched large vendors fail: Technicolor's creative studios (MPC, The Mill, Mikros) collapsed in February 2025, with most of 440+ UK staff made redundant and US offices closed.

The second theme is the delivery spec. Every platform publishes its own: Netflix requires Dolby Vision HDR with L1 metadata on every frame, an IMF package that passes its Photon checks, and a mix at -27 LKFS ±2 LU dialog-gated. Theatrical needs a DCP to DCI and SMPTE specifications, and broadcast needs its own masters and FCC-compliant captions judged on accuracy, synchronicity, completeness and placement. Netflix rejects the whole IMF delivery if any track fails, and a rejection lands days before a premiere date the studio did not set. Around the masters sits a paper schedule of 80–200 items (estimate): music cue sheets, credits, chain-of-title documents and E&O certificates. The distributor's final payment waits until every one is closed.

The third theme is demand and labour. Postproduction follows commissioning, and commissioning fell: FX Research counted ~600 adult scripted series in 2022 and 516 in 2023, before the strikes' full effect. Work moved to wherever incentives are richest. The UK now gives VFX a 39% credit with no cap on UK VFX costs, and Australia offers a 30% PDV Offset. Artists followed the work or lost it. Roto and paint, the most repetitive work, went offshore and is now going to segmentation models. Marvel and Disney VFX crews ratified their first IATSE contracts in 2025, but the thousands employed by independent vendors are not covered.

## Current Tech Landscape
Editorial runs on Avid Media Composer (still the standard for long-form, with shared storage) and Adobe Premiere Pro; colour and finishing on DaVinci Resolve and Baselight; VFX on Nuke, Houdini, Maya and Mocha or Silhouette for roto; sound on Pro Tools with iZotope for repair and loudness. Production tracking runs on Autodesk Flow Production Tracking and ftrack; review on Frame.io and MediaSilo; transfer on Aspera and Signiant; automated QC on Interra BATON, Venera Pulsar and Telestream; content supply-chain orchestration on SDVI Rally at the larger facilities. Bids live in Excel templates and Movie Magic or Hot Budget. Content security is assessed against the MPA's Trusted Partner Network (TPN) best practices. Each tool is competent inside its boundary. The record that decides the studio's margin belongs to none of them: what each shot was bid at, what it took, which notes changed its scope, and which deliverables failed QC and why.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the bid: how much work a shot, a reel, a conform or a mix will take before anyone has done it. Every outcome is recorded somewhere. Artist hours sit in the production tracker, notes in Frame.io, revisions in version numbers and QC failures in the platform's rejection reports. None of it is joined back to the estimate, so a vendor cannot tell which shot types it systematically underbids. Three things block fixing it. Firms are small (about 7 employees per establishment) and run on thin, volatile margins. The footage and every derived file belong to the client under NDAs and TPN security rules, so pooling data across studios is close to impossible. And the clock belongs to someone else: a premiere date, a streamer's delivery window, a theatrical release. Operators below the top tier are therefore poor buyers of enterprise analytics. The stronger openings are inside the largest vendors and rollups (bid memory, QC failure prediction, change-order capture) and in automating the mechanical layers (roto, conform, captions, cue sheets) that clients now expect at falling prices. Upstream, the Pass 2 sweep finds no qualifier. The research layer around postproduction belongs to the studios and streamers that commission it, to software vendors, and to advisers already mapped under [[industries/video-production-smb|SMB Video Production]].
