# Logistics Consultants

## Profile
**Category:** Professional Services
**NAICS 2022:** 541614 — Process, Physical Distribution, and Logistics Consulting Services
**Market Size:** ~$25.0B in receipts across 7,630 firms and 8,676 establishments in 2022 (Census Statistics of US Businesses); the code also covers process and operations-improvement consulting, and logistics and distribution work is an estimated ~40–50% of it (estimate). The bill these firms advise on is far larger: US business logistics costs were ~$2.58T, ~8.8% of GDP, in 2024 (CSCMP State of Logistics)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the solvers and simulators are sophisticated (Coupa Supply Chain Design & Planning, Optilogic Cosmic Frog, AIMMS, AnyLogic, FlexSim), but every engagement starts with weeks of hand-cleaning a client's shipment, order and freight invoice data in Excel and SQL, and the firm's memory of past engagements lives in slide decks and in its senior people.
**Workforce:** Principals and partners who sell the work and own the recommendation; network modellers and operations research analysts; industrial engineers and material-handling designers who lay out distribution centres; transportation procurement analysts who run freight bids; inventory and planning specialists; data analysts who do the cleansing. The 2023 County Business Patterns count 97,017 employees at 8,360 establishments, and ~73% of establishments have fewer than five employees — most of the industry is ex-practitioners working alone or in small teams.

## Key Pain Themes
A logistics consultant sells a recommendation: close this distribution centre and open two smaller ones, award these 400 lanes to these carriers, re-slot this building, cut safety stock on these SKUs. The model behind it projects a saving, and the saving is realised — or not — 12 to 36 months later, inside the client's P&L, after the consultant has rolled off. Nobody reconciles projected to realised savings, because volumes shift, freight markets move, and the client implements part of the plan. So the firm cannot say which of its modelling assumptions were right: the cost-to-serve rates, the productivity it assumed a new building would reach, the rates it estimated for lanes no carrier bid on, the haircut it applied for implementation friction. Those judgement calls are made by senior modellers, by feel, and are recorded nowhere.

The work itself is dominated by data. A traditional network design project takes three to four months, and one to two of them go on the data (Miebach Consulting, quoted by Optilogic): matching ship-to addresses, imputing missing weights, joining freight invoices to shipments, allocating accessorials, reconciling the baseline model to the general ledger until the client's CFO accepts it as the real cost of the current network. The senior modeller's value is knowing which anomalies are real and which are data errors — a skill the analysts doing the matching do not yet have and the firm has never written down.

The clocks are set by others. Freight bids are timed to contract expirations; parcel carriers raise list rates every January (5.9% on average at both UPS and FedEx for 2026); the NMFC moved much of LTL freight to density-based classes on 19 July 2025, invalidating historical class assumptions in every LTL study; distribution centre leases expire on fixed dates; peak season does not move; and 2025's tariff changes sent shippers back to re-model sourcing and flows mid-cycle.

## Current Tech Landscape
Network design runs on Coupa Supply Chain Design & Planning (the former LLamasoft Supply Chain Guru, bought by Coupa for ~$1.5B in 2020), Optilogic Cosmic Frog (which raised $40M in 2025), AIMMS SC Navigator and the planning suites from Blue Yonder, Kinaxis and o9; simulation on AnyLogic and anyLogistix, FlexSim and Simio; warehouse layout in AutoCAD with slotting and labour tools alongside. Freight procurement uses Keelvar, Coupa and Transporeon sourcing modules, priced against benchmark data from DAT, Freightos and Xeneta (see [[freight-brokerage|Freight Brokerage]] and [[cold-chain-logistics|Cold Chain Logistics]]). Warehouse benchmarks come from WERC's DC Measures survey (36 metrics in 2026). Each tool is competent within its boundary. The engagement record — the raw data, every cleansing decision, every assumption, the projected saving, what the client actually implemented and what it actually saved — spans all of them and is owned by none.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the senior consultant's: how fast a new building will ramp, what unbid lanes will cost, how much of a projected saving the client will really capture. Every engagement produces a dated, quantified prediction of savings, and the outcome eventually shows up in the client's freight invoices, labour reports and inventory balances — so unlike most advisory work, the recommendations are gradeable in principle. Three things block grading. Organisationally, the consultant rolls off at the readout and implementation belongs to the client. Legally, the client owns the data, and engagement letters rarely grant the follow-up access a reconciliation needs. Structurally, 90% of firms have fewer than twenty people, so the judgement sits in a handful of senior heads and leaves with them. The strongest commercial opening is therefore not another solver — those are mature — but an engagement memory that records each scenario assumption as it is made and reconciles it against realised outcomes where access allows, plus agents that compress the data month. Note that the operators are weak buyers for a research-insight engine under the Pass 2 rubric: most are too small to have an insight function, and the large ones are walled by client confidentiality. The qualifying pockets in this value chain — freight rate benchmarks, parcel contract benchmarking, freight procurement consultancies and supply chain research advisory — are already indexed under the operating industries; this industry's niche overview names them rather than profiling them a second time.
