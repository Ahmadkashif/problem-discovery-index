# Recording Studios

## Profile
**Category:** Media, Telecom & Information
**NAICS 2022:** 512240, 512290 — Sound Recording Studios; Other Sound Recording Industries. The first code covers studios that record, mix, master and restore audio for clients in a room. The second covers audio production that needs no tracking room: voice-over, sound design, audiobook, radio and jingle production, plus stock music and sound libraries. Both are done by the same engineers on the same per-hour or per-deliverable billing. Picture sound mixing, which the studio code's definition also names, is left to [[industries/postproduction-studios|Postproduction Studios]], and podcast production to [[industries/podcasting-networks|Podcasting Networks]].
**Market Size:** ~$2.47B in employer receipts across ~2,525 employer firms (2022 Economic Census: 512240 at ~$1.58B across 2,057 firms, where the top four take 25.7%; 512290 at ~$889M across 468 firms, where the top four take 57.6%). The Service Annual Survey puts "studio recording" at $662M of 512240's $1.42B employer revenue in 2022. A solo layer of home and project studios, which IBISWorld counts at ~22,000 studios including nonemployers, adds an estimated ~$0.5–1B more (estimate)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — capture, editing, mixing and delivery are fully digital and run on mature software, but bookings, holds, recall sheets, credits and revision notes live in texts, email, paper and memory, and the judgement that decides whether a client comes back, what "make it warmer" means and when a mix is done, is recorded nowhere.
**Workforce:** Owner-engineers who run a room; staff and freelance recording, mixing and mastering engineers; assistant engineers and runners; producers who book rooms for their artists; studio managers. On the 512290 side: audiobook narrators, directors, editors and proofers; voice actors recording from home booths; sound designers and jingle producers. BLS counts 15,000 sound engineering technicians with a $73,130 median wage (May 2025), projected to fall 3% by 2035, while County Business Patterns finds only 6,596 paid employees across 2,131 studio establishments (2023), so most studio work is done by owners and freelancers (estimate).

## Key Pain Themes
A studio sells one of two things: time in a room with an engineer, or a finished deliverable. Hourly rates run from $25–75 at project studios to $100–200 at most independent rooms and $500 or more at the famous ones; a freelance mix sells for ~$75–400 a song on SoundBetter, a master for ~$70–100, and an AI master from LANDR for about $10. The money is made or lost on revisions. A flat-fee mix includes a few rounds of changes, and the notes that arrive ("warmer", "punchier", "the chorus feels small") describe how the artist feels, not what to change. The engineer has to turn each note into concrete moves, and every wrong guess costs another round. A senior mixer's ear, which knows what a given artist means by "warmer" and when to stop, is the industry's core skill and its least documented one. Around it, assistants still reset analog consoles and outboard gear by hand from photos and recall sheets, and credits for who played and engineered are left in a text thread.

On the 512290 side, audiobooks are the largest line of work. US sales reached $2.43B in 2025, up 9%, across more than 750,000 active titles (Audio Publishers Association). Narrators are paid per finished hour, with a SAG-AFTRA minimum of $250 on ACX, and newer narrators spend about ten hours of recording, editing and proofing to produce one finished hour. Files must pass ACX's spec: -23 to -18 dB RMS, peaks below -3 dB, a noise floor below -60 dB. Audible opened AI narration to publishers in May 2025 with more than 100 voices. AI-narrated titles earned 0.03% of 2025 revenue, though the APA reports that more were published that year. Voice actors face the same pressure from synthetic voices, now limited by Tennessee's ELVIS Act (July 2024) and California's AB 2602, which voids vague digital-replica clauses in contracts.

The clocks are set elsewhere. Labels set release dates and streaming services set delivery specs; Spotify normalises to -14 LUFS. Apple Music has paid up to 10% more on recorded royalties since 2024 for tracks available in Spatial Audio, which pulled catalogue into Dolby Atmos remixes at roughly $1,000 a track. Vinyl lacquers have been scarce since the February 2020 fire at Apollo Masters left one lacquer maker in the world. Magnetic tape in archives is degrading on a window IASA put at 10–15 years. And SoundExchange pays producers, mixers and engineers only when the featured artist files a Letter of Direction, so a credit that was never written down at the session can cost the engineer royalties for years.

## Current Tech Landscape
Sessions run in Avid Pro Tools, the studio standard, or Logic Pro, Nuendo, Cubase and Ableton Live, with plug-ins from Universal Audio, Waves and iZotope; Ozone 12's Master Assistant and RoEx Automix bring AI to mastering and mixing. Vocal editing uses Melodyne and VocAlign. Remote sessions run on Source-Connect and Audiomovers, client review on Pibox, Filepass and Samply, and freelancers are found on SoundBetter and AirGigs. Bookings run on Calendly, Acuity, Square Appointments or a text thread. Audiobook producers proof with Pozotron and deliver through ACX or Findaway Voices; voice actors audition through Voices.com, Voice123 and Backstage. Credits live in Muso.AI and Discogs, and Jaxsta closed in November 2025, saying its model did not scale. Each tool covers one step. None records the link from a client's note to the engineer's move to the approval, though the session file, the review comments and the sign-off date all exist.

## Analysis
The central unmeasured judgement is the engineer's ear: turning a client's words and reference tracks into mix moves and knowing when the record is finished. Every part of the evidence already exists. The session file saves every fader and plug-in setting at each save, review tools timestamp every note, and the approval email dates the outcome. Nobody joins them, so the skill is taught only by sitting behind someone for years, and it leaves with the freelancer. Three things block a fix. The session usually belongs to the artist's or label's project rather than the studio. Engineers are freelance and move between rooms. And taste is contested, so labels are noisy and experts disagree with themselves. The market is also a poor buyer. About 2,500 employer firms and tens of thousands of solo rooms (estimate) buy plug-ins and subscriptions, not platforms, and 512290's top four firms hold most of its receipts. The strongest commercial openings are the software layers that already sit on the session (DAW, plug-in and review vendors) and audiobook production, where proofing and spec compliance are mechanical, checkable and paid per finished hour. Pass 2 finds one qualifying pocket, and it sits downstream of the studio: [[niches/recording-studios/soundexchange-royalty-collective/profile|🔍 Sound-Recording & Performer Collecting Societies]] (51). SoundExchange, PPL, GVL and their peers must match plays to recordings, owners and performers inside the EU directive's distribution windows. The line-ups they most often lack, the session players, were known in the room on the day of recording and never sent on. The pocket is the recording-side twin of [[niches/music-publishers/collecting-societies-distribution/profile|🔍 Performing Rights Organisations & Collecting Societies]] under Music Publishers. The rest of the value chain either uses its data for its own releases, sells tools, or is already profiled under other industries.
