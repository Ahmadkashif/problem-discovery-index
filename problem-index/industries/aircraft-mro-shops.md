# Aircraft MRO & FBO Shops

## Profile
**Category:** Transportation & Logistics
**NAICS 2022:** 488190 — Other Support Activities for Air Transportation
**Market Size:** ~$23.3B in 2022 receipts across 3,358 firms and 4,096 establishments (Census SUSB 2022); 95 enterprises with 500+ employees took ~$13.4B (~58%), while 2,825 firms under 20 employees shared ~$3.1B. Oliver Wyman puts North American commercial MRO demand at ~$31.8B for 2026 (engines $14.8B, components $6.6B, airframe $6.1B, line $4.4B) inside a global market of $136B in 2025. That forecast counts airline in-house maintenance and leaves out business and general aviation. The census code undercounts the work too, because factory-level engine and airframe overhaul can be coded to 336411/336412 (recalled scope note).
**Tech Maturity:** Medium-low. Airlines and the large MROs run AMOS, TRAX or IFS Maintenix with electronic task cards. The median Part 145 shop, FBO maintenance department and general aviation hangar still signs paper work orders and logbook entries, and a records clerk keys them into CAMP or Traxxall afterwards.
**Workforce:** A&P mechanics certificated under Part 65, inspectors (including Inspection Authorization holders for general aviation annuals and required-inspection-item buyers for airlines), avionics technicians, NDT and borescope inspectors, planners and production controllers, technical records clerks, quality managers, stores and parts staff, and FBO line service technicians. BLS QCEW counted ~141,000 private employees in 488190 in 2024 at ~$79,700 average pay, led by Texas, Florida, California, Georgia and Arizona. The FAA database lists 337,855 certificated mechanics, average age 54, and repair stations employ about 71% of all aviation technical personnel (ATEC 2025).

## Key Pain Themes
Airworthiness is a paperwork product. An aircraft is legal to fly only if every task is signed against approved data and every applicable airworthiness directive is complied with and recorded by number, revision and method of compliance. 14 CFR 91.417 makes the owner carry that AD status and the current status of every life-limited part, and 145.109 makes a repair station hold current ADs, manuals and service bulletins at the point of work. The FAA published 317 final ADs in 2025, each written against make, model, serial range, part number or modification status, many with recurring intervals and alternative methods of compliance. Checking a work package against that body of rules is still done largely from memory and a printed due list. A repair station must also report serious defects to the FAA within 96 hours (145.221), and those reports go into a public Service Difficulty Reporting database that held 67,652 reports for 2025. Almost nobody reads it back against the job on the bench.

The second theme is people and judgement. ATEC and Oliver Wyman counted a North American shortfall of ~17,800 technical personnel in 2025, including 5,338 certificated mechanics. Their 2026 report projects ~9,100 certificated mechanics short this year and ~20,000 by 2030, even after a record 11,103 new certificates in 2025. The mechanics who are leaving carry the hardest calls in the hangar: whether blade damage seen through a borescope is inside limits or means pulling the engine, whether corrosion is cosmetic, which intermittent fault is really the connector. A new A&P can follow the manual, but the manual doesn't say when the reading is borderline.

The third theme is capacity. Bain put engine shop turnaround up 35% or more for legacy engines and over 150% for new-generation engines against pre-pandemic levels. IATA counted 648 GTF-powered aircraft grounded at the March 2025 peak, waiting for shop visits, spare engines or parts. Engines are the largest MRO segment, and every shop visit turns on records that prove each life-limited part's history back to birth. The AOG Technics case, sentenced in February 2026, showed what happens when that proof is faked: over 60,000 suspect CFM56-family parts entered the supply chain on forged paperwork. FBOs add a nightly version of the same pressure: a transient business jet with a fault has to be diagnosed, legally signed off and gone by morning.

## Current Tech Landscape
Airline and large-MRO maintenance systems (Swiss AviationSoftware's AMOS, TRAX, IFS Maintenix, Ramco, Rusada ENVISION) hold the maintenance programme, task cards and stores. Business aviation tracks due items in CAMP (Hearst), Traxxall (JSSI) and Veryon's Flightdocs, and repair-station ERPs such as Corridor, Avsight, EBIS and Quantum Control run work orders and quotes. Records are scanned and indexed by flydocs, Bluetail and in-house teams. Borescopes from Waygate Technologies and Evident now come with assisted defect recognition, and Aiir Innovations and GE Aerospace build AI blade inspection. A new layer of AI vendors targets the mechanic's paperwork and troubleshooting: Zymbly (YC W26; San Francisco and London), Fleetcraft (San Francisco and London offices), Soji AI (Hamburg and Singapore) and Veryon's AIRE. Each tool does its own job well. The record nobody owns is the inspector's judgement: what was seen through the borescope or on the skin, why it was signed off as within limits, and whether that call held up at the next inspection or the next shop visit.

## Analysis
The central unmeasured judgement in aircraft maintenance is the inspector's disposition of damage: the borescope call on a turbine blade, the corrosion call on a lap joint, the troubleshooting call on an intermittent fault. It decides whether an engine stays on wing or goes into a shop queue measured in months, and it is recorded as a sign-off, never as the image and the reasoning. Three things block fixing it. A certificated person must make and sign the call under Part 43, so automation can only assist. The labels are slow, because the truth arrives at the next inspection or the next teardown, often at a different shop. And the records that would join call to outcome are scattered across operators, lessors, tracking services and MROs, each holding a piece. As a buyer of insight the operator layer fails the Pass 2 gate: repair stations average a few dozen staff, and their engineering serves their own shop visits. The near-term commercial opening is the paperwork that every sign-off depends on (AD applicability on work cards, technician write-ups, records for shop visits and lease returns). Zymbly (a forward deployed engineer in San Francisco or London), Fleetcraft (a founding engineer, London only) and Soji AI (a senior forward deployed engineer, Hamburg only) are hiring for exactly that work, and CAMP (Merrimack, New Hampshire) and JSSI (Chicago) are hiring engineers and data leaders around their tracking records. Whoever captures the inspector's calls as a by-product of that paperwork will hold the dataset the industry lacks.
