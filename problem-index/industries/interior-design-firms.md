# Interior Design Firms

## Profile
**Category:** Professional Services
**NAICS 2022:** 541410 — Interior Design Services
**Market Size:** ~$19.7B US revenue in the 2022 Economic Census (the Census Service Annual Survey put taxable employer firms at ~$19.3B in 2022, up from ~$12.2B in 2017), across ~16,800 establishments with ~55,000 employees (County Business Patterns 2023), plus an estimated ~60,000–90,000 nonemployer sole proprietors (estimate)
**Tech Maturity:** Low to Medium — drawing and rendering tools (AutoCAD, SketchUp, Revit, Enscape) are mature and studio-management platforms exist, but the work that decides margin and reputation (reading a client's taste, chasing to-the-trade orders, checking vendor acknowledgements, specifying code-rated finishes) still runs on email, spreadsheets, texts and memory.
**Workforce:** Principal designers and firm owners, senior and junior designers, design assistants, procurement and purchasing coordinators, bookkeepers, and in commercial practices NCIDQ-certified interior designers, space planners and FF&E specifiers. BLS counts ~97,100 interior designer jobs in 2025, 26% of them self-employed, at a median wage of ~$67,190.

## Key Pain Themes
An interior design firm sells judgement and then delivers logistics. The judgement is taste: turning a client's half-formed preferences, a Pinterest board and a few reactions in a showroom into a palette, a furniture plan and a set of selections the client approves. The experienced designer does this in one or two presentation rounds; a junior takes four or five, and on a flat-fee or capped-fee project every extra round is unbilled. That reading of the client is the firm's real product, and it is recorded nowhere except in the selections that survived.

The logistics are where the money is made and lost. A residential firm typically earns as much from product as from fees, buying to-the-trade furniture, lighting, fabric and wallcovering at a trade discount and reselling with a markup commonly quoted at 10–35%. A single living room can mean thirty to eighty line items from twenty vendors, each with its own purchase order, acknowledgement, deposit, lead time (8–20 weeks for custom upholstery is ordinary, estimate), freight carrier, receiving warehouse and install date. The designer finds out a sofa shipped in the wrong fabric when the receiver opens the crate, or finds out a lead time slipped when the client asks why the room is empty. Sales tax on resold product, resale certificates, client deposits held against vendor deposits, and the client who finds the same lamp cheaper online all land on the same small team.

Commercial practices carry a different clock. Hospitality work is driven by brand property improvement plans (PIPs) that hotels must complete on a deadline set by the franchisor, typically on a 7–15 year cycle, with FF&E specified to brand standards and to fire and performance codes. Workplace work is driven by lease events: a broker needs a test fit and a budget for a candidate building in days, and the tenant improvement allowance rarely covers the fit-out. Twenty-nine states, DC and Puerto Rico regulate the profession in some way, a handful by practice act, which shapes who may submit drawings for permit.

## Current Tech Landscape
Studio Designer (which bought Mydoma in July 2024 and with it serves nearly 20,000 designers), Houzz Pro (with Ivy), DesignFiles, Programa and DesignManager handle proposals, purchase orders, client invoices and product clipping for residential studios, mostly connected to QuickBooks. Drawing and visualisation run on AutoCAD, SketchUp, Revit, Chief Architect and Enscape, with a fast-growing set of generative rendering tools. Material Bank consolidates sample ordering across hundreds of manufacturers. Contract furniture is specified through Configura's CET Designer and dealer order systems; hospitality FF&E schedules and spec books live in Excel. Each tool owns a slice. None owns the record that joins a client's reactions to the selections, the selections to the orders, and the orders to what actually arrived and when, and none records the designer's judgement in a form a successor could use. ## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the designer's read of a client's taste: which of a thousand plausible sofas this particular client will love, and which scheme will be approved on the first presentation rather than the fourth. Every presentation produces labelled data — what the client approved, rejected, hesitated over — and almost no firm keeps it in a usable form, so the read cannot be taught to juniors, priced into fees or kept when a senior designer leaves. Fixing it is blocked structurally rather than technically: firms are small (the 50 largest hold only ~15% of revenue), the data is spread across texts, PDFs and studio software that stores selections but not reactions, and designers are rightly wary of anything that presents taste as a formula. The nearer-term commercial opening is the procurement record, where errors cost real margin every week and where studio-software vendors already compete. For an insight engine the operators are poor buyers: no firm has a research function, the insight is not invoiced separately from the design, and the Pass 2 sweep finds no qualifying pocket around them either.
