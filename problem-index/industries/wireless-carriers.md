# Wireless Carriers

## Profile
**Category:** Media, Telecom & Information
**NAICS 2022:** 517112 — Wireless Telecommunications Carriers (except Satellite)
**Market Size:** ~$308B in revenue for employer firms in 2022 (Census Service Annual Survey); BLS QCEW counted 6,560 private establishments and 79,674 employees in 2024, with authorised dealer stores counted separately; operators invested nearly $30B in their networks in 2025 and served more than 600 million connections, including nearly 16 million fixed wireless homes (CTIA 2026 Annual Survey)
**Tech Maturity:** High in the network, manual in the judgement around it — the radio network runs on vendor software, counters and self-organising features, while diagnosing a bad sector, getting new radios onto a tower and deciding which customer is about to leave still depend on a few experienced people and their spreadsheets.
**Workforce:** RF planning and optimisation engineers, cell site and switch technicians, network operations centre (NOC) staff, spectrum and regulatory specialists, real estate and site development managers, retention and pricing analysts, fraud and identity teams, care agents and company-store staff; at the roughly 2,000 fixed wireless ISPs (WISPs), an owner-operator, a network engineer and a few installers who also climb

## Key Pain Themes
A wireless network is a radio system tuned by people. Each of a few hundred thousand sites (CTIA counted ~419,000 in 2020; small cells are now about 40% of them) carries several sectors, bands and carriers, each with dozens of parameters and a stream of performance counters: dropped calls, accessibility, throughput, interference, physical resource block (PRB) utilisation. When a sector degrades, an experienced RF engineer reads the pattern and knows, often within minutes, whether the cause is an antenna tilt, passive intermodulation, an external interferer, a neighbour list error, a failing radio or a backhaul fault. Self-organising network features tune parameters within limits; they don't diagnose. The diagnosis is never written down in a form that can be joined to what happened next, the engineers who hold it are ageing or now work for the equipment vendor's managed-services arm, and a degraded sector that lingers shows up later as churn. Demand keeps rising: Americans used 159.3 trillion MB of mobile data in 2025, 20% more than in 2024, and the 16 million fixed wireless homes ride on the same sectors as the phones.

Around the network sit hard external clocks. Adding 5G radios to an existing tower needs a tower-owner application, a structural and mount analysis, landlord consent and a municipal eligible facilities request, which the FCC deems granted after 60 days; new small cells get 60 or 90 days. Twice a year every carrier files Broadband Data Collection (BDC) coverage maps built to FCC parameters (5/1 Mbps for LTE, 7/1 and 35/3 Mbps for 5G-NR, at 90% cell-edge probability and 50% loading), answers challenges within 60 days and re-files within 30 days if a verification fails. Outages above Part 4 thresholds go into the FCC's NORS system within hours. The FCC's November 2023 SIM-swap and port-out order (WC Docket 21-341) requires carriers to authenticate customers before a SIM change or port-out and to notify them immediately, but a July 2024 bureau order (DA 24-649) deferred compliance with all of its rules until OMB approval and a Federal Register compliance-date notice, and no such notice had appeared by October 2026. Spectrum itself trades on the FCC's calendar: AWS-3 Auction 113 closed on June 23, 2026 at $3.57B, and the FCC approved EchoStar's sales of spectrum to AT&T ($23B) and SpaceX in May 2026.

The customer side is a retention business. Postpaid phone churn ran at about 0.9% a month in Q3 2025 (0.92% at AT&T, 0.89% at T-Mobile), so a national carrier loses millions of lines a year, and promotions now credit devices over 24 to 36 months. Care agents untangle bills when those credits fall off, fraud teams fight port-out and SIM-swap takeovers, and WISPs qualify each rural roofline by line of sight before an installer drives out, while rural carriers defend roaming revenue and finish replacing Huawei and ZTE gear on extended FCC deadlines. Authorised dealers ([[industries/wireless-retail-agents|Wireless Retail Agents]]) and the MVNOs that resell carrier capacity ([[industries/telecom-resellers|Telecom Resellers]]) live with the same churn from the other side of the contract, and satellite direct-to-cell coverage belongs to [[industries/satellite-operators|Satellite Operators]].

## Current Tech Landscape
Ericsson, Nokia and Samsung supply the radio access network along with its management and self-organising software. Planning runs on Forsk Atoll and Infovista Planet, drive testing on Keysight Nemo, Rohde & Schwarz and Infovista TEMS, and crowdsourced benchmarking comes from Ookla and Opensignal. Service assurance comes from NetScout and the vendors' own tools, and billing and customer platforms from Amdocs, Netcracker, Ericsson and Salesforce. Identity checks come from Prove and the credit bureaus, call labelling from TNS, Hiya and First Orion, site workflow from Sitetracker and the tower companies' portals. WISPs assemble a separate stack: Ubiquiti UISP and Cambium cnMaestro for radios, Preseem for subscriber experience, TowerCoverage and Cambium cnHeat for coverage prediction, Sonar or Splynx for billing. Each tool does its job. No tool keeps the record that matters most: why a sector was changed, by whom, on what diagnosis, and whether it worked.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the RF engineer's diagnosis of a degraded sector. The counters are complete and the parameter changes are logged, but the reasoning between them is not, so a carrier can't say which engineers, playbooks or vendor optimisation teams actually fix sectors and which only move the problem. Three things block a fix. Outcome labels are confounded by traffic, season and neighbouring changes. The equipment vendors that run managed services hold the engineers and often the tooling. And the three national carriers buy almost everything through those vendors, so an independent tool must enter as an app on the vendor's platform. The stronger commercial opening for workflow and ML tools sits lower down: a few dozen regional carriers and about 2,000 WISPs run real radio networks with tiny engineering teams and no vendor optimisation staff. For a research-insight engine the carriers are poor buyers. Their analysis serves their own network and customers and is never invoiced, and the Pass 2 sweep finds the insight businesses around them either small, automated or working on carrier data they can't reuse.
