# Armored Car Services

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561613 — Armored Car Services
**Market Size:** ~$3.5B in annual receipts across 92 employer firms and 766 establishments (Census SUSB 2022), of which six enterprises with 500+ employees took ~$3.1B (~90%); Brink's reported $1.74B of 2025 revenue in its North America segment (US and Canada) and Loomis SEK 15.85B (~$1.6B, estimate) in its USA segment
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the national carriers run telematics, handheld bag scanning, high-speed sorters in their vaults and networks of smart safes, while route plans, shortage investigations and the crew's decision at the curb still run on supervisors' experience, phone calls and manifests.
**Workforce:** Armed driver-guards and messengers on two- or three-person crews, vault and cash-room processors, ATM first-line technicians, dispatchers and route supervisors, branch managers, smart-safe and ATM operations analysts, and security and loss investigators; ~26,000 employees in the code at ~$62,300 average annual pay (BLS QCEW 2024)

## Key Pain Themes
An armored carrier runs a fixed network — branches with vaults and garages, bullet-resistant trucks, armed crews licensed state by state — and fills it with stops. Brink's describes the "significant fixed costs" of the fleet and branch network in its 10-K, alongside pricing pressure in most markets. The cash is not disappearing: it was 14% of US consumer payments in 2024, steady at about seven cash payments per person per month for four years (Federal Reserve Diary of Consumer Payment Choice), and currency in circulation rose by $43.1B between mid-2024 and mid-2025. What is thinning is stop density. Retailers on smart safes are collected weekly instead of daily, bank branches close, and US deployers removed ~6,000 ATMs in 2025 (Datos Insights). Every route plan trades truck-hours against two constraints a parcel network never faces: how much value one truck may carry, and the security rule that a route must not become predictable.

The money itself moves on other people's clocks. A bank orders currency from its Federal Reserve cash office under Operating Circular 2 and pays a fee if it deposits fit $10 and $20 notes and orders the same denomination back in the same week. An ATM owner pays for a cash-out and also for idle cash sitting in a cassette. A retailer on a smart safe expects provisional credit the next business day whether or not the truck has come. The carrier sits between bank, retailer and the Fed and owns the custody record, so it is blamed for every discrepancy. When a bag comes up short at the vault, the crew who carried it are the first suspects: federal law lets armored car employers polygraph applicants who will guard currency, and any employer may ask employees with access to take one during a loss investigation.

The security clock is the industry's own. The FBI recorded 49 violations involving armored carriers under the Hobbs Act in 2023: a firearm was used in 44, violence occurred in 22, five people were injured and two killed; Monday was the most common day and 9–11 a.m. the most common window. Robbers now also jam ATMs to ambush the technicians sent to fix them, and on Easter Sunday 2024 thieves took as much as ~$30M from a GardaWorld cash facility in Sylmar, Los Angeles, through the roof. Losses feed premiums: Brink's says its own loss experience, and to a limited extent other carriers', sets its rates, mostly placed in the London specie market. Licensing is layered — state operator licences (California's Highway Patrol licenses armored car operators), state weapons permits made portable by the Armored Car Industry Reciprocity Act, federal motor carrier rules — and since February 2025 the Bank Secrecy Act. FinCEN fined Brink's Global Services USA $37M, its first enforcement action against an armored car company, for moving bulk cash across the Southwest border without registering as a [[industries/money-service-businesses|money services business]].

## Current Tech Landscape
The three national carriers sell technology as much as transport. Brink's Complete and CompuSafe smart safes and ATM managed services produced ~$1.5B, 28% of Brink's 2025 revenue. Loomis's SafePoint smart-safe line (Automated Solutions) grew double digits for sixteen straight quarters and is now a quarter of its US revenue. GardaWorld's Sesami bought the Dallas smart-safe maker Tidel in 2022. Provisional credit runs through each carrier's partnerships with banks; Loomis cites more than 165. ATM cash forecasting is sold by Morphis, by OptiCash and by ATM makers such as Hyosung, and as part of carriers' managed services; banks order from the Fed through FedCash Services. Route planning uses generic vehicle-routing tools or in-house dispatch boards. The biggest structural change is Brink's agreement in February 2026 to buy NCR Atleos for ~$6.6B including debt, joining the largest US carrier to ~78,000 owned-and-operated ATMs and an installed base of ~600,000. Each tool is competent inside its boundary. The record that matters — a bag's chain of custody joined to the route, the stop, the crew's read of the scene and what happened next — is owned by none of them.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the one made at the curb: whether a stop is safe to work, made in seconds by a messenger who has done it ten thousand times and recorded nowhere unless something goes wrong. Next to it sits the forecaster's override — the experienced cash manager who adds a margin to an ATM before a holiday weekend for reasons the model never sees. Both are hard to fix for structural reasons. Robberies are rare enough that labels are thin, aborted stops and overrides go unrecorded, and the data about any one stop is split between the bank, the retailer, the ATM owner and the carrier. The commercial shape is unusual for an operator industry: six enterprises take ~90% of receipts, so a vendor selling to carriers faces three enterprise buyers and a long tail of small regional independents. The stronger opening is on the customer side of the truck — banks, ATM owners and retailers who pay for idle cash, cash-outs and late credit — or inside the three carriers as an enterprise sale. Under the Pass 2 rubric nothing in this value chain qualifies; the insight layer around cash is thin, and the richest data sits with carriers and vendors under their clients' contracts.
