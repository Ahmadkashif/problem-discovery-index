# Professional Employer Organizations

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561330 — Professional Employer Organizations (firms that co-employ their clients' workers and take on payroll, payroll tax, benefits and workers' compensation responsibilities under a client service agreement). Census counted 2,959 firms with 3.7M employees in 2022, the co-employed worksite employees included, while BLS QCEW counts only ~422,000 employees in the code for 2024, because in client-level reporting states the workers are filed under each client's own unemployment account. Not [[industries/payroll-platforms|Payroll Platforms]], which process payroll without becoming the employer, and not [[industries/hr-consultants|HR Consultants]], who advise without co-employment.
**Market Size:** 523 PEOs co-employed 4.5M worksite employees at 208,000+ clients and paid them $308B in wages at the end of 2022 (NAPEO footprint). Census put the code's 2022 receipts at ~$190B, 94% of it at the 467 firms with 500+ employees. The five largest PEOs hold ~39% of worksite employees: ADP TotalSource averaged ~762,000 in fiscal 2026, TriNet ~334,000 on $5.0B of revenue in 2025, and Insperity ~310,000 on $6.8B. Industry gross profit, the fee plus the insurance margin, is an estimated ~$10B a year (estimate).
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — payroll, benefits enrolment and HR administration run on mature multi-tenant platforms (PrismHR says 60% of the industry uses its technology), but the decisions that set the margin are made in spreadsheets, carrier rating tools and the heads of a few senior underwriters: which prospects to admit, at what bundled price, and with what workers' comp class.
**Workforce:** Business performance advisors, sales reps and the brokers who refer prospects; new-business health and workers' comp underwriters and pricing analysts; payroll specialists and payroll tax analysts; HR specialists and benefits administrators assigned to books of client companies; workers' comp claims coordinators, risk managers and loss-control consultants; implementation teams; and the compliance, licensing and finance staff who keep the PEO certified and licensed.

## Key Pain Themes
A PEO sells small employers a bundle (payroll, HR, a large-group health plan and workers' compensation) for a per-employee fee or a percentage of payroll. Underneath, it runs an insurance pool. It folds hundreds or thousands of small clients into one master health plan and one workers' comp programme, and it keeps the spread between what it charges and what claims cost. Get that spread wrong and the business turns. Insperity's 2025 gross profit fell 14% to $900M on benefits costs, and TriNet ran an insurance cost ratio of 91%. The spread is set at the door. An underwriter looks at a prospect's census, a few health questionnaires, a prescription-based morbidity score, a carrier renewal, loss runs and an experience mod, and decides whether to admit the client and at what price. That price is generally fixed for a year (two for Insperity's middle-market clients). The claims behind it are lumpy. In one cross-PEO study, 15% of worksite groups produced 50% of claims. Of every 165 groups, 25 ran far better than forecast and 25 far worse.

Around the pool sits liability for other people's payroll on a calendar the PEO does not set. Forty-two states recognise, license or register PEOs. State unemployment tax is reported under the PEO's own account in some states, under each client's account in others, and under a hybrid in five, so a client with staff in three states can need three different arrangements. A certified PEO (CPEO) must file a quarterly assertion, with an examination-level CPA attestation, that it deposited every federal employment tax by the last day of the second month after each quarter. It must also deliver audited statements within six months of year end, or risk suspension. Clients churn: Insperity kept about 83% in 2025, so every year the PEO must re-underwrite a sixth of its book from strangers.

## Current Tech Landscape
PrismHR (with its ClientSpace workflow tool) is the default operating platform for mid-sized and smaller PEOs. The giants run their own: ADP and Paychex on their payroll stacks, Insperity moving clients onto HRScale built with Workday, TriNet on its own platform, and Vensure on a platform assembled through 108 acquisitions. Health underwriting uses carrier rating tools, third-party morbidity scores built from prescription and claims history, and consultants such as OneDigital's PEO practice. Workers' comp runs through carriers and programme administrators (Chubb has written Insperity's programme since 2007; Amwins runs a PEO programme) on master policies or multiple coordinated policies, with NCCI class codes and client-level mods. Payroll tax engines compute withholding, and ESAC accredits PEOs and bonds their tax liabilities. Each tool handles its own transaction well. None holds the record the margin depends on: what was known about each client when it was admitted and priced, and what that client then cost in health claims, comp losses and unemployment charges before it left.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is client acceptance: the new-business underwriter's call on whether a 25-person company belongs in the pool, and at what bundled rate for health, workers' comp and unemployment tax. Experienced underwriters carry a read of groups that their own models cannot match (the deck that reported the 165-group result also admits R-squared "still relatively low"), and nobody joins that read to what each client later cost. Three things block it. Health claims are protected health information and, in fully insured plans, sit with the carrier. Clients leave within a few years, often before their claims experience means anything. And sales pressure works against declining a prospect, even though the same deck says plainly "we shouldn't override declines". The large PEOs are capable buyers of underwriting and pricing tools, and the smaller ones need them most, but under the Pass 2 rubric the PEO itself is not an insight seller. Its research sits inside its premium and fees, which is why [[niches/hr-consultants/peo-compliance-research/profile|PEO Compliance & Workforce Research]] stops at 49. The pockets around it that do qualify, workers' comp rating bureaus and unemployment claims managers, are already indexed under other industries.
