# Tour Operators

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561520 — Tour operators
**Market Size:** ~$9.0B in receipts across 2,513 US employer firms with 28,158 employees (2022 Economic Census). The industry is small-firm: ~66% of its 2,584 establishments had fewer than five employees in 2023 (County Business Patterns). A few dozen large brands account for a large share of receipts, among them escorted and small-group operators, educational tour companies and expedition brands. Lindblad's five land brands alone reported $275.4M of tour revenue in 2025. Viator, the largest experience marketplace selling operators' tours, reported ~$4.7B in gross bookings in 2025 (Tripadvisor 10-K)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium. The selling edge is digital: reservation systems, marketplace connections and web booking engines are standard. The decisions that set the margin still run on spreadsheets, supplier emails and the operations manager's memory: what to cost, what to hold, which departures to run and which rooms to give back.
**Workforce:** Product managers and itinerary designers; contracting managers who negotiate hotel allotments, coach seats and ground-handler rates; reservations and sales staff serving travellers and travel advisors; operations coordinators who send rooming lists and manifests; tour directors, tour leaders and local guides, mostly seasonal contractors (~46,760 tour and travel guides nationally in May 2023, median ~$36,060, BLS); group sales staff who recruit teachers and group leaders; and finance staff managing deposits, supplier payments and currency.

## Key Pain Themes
A tour operator buys capacity before it sells it. Months before a departure the operator commits to hotel allotments with release dates, coach seats, guide days, river or rail space and restaurant blocks, and prices the package in a brochure or on a website. It then waits for the bookings to arrive. At set points before departure, ~90, 60 and 45 days out (estimate), someone decides departure by departure what happens next. One option is to guarantee the departure, telling advisors it will run so they can sell into it. Another is to consolidate it with a neighbouring date, or cancel it for failing to reach minimum numbers. Alongside that call come rooms to release before the hotel's cut-off, extra rooms to request, and whether to keep the second coach. That call is made by a product or operations manager reading the booking curve against years of memory. It decides whether the season makes money, and it is never written down beside its outcome.

Around that decision sit three clocks set by others. Suppliers' release and cancellation-penalty dates turn an unsold room into a cost on a fixed day. Final-payment dates, usually 60 to 120 days before travel (Lindblad's land brands quote that window), decide when a traveller can still walk away cheaply. Currency moves turn a tour priced in dollars a year ahead into a loss when the hotels, guides and coaches are paid in euros, pounds or rand. Distribution adds a fourth pressure. Day tours and short experiences increasingly sell through Viator, GetYourGuide and Klook at commissions in the 20-30% range. GetYourGuide moved some suppliers from 20% to 30% in July 2025. For many operators, ranking on those marketplaces now decides demand as much as the itinerary does.

The people who carry the trip are the most exposed. A tour director on a two-week escorted tour is on call around the clock, reconfirms every supplier daily, settles cash payments, handles a lost passport or a fall at two in the morning, and is then scored by travellers on a survey that also measures the weather and the hotel. In the office, operations coordinators send and correct rooming lists, flight manifests and dietary lists for dozens of departures at once, by email, and learn of an error when the group arrives at a hotel that has the wrong names.

## Current Tech Landscape
Day-tour and activity operators run reservation systems such as FareHarbor (owned by Booking Holdings since 2018), Peek, Rezdy and Checkfront (merged in 2023 with Regiondo as RC TopCo), Bókun (Tripadvisor) and Ventrata. These systems handle availability, payments, waivers and channel connections to the marketplaces. Multi-day, FIT and inbound operators use Tourplan, Tourwriter or TourCMS for costing, quoting and supplier bookings, or Excel. Larger escorted operators run in-house reservation platforms connected to travel advisors' booking tools. Hotel beds are bought direct or through bedbanks such as Hotelbeds; currency is hedged through banks or FX specialists; reviews accumulate on Tripadvisor, Google and the marketplaces. Each tool does its own part well: taking a booking, holding a rate, connecting a channel. None holds the record that matters most. That record is each departure's booking curve, the commitments behind it, what the operator decided at each checkpoint, and how the departure turned out.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the departure call. It is a dated, checkable prediction: this departure will reach its minimum, these rooms will not be needed, this date should be consolidated. The outcome arrives weeks later, as a departure that ran full, ran half empty, or was cancelled with penalties paid. Operators keep the bookings and the supplier invoices. Almost none keep the decision, so they cannot tell whether their best operations manager is right more often than a simple pace rule. When that manager leaves, their read of each itinerary's booking curve leaves too. The obstacles are structural rather than legal. The data is the operator's own, but it is split between the reservation system, the contracting spreadsheet and email. Cancelled departures never show what would have happened. Most firms have fewer than five employees, so no one is employed to look. The commercial opening is a departure-and-inventory decision tool for the mid-sized and large operators, plus a costing tool that knows currencies and a channel layer that knows commission. It is best sold through the reservation and tour-operations software vendors that already hold the data. The operators themselves are poor buyers of research. Above them, the experience marketplaces, bedbanks and software vendors hold the richest data in the chain and sell bookings and software rather than insight, so none of them clears the Pass 2 bar.
