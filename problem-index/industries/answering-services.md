# Telephone Answering Services

## Profile
**Category:** Business Support & Waste Services
**NAICS 2022:** 561421 — Telephone answering services
**Market Size:** ~$2.59B in 2022 employer receipts across 934 firms and 1,043 establishments (2022 Economic Census), plus an estimated ~$0.5-1B of nurse-triage, virtual-receptionist and AI-answering spend that sits outside the code (estimate)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the switching, scripting and secure-messaging platforms (Startel, Amtelco, Telescan) are mature and HIPAA-capable, but the decision at the centre of every call (is this urgent, and whom do I wake) is made by an operator reading a client's free-text instructions, and nothing records whether that decision was right.
**Workforce:** Telephone answering operators (switchboard operators in the BLS taxonomy) working rotating shifts, night supervisors, account setup and "programming" staff who build each client's script, quality assurance staff, owners who still take shifts, and at medical services registered nurses doing telephone triage. County Business Patterns counted 41,582 employees in 1,035 establishments in 2023; a third of establishments have fewer than five employees, and fifteen have more than five hundred.

## Key Pain Themes
An answering service sells a decision made thousands of times a night by people paid near the median for switchboard operators (~$36,750 a year per the BLS page). A caller reaches a closed medical practice, an HVAC contractor, an apartment community or a funeral home, and the operator has ~90 seconds (estimate) to decide: page the on-call clinician or technician now, hold the message for morning, send the caller to an emergency room, or patch the call through. Each client account carries its own rules ("page for any post-op fever over 101", "no-heat calls are emergencies only below 40 degrees"), and a mid-size service holds hundreds to thousands of accounts (estimate). Experienced operators learn what the scripts never say: the calm caller who is minimising, the "quick question" that is a febrile newborn, the manager who will cancel after one 3 a.m. call about a closet door. Wrong in one direction, a court can find the service negligent, as a 2017 New York decision did over messages sent to an outdated pager; wrong in the other, the client leaves after too many nights woken for nothing.

Around that decision sits a data problem nobody owns. On-call schedules arrive by fax, email, spreadsheet or scheduling portal and are keyed in by hand, so the 6 p.m. swap often never reaches the operator. Account instructions change by phone and live as prose in a notes field. The service is a HIPAA business associate for every medical client, because it stores messages and recordings and so falls outside the narrow conduit exception, yet many messages still go to pagers (Spok alone had ~720,000 units in service at the end of 2024). Third-party clocks bind the work: a hospice must make nursing available around the clock under 42 CFR 418.100, a funeral home's removal team needs a complete first call within ~an hour (estimate), and a triage nurse must be licensed where the caller is, which the Nurse Licensure Compact eases in 41 states and two territories but not everywhere.

The economics are squeezed from both sides. Operators turn over fast (estimate), taking the tacit judgement with them, and clients pay by the minute, which pays for time rather than for being right. AI voice agents for trades and medical practices quote a fraction of the live price for routine calls, while consolidation runs at the platform layer (Startel absorbed Professional Teledata in 2015 and Alston Tascom in 2017) and among services (Moneypenny bought VoiceNation in 2020).

## Current Tech Landscape
Answering services run on telephone answering service (TAS) platforms: Startel (with the Professional Teledata and Alston Tascom product lines), Amtelco's Infinity and the software-only Genesis, and Telescan's Spectrum. These provide skills-based call distribution, branching scripts by call type and time of day, on-call schedule modules, call recording and secure-messaging apps such as Amtelco Secure Messages. On the client side sit physician scheduling tools (Amion, QGenda), paging networks (Spok), EHR task inboxes, field-service platforms (ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber) and property-management systems (AppFolio, Yardi). Nurse triage runs on software carrying Schmitt-Thompson protocols, licensed through vendors such as TriageLogic and ClearTriage and used in over 400 call centres. AI voice agents (Smith.ai's hybrid service, Sameday, Avoca, Rosie, and AI built into ServiceTitan) are taking routine calls. Each tool works within its own boundary. The record no tool owns is the outcome of a disposition: whether the call held till morning was safe, whether the page that woke a surgeon was warranted, and which operator's read of which caller turned out to be right.

## Analysis
The industry's central unmeasured judgement is the escalation disposition: page now, hold, redirect or patch. It is made by the least-paid, fastest-churning person in the chain. Its correctness is defined by a client's prose instructions, and its outcome is visible only to the client, in an EHR, a field-service job or a work order the service never sees. Three things block fixing it. The outcome data is the client's, and for medical accounts it is protected health information held under a business associate agreement. Contracts priced per minute reward handling time, not right answers. And operator turnover means any learning that is not written into the system walks out with the shift. The strongest commercial opening is not the small operator, who is a weak buyer with thin margins, but the platform layer and the larger multi-site services. A platform that turns each client's instructions into an executable, testable decision tree and logs every disposition with the client's next-morning verdict would give the service a quality record it can sell at renewal, and the same machinery is what AI voice agents need before anyone will trust them with the urgent call. An insight business built on this industry's data runs into the same wall the operators do: the calls belong to the clients, so the Pass 2 sweep finds no qualifier.
