# Pension Funds

## Profile
**Category:** Financial Services
**NAICS 2022:** 525110 — Pension Funds (legal entities organised to provide retirement income benefits exclusively for the sponsor's employees or members). The code counts only 919 private establishments and 4,997 employees (BLS QCEW, 2024), because public retirement systems report under public administration and corporate plans' staff sit inside the sponsor. This hub covers the activity wherever it is filed: the investment office and benefits administration of public, corporate and multiemployer defined benefit plans. The managers they hire sit in [[industries/asset-managers|Asset Managers]] and their custodians in [[industries/trust-companies|Trust Companies]].
**Market Size:** ~$6.49T in state and local pension assets in 2025, paying $418.25B a year in benefits to more than 37 million members (Census Annual Survey of Public Pensions); private defined benefit plans held ~$2.99T across 46,233 plans and 29.3M participants in 2023, including 1,341 multiemployer plans with 10.7M participants and ~$729B (DOL Form 5500 abstract)
**Tech Maturity:** Medium — the investment book runs on custodian platforms and private markets monitoring software, and benefits on pension administration systems often decades old and now being replaced in multi-year programmes. The decisions on top of both (which fund to re-up, whether a pay item is pensionable, whether a payee is real) are made from memos, board packets and staff memory.
**Workforce:** Boards of trustees and executive directors; the CIO and asset-class investment officers, investment operations and performance staff; member counselors, benefit calculation analysts, employer reporting and audit staff, retiree payroll and death-claims processors; finance staff who feed the outside actuary; legal, compliance and IT. Corporate plans run the same work with a small treasury team and outsource most of it, and multiemployer plans run it through a fund office under joint union and employer trustees.

## Key Pain Themes
The investment office promises returns, at an assumed rate near 7% for the median large public plan (Milliman). Alternatives rose from 27% to 35% of public pension assets between 2019 and 2022 (Pew). That moved the core decision from picking a public-markets manager to committing capital to a private fund for a decade, then deciding, two to four years later, whether to commit to the same manager's next fund before the first has shown much. These decisions are recorded in board minutes and almost never graded. The best evidence says sponsors hire managers after strong past performance and the hires then deliver no excess return (Goyal and Wahal, 2008).

The benefits office promises a payment for life, calculated from decades of service and salary reported by the member's employers. Errors made at a school district's payroll desk surface at retirement: California had to move the cost of "disallowed compensation" from retirees to employers in 2022. Member service is capacity-bound. North Carolina's counseling centre took 33,149 calls in March 2026 at an average wait of over 25 minutes, and about 30% of callers hung up. Retiree payroll is now a fraud target: New Hampshire blocked faxed attempts to redirect six pensions in July 2025.

Third parties set the clocks: fiscal-year actuarial valuations and GASB 67/68 reports, a multiemployer actuary's zone certification by the 90th day of the plan year, California's annual public report of private fund fees and carry, and ILPA's new reporting template from Q1 2026. With large corporate plans around 110% funded in mid-2026, frozen plans are being prepared for exit: 2025 saw $31.3B of annuity buy-outs across 683 contracts (LIMRA). The multiemployer system is still absorbing ~$70B of Special Financial Assistance approved for 108 plans.

## Current Tech Landscape
Custodians (State Street, BNY, Northern Trust, J.P. Morgan) keep the investment book of record. Investment consultants (Meketa, NEPC, Callan, Verus, Aon, Mercer, WTW) turn it into quarterly board reports and asset-liability studies. Private markets programmes are monitored on MSCI's Burgiss, FactSet's Cobalt, Chronograph, Nasdaq's Solovis or Colmore. Benefits run on pension administration systems from Sagitec (Neospin), Vitech (V3locity, owned by Majesco since 2026), LRS (PensionGold) and others, or on in-house mainframes, with employer payroll arriving through their portals. CEM benchmarks cost and service, putting Washington at $98 per member against a $146 peer average. Each tool is competent within its boundary. No tool owns the decision record: why a fund was re-upped and what staff expected, or why a pay item or period of service was accepted and what it later did to the benefit.

## Analysis
The pension fund's central unmeasured judgement is the commitment decision, above all whether to re-up with a private markets GP while the current fund's numbers are still noise. Staff make it, a consultant endorses it, a board approves it in public, and its outcome arrives ten years later without ever being compared with what staff believed at the time. Three things block a fix. Accountability is shared, so nobody owns the grade. Labels are slow, and one plan makes too few commitments to grade one officer. And public pay scales turn investment staff over, so their reads leave with them. The benefits side has the same pattern with a longer fuse: a member record is graded only at retirement. Public plans are real but slow buyers, purchasing through procurement on multi-year cycles and asking first what their consultant and auditor will accept; corporate plans are buying exits, not tools. The Pass 2 sweep finds no qualifier. The firms that see across many plans either work on plan-owned data (actuaries, performance consultants, monitoring platforms, PRT insurers), sell to a handful of buyers (cost and longevity benchmarks), or compile public board records any competitor can read.
