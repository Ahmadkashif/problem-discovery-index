# History: Privacy Tech Vendors

**Industry:** [[industries/privacy-tech-vendors|Privacy Tech Vendors]]
**Primary Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-09-programmatic|9 — Programmatic]]
**Secondary Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-07-big-data|7 — Big Data]]

That is the finding, not a gap: privacy tech was not born by a pre-existing industry moving onto the web or digitising an existing practice. **Episode Tier:** 1
**Transferable Pattern:** When a law manufactures a market, the product it funds is compliance evidence for the law — not the underlying good the law was written to protect. Measure the two separately, because the industry's own metrics will not distinguish them for you.

## Before the Statute — a voluntary attempt that predates this by twenty years

Privacy tech has a false start worth recording precisely, because it changes the "GDPR created this industry" story from clean to instructive.

**1997.** **TRUSTe**, founded by Lori Fena (formerly of the Electronic Frontier Foundation) and Charles Jennings, launched as a non-profit industry association offering a **privacy seal** — a badge a website could display, in exchange for agreeing to disclose its data practices, meant to let consumers self-identify trustworthy sites in the absence of any binding law. It extended the model to **EU Safe Harbor certification in 2000** and **COPPA compliance seals in 2001**.

The seal model's failure mode is the one every consent-management platform in this industry inherits, unexamined, twenty-five years later: **it substituted a certification for the thing being certified.** A site could display the badge while structuring its actual privacy practices however it liked, because there was no external authority checking, no meaningful penalty for a mismatch, and the market for the seal was driven by companies wanting to *look* privacy-conscious to a public that had no independent way to verify the claim. TRUSTe (later TrustArc) survived the transition into the statutory era, but the seal-era lesson — a compliance artefact and the underlying protection it represents are not the same object, and the market will reliably underinvest in closing that gap once the artefact alone satisfies the audience it is aimed at — is this file's entire subject, restated with a bigger budget and an actual regulator behind it.

## The Origin Event — two statutes, not one moment

There is no single founding date here, and naming one would misstate the industry. There are two, nine years apart, from different jurisdictions, imposing different obligations, and a company selling into this market has to satisfy both.

**25 May 2018.** The **General Data Protection Regulation** becomes directly applicable across the EU, having entered into force two years earlier on 24 May 2016 following adoption in April 2016. GDPR requires, among a wide obligation set: consent that is "specific, freely given, plainly worded and unambiguous" (an opt-out-by-default form is explicitly non-compliant); a maintained **record of processing activities** under Article 30 — what data is held, why, where it flows, how long it is kept; and the operational machinery to honour a data subject's request to access, correct or delete their data.

**1 January 2020.** The **California Consumer Privacy Act** (signed into law 28 June 2018, as AB-375) takes effect, built on a different logic — not consent-to-collect but a right to **know, opt out of the sale of, and request deletion of** personal information, with a "Do Not Sell My Personal Information" link as its most visible, and most parodied, interface requirement. Proposition 24 (the California Privacy Rights Act) expanded it further in November 2020.

**Neither statute created a new technology.** Both created a compliance *obligation* that a company of any size now had to discharge continuously, at a scale no legal department could do by hand — and a market appeared within roughly two years to sell the discharging of it. **OneTrust, founded 2016 by Kabir Barday in Atlanta**, is the clearest case: founded in direct anticipation of GDPR taking effect, it reached over 14,000 customers by 2025 selling exactly the artefacts the two statutes above require a company to produce.

> **This is a statutory origin in the same structural sense as [[origins/hospital-systems/profile|Hospital Systems]] and HITECH** — a federal or supranational law created enforcement risk where none previously existed, and a vendor market appeared to sell the tooling that converts the law's requirements into a repeatable, auditable workflow. The difference worth naming: HITECH funded adoption with subsidy money. GDPR and CCPA fund it with penalty exposure. Both produced the same shape of market — sudden, statute-triggered, and measured by the regulator's own enforcement calendar rather than by customer demand for the underlying good.

## What Became Cheap

**Producing the document a regulator asks for.** Before 2018, assembling a data-processing inventory, a consent log, or a request-fulfilment trail meant bespoke legal and engineering work, commissioned once, going stale immediately. After it, a platform could generate the record of processing, log every consent event at web scale, route a deletion request through a defined workflow, and produce all of it on demand for an auditor. **What did not get cheaper is knowing whether the underlying map is accurate, the consent was informed, or the deletion actually happened everywhere the data went.** That gap is this industry's entire remaining story, and it is structurally identical to TRUSTe's seal twenty years earlier: the artefact scaled. The thing the artefact claims to represent did not.

## The Binding Constraint

**The product this industry sells is compliance evidence, not privacy.** That is not a cynical reading — it is a description of what the tooling was built to produce, and it explains three separate patterns this vault's own industry note has already documented independently:

- **The consent banner is optimised for acceptance rate, not comprehension.** Regulators in multiple jurisdictions have found, repeatedly and consistently enough to constitute a body of enforcement precedent, that specific interface choices — pre-ticked boxes, a prominent "accept" against a buried "reject," bundled purposes presented as one choice — do not obtain valid consent under GDPR's own "freely given and unambiguous" standard. The vendor supplies a configurable banner; the customer configures it toward the acceptance number their business needs; the platform's own dashboard reports the resulting rate as a compliance success metric, because from the platform's side, a captured consent event *is* the compliant outcome, whatever the shopper understood.
- **The record of processing is a survey artefact.** It is assembled by interviewing engineering and product teams about what systems exist and what they do with data, and it is wrong before it is finished, because systems are created, connected and retired continuously and no interview cadence keeps pace. Every downstream obligation — a deletion request, a transfer assessment, an incident response — rests on a map that was accurate on the day someone answered a questionnaire.
- **Deletion requires deletion capability, and much of the infrastructure being asked was not built with it.** Backups, analytics warehouses, logs and third-party processors routinely have no clean per-person delete path. The workflow tool can route the request and mark it complete. Whether the data is actually gone from every system it reached is a fact the tool was never positioned to verify — it orchestrates a request that, in practice, ends with an engineer writing a one-off script and no independent check that the script covered everything.

**The IAB's own consent framework supplies the sharpest instance of this pattern, and it belongs to the party writing the industry's own standards, not a vendor's customer.** IAB Europe's **Transparency and Consent Framework**, published November 2017 to help the advertising industry obtain and record GDPR-compliant consent at scale, was itself found deficient by the **Belgian Data Protection Authority in 2022**, which fined IAB Europe €250,000 for "multiple failings" in the framework's own compliance design. The standard-setting body for consent management could not make its own consent mechanism satisfy the law it was built to satisfy. **The pattern this industry sells a fix for reproduced itself one layer up, inside the standard.**

## The Transferable Pattern

> **When a market exists because a statute created it, ask what the statute actually requires a company to produce — a document, a rate, a log — and separate that sharply from the outcome the statute was written to achieve. The vendor's product is almost always the former. The customer's regulator checks the former. Nobody's metrics track the latter, which is exactly why it can degrade for years without appearing anywhere as a failure.**

This is a sharper version of the pattern [[origins/hospital-systems/profile|Hospital Systems]] and HITECH already taught this vault: subsidy or penalty can move an adoption curve fast, but the curve it moves is *adoption of the required artefact*, not automatically the underlying outcome the law wanted. Dental practices, in this vault's own history, show the same statutory logic running in reverse — an industry the statute mostly excluded, with the clinical record layer visibly underdeveloped thirty years later as a direct consequence. Privacy tech is the case where the statute did not exclude anyone: the market is universal, well-funded, statutorily guaranteed, mature as workflow software — and still resting on an input, the map of what a company actually does with data, that nobody has found a way to make current rather than periodically re-surveyed.

An FDE evaluating a compliance-driven category should ask, before building anything: **is the customer buying evidence for an auditor, or a change in the underlying practice — and does the vendor's own product roadmap suggest which one it believes it is selling?**
