# History: Staffing Agencies

> **Origin Parent — omitted.** None of the eighteen `origins/` fits, for the opposite reason to most other omissions in this batch: staffing is **older** than every origin in the spine, not younger. Manpower (1948) and Kelly Services (1946) predate SABRE (1957–60) by a decade. Its computing history was not inherited from a parent industry; it arrived on its own schedule, described below.

## Before

**Kelly Services** began in 1946 when William Russell Kelly founded "Russell Kelly Office Service," providing temporary clerical help — initially performed at Kelly's own office, later at the client's, as demand grew. **Manpower** followed in 1948 in Milwaukee, founded by Elmer Winter and Aaron Scheinfeld: the most-told version of the story has the two lawyers needing temporary secretarial help to file a brief with the Wisconsin Supreme Court under time pressure, raising $7,000 and opening an office on the strength of it.

Both had to sell employers on an idea that met real resistance at the time: borrowing a worker for a day or a week, rather than hiring and firing directly, when corporations of the era generally valued employment stability and treated outside labour as suitable only for covering a sick day, not for anything more.

Kelly's temporary workers — predominantly women in this period — became known as **"Kelly Girls,"** and the name outlived the company that coined it, generalising into the industry's own word for a temporary worker before "temp" displaced it. The same naming pattern recurs elsewhere in this vault: the freight-brokerage "load board" outlived the actual corkboard it was named after by nearly fifty years.

The industry needed lobbying muscle almost immediately, and this is the closest thing this file has to a competitive fight — except the opponent was not a company. In **1966**, agency operators founded the **Institute of Temporary Services** in Washington, DC, specifically to contest the US Employment Service — the federal government's free public job-placement service — expanding into territory the private agencies considered their own market, without the tax disadvantages private firms carried. The organisation renamed itself the National Association of Temporary Services (1970), the National Association of Temporary and Staffing Services (1994), and finally the **American Staffing Association (1999)**, tracking the industry's own broadening scope as its trade body's name changed to match. **The founding fight here was regulatory, staged against a public-sector competitor with no fee, not a market fight against a rival firm** — the same shape H2 found for telecom carriers and pharma CROs, and worth naming for the same reason: not every origin has a duel.

## The Origin Event — Computing Arrives Fifty Years Later

Two separate technologies did to staffing what a corkboard did to freight in 1978: they made discovery searchable.

**Job boards** put postings and resumes on the open web for the first time — Monster.com launched in 1994 as "The Monster Board," CareerBuilder followed in 1995 — replacing a filing cabinet and a classified section with something both sides could search.

**Bullhorn**, founded in 1999 by Roger Colvin, Barry Hinckley and Art Papas, carries a small irony worth keeping in the file: it launched as a freelance-collaboration marketplace — the same category as [[industries/freelance-marketplaces|Freelance Marketplaces]] — and pivoted in 2001 to building CRM/ATS software specifically for the staffing and recruiting vertical, when the marketplace model it first tried did not take off. It grew from $2M revenue (2004) to $67M (2013) serving 6,000 customers in 34 countries, and is now, alongside Avionté and TempWorks, the dominant system of record this vault's own hub note describes.

## What Became Cheap

Searching. A recruiter could query thousands of resumes by keyword instead of paging through a card file or relying on memory of who they placed two years ago.

## What Did Not Become Cheap — and this is the vault's own finding

Matching remained a **vocabulary problem**, not a search problem, and no amount of ATS adoption has closed it. This vault's own high-impact note states the gap precisely: **"forklift operator with 3 years warehouse experience"** and **"material handler — distribution center, certified reach truck"** describe the same person to an experienced recruiter and describe two different people to keyword search. Firms holding 50,000+ candidate records reportedly surface only a small fraction of viable matches for exactly this reason. Cheap search made the haystack bigger. It did not make the needle easier to describe.

## The Binding Constraint

The staffing agency sits legally between two employers of the same worker, and almost everything expensive about running one is a consequence of that position, not of its software. A single worker can be assigned to three different client sites in a week, each with its own overtime rule, meal-and-break requirement and workers'-compensation class code; the agency's own bill rate to the client has to absorb state unemployment tax (SUTA) variation, ACA obligations, and a burden cost that changes per placement. This vault's own low-impact-2 note is precise about the consequence: an account manager quoting a bill rate on the phone is "guessing at the true margin" because the real cost lives in five different systems and one senior person's head.

**Worker classification is the sharper version of the same constraint.** Whether a placed worker is legally the staffing agency's employee, the client's, or jointly both determines who is liable for wage-and-hour violations, who owes payroll tax, and who bears workers'-compensation risk — and the answer has moved under the industry's feet more than once as the joint-employer standard has shifted at the NLRB and in state law. No ATS resolves this. It is decided in labour law, not in code, which is why this vault carries it as its own advisory niche rather than folding it into a matching problem.

## Why There Is No Named Rival, Even Now

Staffing agencies compete on placements per branch, not on owning a platform, and the software vendors (Bullhorn, Avionté, TempWorks) compete with each other one layer up from the roughly 25,000 agencies that are this vault's actual subject. There is no Convoy here — no company that tried to disrupt staffing with an algorithm and burned nine figures proving the relationship layer could not be replaced by one. That absence is itself informative: the parts of the job that resist automation — knowing which candidate will actually show up, which client site is misreporting its own overtime policy — are exactly the parts a venture-scale bet in this industry would have to automate, and nobody has staked that bet here at anything like the scale freight brokerage saw.

## The Transferable Pattern

> **Before proposing a matching algorithm, find out whether the bottleneck is search or vocabulary. Cheap search does nothing for a business whose real problem is that the buyer and the seller describe the same fact in two different languages.**

A second, sharper pattern belongs to this industry specifically, because it is one of this batch's three labour-matching platforms. The agency knows the bill rate it charges the client and the pay rate it gives the worker; the spread between them is the business, and the worker essentially never sees it. This is a milder version of the **asymmetric hold** that recurs across this batch — milder because a human recruiter, not an algorithm, still makes the placement decision, and because a worker can often simply ask their recruiter directly, even where the answer is not owed to them as a matter of policy.
