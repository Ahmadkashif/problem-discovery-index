## Before

Local newspapers ran on a bundle, not a single product. Subscribers paid for news; advertisers paid for two entirely different things bundled into the same physical object — **classified advertising** (want ads, real-estate listings, job postings, obituaries) and **display advertising** (the department-store half-page, the car dealer's spread). Classifieds were low-effort, high-margin inventory: a few lines of text sold by the word, requiring no creative production. Both products existed because the newspaper held a **local monopoly on discovery** — if you wanted to sell a car or reach a shopper in a specific town, the local paper was close to the only channel that existed.

## The Origin Event — Two of Them, a Decade Apart, Neither Built to Compete With a Newspaper

**Craigslist**, founded in 1995 by Craig Newmark as an email list of San Francisco Bay Area events for friends, went live as a website in 1996 and expanded to nine US cities by 2000 and fourteen more by 2003, introducing paid job listings in New York and Los Angeles in 2004. It was never conceived as a competitor to a newspaper's classified section — it displaced one as a side effect of being free, without ever employing a reporter or selling an ad against local news content.

A decade later, the mechanism this vault's own [[history/programmatic-ad-platforms|history of programmatic ad platforms]] documents in full did the same thing to the other half of the bundle. **Right Media** went live with a real-time auction for display impressions on 1 April 2005; **Google's DoubleClick Ad Exchange** brought it to mass scale in 2009. Once an advertiser could buy an audience wherever it appeared rather than a placement in a specific paper, a local publisher's inventory competed in the same auction as every other page on the internet, priced by a mechanism with no concept of "local" as a category worth a premium.

**Neither event was an attack on journalism.** Newmark built a favour for friends. Right Media built a way to sell remnant banner inventory. Both happened to sit exactly on top of the two products a newspaper actually sold, and both destroyed the economics of local reporting as an unintended consequence of solving someone else's unrelated problem.

## Why There Is No Fight to Win

**This is not a story about journalism quality, and an episode must say so directly rather than let it be implied.** No competitor out-reported the local paper. No algorithm wrote better local stories than a beat reporter. The category a local paper occupied — "the place that reaches everyone in this town" — was unbundled from the town entirely: classifieds became a global directory, and display advertising became a global auction that priced attention, not geography. A local newspaper's revenue depended on both functions requiring physical or institutional locality. Once neither did, the newspaper kept the cost of local reporting and lost both businesses that used to pay for it.

## The Graveyard

This is the one industry in this batch with a real, countable graveyard, and the count is large.

**More than 3,200 US print newspapers have closed since 2005** — roughly 130 in 2024 alone, close to two a week — leaving fewer than 5,600 in operation, 80% of them weeklies. **206 counties** have no local news source producing original reporting at all, affecting more than 3.5 million people; a further 1,561 counties have only one. Newspaper industry employment fell **73% from 2005 to 2023** (to 98,960 positions); newsroom jobs specifically fell **60%**, more than 45,000 positions gone.

Pew's parallel 2008–2020 figures make the sectoral shift precise: newspaper newsroom employment fell from roughly 71,000 to 31,000 (**–57%**), broadcast TV held flat around 28,000, and digital-native newsrooms grew 144% — from 7,400 to about 18,000 — without coming close to replacing what newspapers lost. **Total newsroom employment across all media, the aggregate figure most often quoted alone, fell only 26% over the same period** — a number that flatters the industry by hiding which sector actually did the dying. Advertising revenue tells the same story from the money side: **US newspaper ad revenue peaked around $48.7 billion in 2000 and had fallen to roughly $9.8 billion by 2022** — an 80% collapse.

The ownership structure of what survives is its own finding. **Ten companies now control roughly a quarter of all US newspapers and more than half of all dailies**, and several of the largest are private-equity- or hedge-fund-owned. **Alden Global Capital**, founded 2007 by Randall Smith, acquired MediaNews Group out of bankruptcy in 2010 and completed a $635M acquisition of Tribune Publishing in May 2021, becoming the second-largest US newspaper publisher by circulation and placing titles including the *Chicago Tribune*, *Denver Post* and *Baltimore Sun* under a real-estate-monetisation strategy — its Twenty Lake Holdings unit has sold off roughly 180 newspaper properties — layered on aggressive staff reduction. **Alden did not cause the classifieds-and-auction collapse described above. It is what moves into a local monopoly once the monopoly's revenue is gone and only the real estate and the brand remain valuable.**

## What Got Built Instead

Nothing above got reversed by software, and the tools that did get adopted are coping tools, in the same register as this vault's dental-practices finding. CMS platforms (WordPress, Arc Publishing) handle multi-platform publishing; Chartbeat and Google Analytics measure engagement after the fact; Sailthru and Mailchimp run newsletter distribution. This vault's own hub note is exact about the gap all of this leaves: an editor allocating scarce reporter-hours has "almost no quantitative signal about which stories will drive subscriptions" and still works from "gut instinct honed over decades" — because none of the adopted tools were built to answer that question, only to publish and measure whatever was already decided.

## The Transferable Pattern

> **Find out which of a business's revenue lines are actually a fee for scarce local access, and which are a fee for a directory or reach service that someone with more reach can produce more cheaply. The first survives disintermediation. The second doesn't, and no amount of technology adopted afterward buys it back.**

For an FDE this is the sharpest possible caution against a pitch that "AI will save local news" without first identifying which half of the old bundle it is trying to rebuild. A story-engagement model helps an editor spend scarce reporter-hours better — a real, buildable improvement this vault's own high-impact note describes. It does not reprice a classified ad, and it does not win back an advertiser who can now buy the same audience, cheaper, from an exchange that has never heard of the town. Two different technologies destroyed two different revenue lines, a decade apart, and neither destroyer was trying to be in the news business. The correct model for what comes next has to be built for the business that is actually left, not the one that used to exist.
