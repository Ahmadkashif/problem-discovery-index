## Before

Before the PC, a small firm's economics ran on paper time sheets filled in from memory at the end of the day, a paper docket for deadlines, and — if the firm did legal research at all beyond its own library — a trip to a law library's reporters and digests, or a phone call to a librarian at a bigger firm. Billing was frequently a **flat or minimum fee set by the local bar's fee schedule**, not an itemised hourly invoice; the schedule, not the clock, was the unit of account.

## The Origin Event — a court case, not a computer

**June 16 1975. *Goldfarb v. Virginia State Bar.*** The US Supreme Court held that a bar association's minimum-fee schedule was price-fixing under the Sherman Act — legal services were "trade or commerce," and the profession's antitrust exemption did not survive the ruling. Fee schedules that had set a price floor for routine legal work (the case concerned a title search) became illegal essentially overnight.

**This, not any software product, is why the billable hour became the industry's dominant pricing unit.** Once a bar-set schedule could no longer set the price, hourly time became the least legally exposed way for a firm to justify a bill to a client or a fee-dispute panel: a contemporaneous log is evidence a fixed schedule never needed to be. *(The billable hour's earlier history — commonly credited in law-firm-management literature to Reginald Heber Smith's writing and a 1958 ABA recommendation that firms move toward hourly billing — could not be verified to a primary source in this session; treat pre-1975 dating as unconfirmed and Goldfarb as the one hard date.)*

Small firms adopted timekeeping software for the same reason big firms did, a decade or more later and with far less back-office support: **Timeslips**, one of the earliest dedicated legal timekeeping products, dates from the mid-1980s PC era, giving a solo practitioner a fraction of a large firm's billing department in a box. *(Founding year not independently reconfirmed in this session.)*

**Cloud practice management arrived specifically for this segment far later. Clio**, founded around **2008**, is commonly credited as one of the first cloud-based legal practice management platforms — built to give a solo or small firm the matter-management, billing and trust-accounting stack that only a larger firm's IT department could previously assemble. *(Founders and precise founding details are drawn from company and legal-press sources; not independently re-verified to a primary record in this session.)*

## What Became Cheap

**Recording that work happened.** Digital timekeeping turned "what did I do today" from an end-of-day memory exercise into a running log — in principle. In practice this vault's own hub note records that small-firm attorneys still **under-bill by 20–35%**, losing **$50,000–$150,000 a year per attorney**, because entering time in real time competes with billable work itself for the same minutes. The tool made recording cheap. It did not make remembering to record cheap, and that gap is the whole worker-life problem this vault documents for the industry.

## The Trade-Off

**The billable hour rewards exactly the behaviour a client does not want, and it has survived forty years of criticism anyway.** An hourly unit prices slowness the same as diligence, and every serious critique of law-firm economics says so. What has not displaced it, at least for small-firm litigation and general-practice work, is any alternative fee arrangement — flat fees, contingency, subscription retainers exist and this vault's own hub note flags the resulting **cash-flow unpredictability** for firms mixing them with hourly work, but none has become the default the way hourly billing did after 1975. The honest account is that the billable hour is criticised constantly and replaced rarely, because it remains the one unit a client's own bill-review software (see this vault's [[niches/small-law-firms/legal-bill-review-spend-management/profile|Legal Bill Review & Spend Management]] niche) can actually audit line by line.

## The Binding Constraint

**IOLTA trust-account reconciliation rules.** Every US state requires client trust funds to be held separately from firm operating funds, reconciled — commonly monthly, three ways (trust ledger, client ledger, bank statement) — under rules set by the state bar, not by any software vendor. **Trust-accounting violations are the single most common basis for attorney discipline regardless of intent** — a strict-liability-flavoured rule that punishes a bookkeeping error the same as theft, because the bar cannot easily tell the two apart from the outside.

This is why the spreadsheet survives here even inside firms that have adopted Clio, PracticePanther or MyCase: **many solo and small-firm bookkeepers keep a shadow reconciliation spreadsheet for the trust account**, because a general practice-management tool's trust module has to match a specific state's exact reconciliation format and audit trail, and a spreadsheet a bookkeeper built themselves is easier to defend to a bar auditor than a vendor's black box the bookkeeper cannot fully explain. The rule long predates the software; the software has to prove itself against a rule it did not write.

## Why There Is No Fight, and One Adjacent Contest Worth Naming

**Small firms are not fighting each other over technology**, and no vendor has displaced a rival small-firm platform the way Excel displaced Lotus. Clio, PracticePanther and MyCase compete on features, not on a market-defining event. Absence recorded, not manufactured.

**One real, well-documented contest sits adjacent to this industry rather than inside it: *Zubulake v. UBS Warburg* (2003–2005, SDNY, Judge Shira Scheindlin).** The case's four major opinions defined modern e-discovery — a litigant's duty to preserve electronic evidence once litigation is reasonably foreseeable, and a seven-factor test for shifting the cost of recovering hard-to-access data. It produced a **$29.3M jury verdict** and, well before the 2006 amendments to the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, set the terms every e-discovery vendor now sells against. It is largely **not this industry's fight** — small firms rarely run the kind of litigation that generates Zubulake-scale discovery — but it explains why an entire adjacent niche, [[niches/small-law-firms/e-discovery-providers/profile|E-Discovery Providers]], exists at the edge of this vault's small-firm map: the obligation Zubulake created scales down to any firm handling even modest civil litigation, just at a much smaller volume.

## The Transferable Pattern

> **When a court or a regulator removes a pricing floor, look at what fills the vacuum — it is rarely the most efficient unit, it is the most defensible one.** Hourly billing did not win because it is the best way to price legal work. It won because after 1975 it was the version a lawyer could prove in a fee dispute.

The FDE lesson generalises past law: any professional-services pricing model an AI product wants to replace should first be checked for whether it exists because it is efficient, or because it survives an audit. Automating away the audit trail — even in the name of speed — is the one mistake that gets a vendor fired by a bar complaint rather than by a competitor.
