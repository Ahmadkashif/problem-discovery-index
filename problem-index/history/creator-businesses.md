> **Origin Parent — omitted.** None of the eighteen `origins/` industries has a real claim on this one. A creator business is not a computerised version of a pre-existing 20th-century industry the way a dental practice or an airline is; it is a genuinely new occupation that Wave 10's platforms manufactured directly, wholesale, within the span this vault already covers. The closest pre-digital analogue — a working musician, a syndicated columnist, a touring comedian — never had to negotiate with an algorithm for permission to be seen, which is precisely the discontinuity this file is about.

## Before the Channel Was a Business

Making a living from an audience required an institution willing to carry the distribution cost and take a cut of the result: a record label pressed and shipped the vinyl, a newspaper syndicate sold the column into a hundred markets, a television network bought the airtime. The institution's willingness to say yes was the scarce resource. Reaching people you had not already met, at your own initiative, with no one's permission, was not something an individual could do at any price that made a living plausible.

## The Origin Event — a Payout, Not a Company

There is a specific date, and it belongs to a feature, not a founding. **The YouTube Partner Program**, which had existed in a limited invite-only form, **expanded on 10 December 2007** to a broader set of uploaders, attaching a revenue share to advertising run against user-uploaded video for the first time at meaningful scale. That is the moment uploading stopped being a hobby with an audience and became, for a small number of people, an occupation with a paycheck.

Two further platforms completed the shape within six years. **Twitch**, founded in 2011 as a spinout of Justin.tv focused specifically on live game streaming, was **acquired by Amazon in August 2014 for $970 million** — a purchase price that told the market a continuous, unscripted, audience-supported broadcast was a business category, not a curiosity. **Patreon launched in May 2013**, founded by musician Jack Conte specifically because he was, in his own account, looking for a way to make a living directly from the audience his YouTube videos had built rather than from the platform's advertising split — the first widely adopted mechanism for a creator to be paid by their audience instead of by a platform's advertisers.

By 2013, in other words, all three of the revenue mechanisms a creator business still runs on today — ad share, live/audience-subscription, and direct patronage — existed and were live.

## What Became Cheap

**Getting paid for an audience you built yourself, without anyone else's institutional sign-off.** Production tools became cheap in parallel — a laptop and a camera replaced a studio — but that is a smaller and less interesting change than the payment mechanism. The scarce resource stopped being institutional permission and became, instead, whatever a recommendation system decided to do with your upload.

## The Contest — Over the Relationship, Not the Content

This vault's own hub note for the industry names the mechanism precisely: "the creator chooses, the platform decides how many people see it, and the analytics return click-through and retention curves that conflate the creator's decision with the platform's distribution choice." That is the whole contest. It is not creator versus creator, and it is not creator versus advertiser. It is **creator versus the ranking system that sits between the creator and the audience the creator believes is theirs.**

The take rates make the economics of that relationship explicit, though all figures here are approximate and move with programme terms and date: YouTube's ad-revenue split runs roughly 55/45 in the creator's favour; Twitch's subscription split is 50/50 as the standard tier; Patreon takes roughly 5–12% of what a patron pays. On top of every one of these, **Apple's 30% App Store cut applies to in-app purchases on iOS**, a tax layered on top of the platform's own take that neither YouTube, Twitch nor Patreon controls and none can waive on the creator's behalf.

## The Trade-Off

Every distribution channel available to a creator business trades reach for ownership, and no channel offers both. YouTube, TikTok and Instagram carry an existing, algorithmically-fed audience of strangers who might discover you, at the cost of a take rate, an opaque ranking system, and zero portability if the account is suspended or the algorithm's preferences shift. Patreon and a creator's own email list carry no discovery mechanism at all — the audience must already know to look — but the relationship is contractually and technically the creator's, transferable to another platform overnight. **Substack's founding pitch, built explicitly on this trade-off, is that a list is worth more than reach because a list cannot be deranked.** A creator business is, in practice, always running both sides simultaneously: platform video to be found, and an owned channel to survive being un-found.

## The Graveyard — a Corpse That Teaches the Wrong Lesson If You Are Not Careful

**Vine** is the case study this industry reaches for, and it needs handling with the same discipline this vault applied to it in Wave 8's file. Vine launched in January 2013, was acquired by Twitter before it even shipped (October 2012, roughly $30 million), reached 200 million active users by December 2015, and was discontinued in October 2016, with uploads ending 17 January 2017. The popular version of this story is "the platform's algorithm starved Vine's creators of reach and killed the app." **That version is not what the evidence supports.** Vine's decline is documented as **Twitter's own monetisation and strategic failure — it never built a creator payment mechanism at all**, while Instagram (which added 15-second video in June 2013) and the wider YouTube/Snapchat ecosystem simply gave creators somewhere to be paid instead. By mid-2016, more than half of Vine's accounts with 15,000+ followers had stopped posting or deleted their accounts, and named creators — David Dobrik, Jake Paul, Liza Koshy among them — decamped to YouTube specifically.

The corpse is real. The cause of death commonly attached to it — an algorithmic distribution cut, on the model of the 2017 adpocalypse — is not. **Vine died of never building the thing this whole industry is organised around: a way to pay the people who made it worth using.**

## The Transferable Pattern

> **When a business's central input is a decision made by a system that reports outcomes but not mechanisms, the highest-value product is not more content — it is the inference layer that recovers the mechanism from the business's own historical record.**

This vault's own analysis of the industry states the opportunity almost exactly: a creator with a few hundred uploads already holds the experimental record — topic, title, thumbnail, length and posting time, each followed by a measured outcome — needed to separate the creative decision from the platform's distribution choice. Almost none of them build it, because the tooling that exists (VidIQ, TubeBuddy, Social Blade) analyses what is publicly visible rather than modelling the confound directly. An FDE walking into this industry is not being asked to make better content. They are being asked to build the causal-inference tool the platform will never build for you, because the platform has no incentive to show you which of its own decisions overrode yours.
