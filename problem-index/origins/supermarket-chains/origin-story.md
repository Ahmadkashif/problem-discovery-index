# Origin Story: A Packet of Gum

**Origin:** [[origins/supermarket-chains/profile|Supermarket Chains]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-02-departmental-item-level|2 — Departmental & Item-Level]]

## What Was True Before

A cashier read the price off a stamp on each item and keyed it in. Every keystroke was an opportunity for error, checkout speed was bounded by typing, and repricing meant re-stamping the stock by hand.

More consequentially: **the store had no idea what it sold.** It knew its total takings and it knew what it had ordered. Between those two numbers sat thousands of items and a fog. Inventory was counted by people walking the aisles with clipboards, periodically, and the count was wrong by the time it was finished. Shrink was whatever did not reconcile.

Buying decisions were made by experienced people using judgement, because there was no alternative.

## The Standard Before the Scan

The barcode concept dates to a 1952 patent by Norman Joseph Woodland and Bernard Silver. It sat unused for two decades — a solution with no economic reason to exist until checkout labour and inventory error grew expensive enough.

What made it real was **an industry agreeing on one format.** A grocery-industry ad hoc committee selected the UPC symbol and format in **1973**; IBM's George Laurer designed the final symbol. That is the actual achievement: not the invention, but competitors agreeing to a shared standard none of them owned.

## The Moment

**June 26 1974, 8:01am.** At a Marsh Supermarket in Troy, Ohio, Clyde Dawson — Marsh's head of R&D — passed a 10-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit across an NCR scanner. Cashier Sharon Buchanan rang it up: **67 cents.**

The Smithsonian holds the receipt and the packet of gum.

*(The story is unusually well corroborated for a founding anecdote. Some retellings differ on whether it was 8:01 or "just after 8am." That is the entire extent of the dispute.)*

## Why It Mattered

Three things arrived at once, and only the first was the stated goal.

**Checkout got faster and more accurate.** This was the business case, it was real, and it is the least important consequence.

**The store learned what it sold.** Item-level, store-level, hour-level. Shrink became measurable rather than residual. Reordering could be automatic. The clipboard stopped being the source of truth.

**The data became a product.** Once thousands of stores held item-level sales records, someone would aggregate and sell them back to manufacturers, who had *never* been able to see how their products actually performed at the shelf. **IRI was founded in 1979** to do exactly that, and Nielsen followed the same logic.

That third consequence was nobody's plan. It is the most valuable one.
