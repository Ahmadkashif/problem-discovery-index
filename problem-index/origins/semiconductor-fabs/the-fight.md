# The Fight: American Fabs Against Japanese Yield

**Origin:** [[origins/semiconductor-fabs/profile|Semiconductor Fabs]]
**Outcome:** American DRAM manufacturers lost the memory-chip market through the 1980s. The industry's response was SEMATECH — a consortium, not a single winner.

## The Opening

Through the 1970s, American semiconductor companies led the world in memory chips. That changed in the early 1980s, and the moment that made the change undeniable to American industry has a name and a date.

## The Anderson Bombshell

In **1977**, Hewlett-Packard began building a computer around 16-kilobit RAM chips and, unable to source enough acceptable chips from American suppliers, turned to Japanese manufacturers as a second source. HP noticed something unexpected: the Japanese chips passed HP's own incoming inspection at a far higher rate, and the finished machines ran far longer without a memory failure than comparable machines built with American chips.

In **March 1980**, HP executive **Richard W. Anderson** presented these findings to a meeting of American electronics executives in Washington, D.C. — a talk since remembered as **"the Anderson Bombshell."** His figures: Japanese 16K RAM chips were beating American-made chips on quality and reliability by a margin reported at roughly **six to one**.

This was not a claim about price or volume, where the American industry could at least contest the numbers. It was a claim about **defect rate** — about whether the chip worked at all — and it came from a customer, not a competitor, which made it very difficult for American manufacturers to dismiss.

## What It Cost

The American DRAM industry did not recover the ground it lost in the early 1980s. By the middle of the decade several major American manufacturers had exited the commodity DRAM business entirely rather than continue competing on a cost and yield basis they could not match — a retreat, not a comeback.

**The lesson the industry drew was specifically about process, not product design.** Japanese manufacturers were not shipping a fundamentally different chip. They were running a more disciplined manufacturing process — one that caught and corrected deviation earlier and more consistently — and turning that discipline directly into a lower defect rate at the same design.

## The Response: a consortium, not a competitor

Unlike the airlines' fight, this one did not resolve into a single winning company. The American industry's collective response, formed in **1987**, was **SEMATECH** — a nonprofit research consortium of fourteen founding members (AT&T Microelectronics, AMD, IBM, Digital Equipment, Harris Semiconductor, Hewlett-Packard, Intel, LSI Logic, Micron, Motorola, NCR, National Semiconductor, Rockwell and Texas Instruments), funded initially through the Defense Department's DARPA and later by member dues, built explicitly to close the manufacturing-quality gap that the Anderson Bombshell had made impossible to ignore.

SEMATECH's approach was itself telling: rather than one company trying to out-invent the others, fierce competitors agreed to jointly fund and share **process-control research** — the run-to-run and fault-detection work in [[origins/semiconductor-fabs/the-mechanism|The Mechanism]] — on the theory that manufacturing discipline, unlike product design, was not where any of them wanted to compete.

## The Honest Caveat

**This is not a story with a named corporate loser the way People Express is.** No single American chipmaker's collapse is directly and solely attributable to the Anderson Bombshell; the DRAM exits of the mid-1980s had multiple causes, including Japanese firms' capital-intensive scaling and, separately, pricing practices later contested through US anti-dumping actions. What is well documented and safe to state is narrower and still significant: **a customer's quality measurement in 1980 became the specific, named catalyst for an industry-wide, government-supported consortium formed in 1987 to fix a shared manufacturing weakness** — a very different shape of "fight" from a market contest between two named rivals.
