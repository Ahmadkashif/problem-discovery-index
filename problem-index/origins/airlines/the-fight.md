# The Fight: American Airlines versus People Express

**Origin:** [[origins/airlines/profile|Airlines]]
**Outcome:** American won. People Express ceased to exist on Feb 1 1987.

## The Opening

The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 removed the federal government's control over routes and fares. For the first time an airline could fly where it liked and charge what it liked, and a new kind of competitor became possible: the low-cost carrier with no legacy cost base.

**People Express**, founded April 30 1981 in Newark by Donald Burr and colleagues from Texas International, was the most successful of them. It flew with high seat density, minimal service, cross-trained staff, and fares the incumbents could not profitably match. By 1985–86 it was carrying roughly four million passengers a year, had acquired Frontier Airlines, and was flying 747s across the Atlantic.

It was, in Burr's own description, *"a vibrant, profitable company from 1981 to 1985."*

## The Weapon

American Airlines could not cut its costs to People Express's level. Its cost base was structural — unionised labour, full-service operations, an established network.

So it did not compete on cost. **It competed on the allocation of inventory**, which is a computing problem, and which People Express's simpler systems could not do at all.

Robert Crandall had sponsored the modernisation of SABRE in 1973 and introduced discounted **Super Saver** fares in 1975. In **1985** American launched **Ultimate Super Saver** fares, priced *below* People Express's fares — but released in strictly limited numbers, controlled by a system called **DINAMO** (Dynamic Inventory Optimization and Maintenance Optimizer).

The insight is elegant and slightly brutal. American did not need to sell every seat at People Express prices. It needed to sell *enough* seats at those prices to take the price-sensitive passenger, while protecting the rest of the aircraft for the late-booking business traveller paying many times more. A low-fare carrier with one fare class cannot answer that; it has already sold the seat.

## The Collapse

Burr understood immediately what was happening. On seeing the Ultimate Super Saver launch, per Robert Cross's account:

> *"This is it! This is a shot across our bow! If we don't invent a way to deal with [yield management], we're history!"*

And afterwards:

> *"We had been profitable from the day we started until American came at us with Ultimate Super Savers... then we tipped right over into losing $50 million a month."*

In June 1986 People Express was quietly shopped for sale. Texas Air, run by Frank Lorenzo — Burr's former boss — offered $301M in cash and stock. On **Feb 1 1987** People Express folded into Continental Airlines.

The INFORMS Edelman Prize committee credited American's yield management systems with **$1.4 billion in incremental revenue over three years**.

## The Honest Caveat

**"Yield management killed People Express" is a strong simplification, and an episode that repeats it uncritically is doing PR for the winner.**

Business historians attribute the collapse to several causes acting together: rapid overexpansion, the debt-heavy 1985 acquisition of Frontier, deteriorating service quality and reliability as growth outran operations, and labour and management culture problems. Yield management was a serious competitive blow. It was not the sole cause.

Burr's quotes are real and citable. His causal claim is a participant's account, not a finding.

**What survives the caveat** is the part that matters: American solved an allocation problem with computing that its competitor could not solve at all, and that capability gap was real, decisive at the margin, and unavailable for purchase.
