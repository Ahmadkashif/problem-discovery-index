# The Fight: Deregulation Manufactures a Competitor

**Origin:** [[origins/telecom-carriers/profile|Telecom Carriers]]
**Outcome:** No single duel. A monopoly was legislated into a multi-carrier market, and churn modelling is the industry's answer to the market that resulted.

## Why This Fight Looks Different

The airlines origin has American Airlines against People Express, a named winner and a dated collapse. Telecom offers no equivalent single contest, and an honest file says so rather than manufacturing one. **The fight here is structural, not personal: regulation created competition where none had existed, and the industry had to learn, all at once, how to keep a customer who now had somewhere else to go.**

## The Opening

Until 1984 there was effectively one long-distance carrier and one local carrier in most of the country, both usually AT&T or a Bell affiliate. The Modified Final Judgment that took effect **January 1 1984** split local service into seven regional Baby Bells and left AT&T to compete in long distance against **MCI** and **Sprint** — companies that had spent the 1970s fighting in court for the right to interconnect with AT&T's network at all.

Long-distance carriers now competed for the same customer on price, and **presubscription** — choosing a default long-distance carrier — made switching a paperwork exercise rather than a physical one.

Cellular did the same thing from a different direction. The FCC's licensing structure guaranteed **two** carriers per market from the start — a wireline incumbent and a non-wireline challenger — when AMPS launched commercially in October 1983, and by the 1990s PCS entrants added a third, fourth and fifth competitor in many metro markets. A wireless subscriber choosing between five carriers offering broadly similar coverage had almost nothing but price, service, and retention offers to decide on.

## The Weapon Nobody Had Yet

Every carrier in this fragmented market held the same asset: a billing system recording exactly how each subscriber used the network. What none of them had, in the industry's first competitive decade, was a way to turn that record into a prediction of which subscribers were about to leave.

The Mozer team's work — detailed in [[origins/telecom-carriers/the-mechanism|The Mechanism]] — is best read as the industry's answer to that gap: a demonstration that the billing and complaint records a carrier already possessed contained a usable signal, and that acting on it early was worth tens of millions of dollars a year to a single mid-sized carrier.

## The Honest Caveat

**There is no named corporate casualty here**, and an episode should not invent one. Carriers did not go bankrupt for failing to predict churn the way People Express went bankrupt for failing to match yield management. The cost of missing this fight was diffuse — a slow bleed of subscribers and margin across an entire industry, rather than a single dramatic collapse.

What is real and citable is the **shape** of the fight: an industry moved in under two decades from having no concept of subscriber churn as a manageable number to treating it as the primary determinant of profitability, because regulation manufactured a competitor for every carrier's customers where none had existed before.
