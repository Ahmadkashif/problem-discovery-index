# The Fight: In-House Versus Outsourced

**Origin:** [[origins/pharma-rd-cros/profile|Pharma R&D and CROs]]
**Outcome:** Outsourcing won, gradually and without a single decisive battle — which is itself the point.

## Why This Fight Doesn't Look Like the Others

Most origin files in this vault describe a contest with a winner and a named loser — American Airlines against People Express, Bank of America's scale against smaller rivals. Pharma R&D's founding contest has no equivalent single dramatic episode. It was a **slow, structural reallocation of trial operations from sponsor payroll to a standing third-party industry**, decided company by company over roughly a decade, and it is more honestly told that way than forced into a duel it was not.

## What Sponsors Actually Faced

A pharmaceutical company running clinical trials needs statisticians, monitors, regulatory-affairs specialists and data managers — but only in the volume its current trial pipeline demands, and that pipeline is inherently **episodic**: a company might run three large trials one year and none the next, depending on which compounds are in which phase. Staffing permanently for peak trial volume means paying for idle specialists most of the time. Staffing for average volume means scrambling for contractors whenever a trial phase opens.

Through the **late 1970s and early 1980s**, sponsors increasingly concluded that permanent in-house trial-operations staff was the wrong answer to episodic demand, and a standing third-party industry — the CRO — formed to absorb that variability across many sponsors at once.

## The Companies That Formalised It

**Quintiles** was founded in **1982** by **Dennis Gillings**, a biostatistician who had been formalising statistical consulting for pharmaceutical trials since the mid-1970s. It grew to become the largest CRO in the industry (later merging into IQVIA). **Covance** was spun off from **Corning in 1997** as an independent contract-research business. **PPD**, through a **1997 acquisition**, became the first CRO to offer outsourced **drug discovery** work — not merely trial execution — extending the outsourcing logic upstream from running trials into finding the compounds that would enter them.

## What the CRO Actually Sold

Not primarily cheaper labour. The CRO sold **elasticity**: the ability for a sponsor to scale trial-operations capacity up and down with its actual pipeline, plus (increasingly, post-Part 11) a standing, auditable infrastructure for exactly the electronic-records validation problem described in [[origins/pharma-rd-cros/origin-story|the origin story]]. A CRO that had already built Part 11-compliant systems and already trained its staff to defend them under FDA inspection was selling **regulatory readiness as a service**, which is a harder thing for an occasional in-house trial team to maintain at the same standard.

## The Honest Caveat

There is no single year, no single sponsor decision, and no single loser to name here — which is precisely why this file resists the dramatic framing every other origin's fight uses. **Treat any account that names a specific "CRO industry was born on date X" moment with suspicion**; the formation was gradual and multi-company, running roughly from the mid-1970s (Gillings' statistical consulting) through the 1997 spin-offs and acquisitions (Covance, PPD) that gave the model its now-familiar large-player shape.
