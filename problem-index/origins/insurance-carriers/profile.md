# Insurance Carriers

**Layer:** Origin — a parent industry, not a prospect
**Primary Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-01-mainframe-batch|1 — Mainframe & Batch]]
**Founding event:** IBM punched-card tabulators in large-scale insurance use by the 1930s; general-purpose computers for actuarial and premium-billing work from 1956; Insurance Services Office formed 1971
**Children in this vault:** independent insurance agents, insurtech platforms, insurance TPAs, public adjusters, insurance restoration

## Profile

**What it is:** Underwriting and pricing risk, pooling premiums against future claims, and paying the claims that materialise. The product is a promise, priced today, against an event that may or may not happen decades from now.

**Who pays:** Policyholders pay premiums calibrated to a risk class they are placed into by statistical methods most of them never see. The pricing input is frequently not their own history — it is the pooled history of everyone like them, computed by an infrastructure layer that sits above any single insurer.

**The economics:** The entire business is a bet on a probability distribution. Get the loss estimate wrong in either direction and the company either prices itself out of the market or prices itself into insolvency. That single fact is why insurers touched large-scale computation earlier than almost anyone else in this vault.

## Why This Is an Origin — and a Myth to Kill First

**"Insurers digitised late" is backwards, and an episode should say so directly.** Metropolitan Life and Prudential ran large IBM punched-card tabulating installations by the 1930s; IBM controlled roughly 90% of all installed punched-card equipment in the US by the start of the 1950s, and insurers were among its largest customers. Insurers moved to general-purpose computers for premium billing and actuarial work in the mid-1950s — the same years airlines and banks were solving the same problem for their own records. Insurance was not a laggard adopting computing decades after everyone else. It was in the first cohort.

What makes insurance distinct as an origin is not *when* it computerised but *what it computerised first*: not a transaction record, but a **shared statistical estimate** — the loss cost — that no single company could produce alone and that competing companies nonetheless had to agree to share, under a specific act of Congress that let them.

## The Contested Decision

> **How much of the pricing calculation should insurers compute together, and how much should each insurer compute — and compete — alone?**

The answer moved twice in this vault's record: toward "together" in 1971 with the formation of ISO, and back toward "alone" in 1989 when ISO stopped publishing rates and started publishing loss costs only. See [[origins/insurance-carriers/the-fight|The Fight]].
