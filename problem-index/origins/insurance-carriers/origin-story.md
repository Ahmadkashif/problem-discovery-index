# Origin Story: From Tabulators to Computers

**Origin:** [[origins/insurance-carriers/profile|Insurance Carriers]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-01-mainframe-batch|1 — Mainframe & Batch]]

## The Myth This File Kills

There is a persistent, comfortable story in which insurance is a slow-moving, paper-bound industry that finally got dragged onto computers sometime around the 1990s. **It is not true, and the record on this is unusually clean.**

Metropolitan Life and Prudential — among the largest financial institutions in the country — had acquired large installations of IBM punched-card tabulating equipment by the **1930s**, running renewal accounting and commission calculation at a scale few other industries attempted that early. By the start of the 1950s, **IBM controlled roughly 90% of all installed punched-card equipment in the United States**, and insurers were among its heaviest users.

## What Was True Before Computers, Specifically

The tabulator solved arithmetic at volume. It did not solve *judgment* at volume. An actuary could tabulate the claims experience of a large pool of similar risks, but translating that pool's experience into a fair premium for one applicant, whose own history was too thin to trust on its own, was still a manual, judgment-heavy exercise, repeated policy by policy.

## The Trigger

In **1950**, Remington Rand hosted a forum specifically on the use of electronics in the insurance industry, with UNIVAC representatives presenting to insurance executives directly — a sign the industry was already courting general-purpose computing before most other sectors had a use case for it. A Society of Actuaries committee, with Metropolitan Life representatives prominent among its members, spent the early 1950s educating the wider life insurance industry on what computers could do for it.

The industry acted on that education quickly. In **1956**, Metropolitan Life expanded its computer use specifically to handle **premium billing** at scale — moving a core, high-volume administrative function off tabulators and onto general-purpose machines in the same mid-1950s window that produced ERMA for Bank of America and SABRE's development for American Airlines.

## Why This Belongs Beside Airlines and Banking, Not After Them

Three institutions solved three different problems with the same class of machine in the same decade: banks solved cheque volume, airlines solved seat inventory, insurers solved actuarial calculation and premium administration at population scale. **None of them was ahead of or behind the others in any meaningful sense — they were parallel, contemporaneous responses to the same underlying fact, that a business could suddenly compute over its whole customer file instead of a sample of it.**

What insurance did with that new capability, however, took a distinctive path: rather than each company hoarding its own computed advantage, the industry built a *shared* statistical infrastructure — the rating bureaus that became the Insurance Services Office in 1971 — because no single insurer, however good its tabulators, had enough claims experience on its own to price rare or unusual risks reliably. That decision, and the antitrust question it raised, is [[origins/insurance-carriers/the-fight|the fight]].
