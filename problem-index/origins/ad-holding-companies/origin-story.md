# Origin Story: The 15%, and Its Long, Uneven Death

**Origin:** [[origins/ad-holding-companies/profile|Ad Holding Companies]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-09-programmatic|9 — Programmatic]]

## What Was True Before

The advertising agency's fee was, for the first hundred years of the modern industry, not a fee the advertiser paid. It was a **commission the media owner paid the agency**, calculated as a fixed 15% of the price of the space or airtime the agency placed. The practice traces to **N.W. Ayer**-era conventions in the **1860s–70s** and was **codified as a settled industry standard by the 1920s**.

The arrangement is worth sitting with because of what it structurally implies: the agency's income rose with the price of the media it bought *for* the advertiser, and fell if it negotiated that price down. **The party responsible for getting the advertiser a good price was paid more when the price was higher.** For a hundred years this was simply how the industry worked, and it was rarely described in those terms by the people who benefited from it.

## The Legal Scaffolding, and Its Removal

A **1956 DOJ consent decree** forced media owners to sell advertising space to *any* buyer — including the advertiser directly — at "net" rates, removing the legal requirement that guaranteed agencies the 15% cut. In principle this should have ended the commission model immediately: any advertiser could now buy direct and pocket the difference.

**In practice the 15% norm persisted for another quarter-century.** It was not until the **1980s** that procurement-driven fee-for-service arrangements and hourly billing began meaningfully displacing straight commission — driven by advertisers' own procurement departments demanding itemised, auditable costs rather than an opaque percentage.

## Why There Is No Clean End Date

**The 15% commission did not die on a specific date, and any account that names one should be treated with suspicion.** It eroded unevenly across the 1980s and 1990s as fee-for-service spread, but it was never fully replaced — and, as [[origins/ad-holding-companies/the-mechanism|The Mechanism]] documents, the underlying economics it represented would resurface decades later inside programmatic media buying, in a form considerably harder for an advertiser to detect than a flat, disclosed percentage ever was.

## What Replaced It, Nominally

Fee-for-service billing, hourly rates, and — much later — performance-based compensation tied to campaign outcomes. All of these are more transparent *in form* than the commission model. What the vault's research found is that transparency in form and transparency in practice are not the same thing, and the gap between them is precisely what [[origins/ad-holding-companies/the-fight|the 2016 ANA/K2 report]] went looking for and found.

Ayer and the establishment of the 15% commission norm; 1956 DOJ consent decree affecting media-rate practices (widely cited in advertising-industry economic histories); trade coverage of the 1980s shift toward fee-for-service and procurement-driven agency compensation.
