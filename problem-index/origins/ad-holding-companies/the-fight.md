# The Fight: The ANA/K2 Transparency Report

**Origin:** [[origins/ad-holding-companies/profile|Ad Holding Companies]]
**Outcome:** The report did not end the practice it found. It made the practice impossible to plausibly deny.

## The Investigation

On **7 June 2016**, the Association of National Advertisers (ANA) — the industry body representing advertiser-side clients, not agencies — published **An Independent Study of Media Transparency in the U.S. Advertising Industry**, a 58-page report produced with the investigative firm **K2 Intelligence**. Fieldwork ran from **October 2015 to May 2016** and drew on **150 sources** across the media-buying supply chain.

Its central finding: **non-transparent rebate practices were "pervasive" in US media buying.** Media owners were paying agencies cash rebates — calculated as a percentage of the total media spend the agency placed with them — that were not disclosed to the advertiser whose budget generated the spend in the first place. The report measured these rebates ranging from **1.67% to 20% of aggregate spend**, a range wide enough to make clear this was not a marginal or occasional practice.

## Who Knew What

The report's most consequential finding was not the rebate figure itself — advertisers had long suspected something like it existed — but the specificity of who was involved: **contracts governing these rebates were negotiated and signed by senior agency executives**, not discovered as a rogue local practice. And in the large majority of cases, **advertisers were either unaware the rebates existed or were not passed any share of them**, despite the media spend being the advertiser's own budget.

The report characterised this as a **"fundamental disconnect"**: advertisers structurally assumed their agency owed them a **fiduciary duty** — the obligation of an agent to act in the principal's best interest, the standard a lawyer or financial adviser is held to. Agencies, per the report's account of their own contractual position, treated the relationship as **purely contractual** — bound only to the specific services enumerated in the agreement, with no broader duty to disclose financial arrangements with third parties that were not explicitly covered.

**That gap — fiduciary expectation versus contractual reality — is the actual finding.** The rebate percentages are the evidence; the disconnect in understood obligation is the mechanism.

## What Changed, and What Didn't

The report is widely credited with **accelerating two real, measurable trends**: advertiser **in-housing** of media functions, and the spread of explicit **fee-transparency clauses** in agency contracts. Both are documented independently of the report itself (see [[origins/ad-holding-companies/legacy|Legacy]] for the in-housing figures), which supports treating the report as an accelerant of pre-existing pressure rather than the sole cause of everything that followed.

What the report did **not** do is end rebate-based compensation. It made the practice legible enough to negotiate against — advertisers who read it could write contracts that explicitly forbade or required disclosure of rebates — but it did not, by itself, remove the structural incentive for an agency to prefer a media owner who pays it a rebate over one that does not.

## The Honest Caveat

**"The 2016 report ended non-transparent agency compensation" is not supported and should not be asserted.** What is supported: it is the single most detailed, most sourced, most widely cited documentation of the practice, it prompted contractual change at many advertisers, and it coincided with an acceleration in in-housing that removes the agency intermediary from the equation entirely in some cases. Cause and coincidence are not the same thing, and the report itself does not claim to have single-handedly fixed the market it examined.

**Sources:** Association of National Advertisers (ANA) and K2 Intelligence, *An Independent Study of Media Transparency in the U.S. Advertising Industry*, published 7 June 2016 (58 pages; fieldwork October 2015–May 2016; approximately 150 sources); trade press coverage (Ad Age, AdExchanger and similar) of the report's publication and industry response.
