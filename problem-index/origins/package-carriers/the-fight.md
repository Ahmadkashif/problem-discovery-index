# The Fight: FedEx Creates a Market, UPS Refuses to Lose It

**Origin:** [[origins/package-carriers/profile|Package Carriers]]
**Outcome:** Unlike this vault's airlines origin, nobody died. FedEx and UPS remain a duopoly today — a different, and in some ways more instructive, ending than American Airlines versus People Express.

## The Opening

Guaranteed overnight delivery, when FedEx launched it in 1973, was not a service the existing package and mail carriers offered, in part because none of them had built a network shaped to guarantee it. FedEx did not enter an existing market and win share — **it built the market**, on the hub-and-spoke topology and, from 1979, on COSMOS's tracking.

## UPS's Response

United Parcel Service was not a startup facing this threat — it had been moving packages, largely by truck and largely under a unionised workforce, since **1907**, and it was not going to concede the fastest-growing, highest-margin segment of its own industry without a fight it could actually afford. UPS built its own airline, **UPS Airlines, in 1988**, and its own central hub — **Worldport, in Louisville** — to answer FedEx's Memphis directly. It brought the same asset the incumbent always has in this vault's origins: an enormous existing ground network, which it now paired with the air-and-hub model FedEx had proven.

## The Second Act, Decades Later

The fight did not end in 1988; it moved to a different terrain, and this vault's mechanism file for this origin covers it in detail. From **2003**, UPS's own operations-research group began a decade-long research programme — **ORION** — to optimise not which cities to fly between, but which order a single driver should visit their own stops in, across roughly 55,000 US routes. It reached full-scale rollout starting **October 2013** and was substantially complete by **2016–17**, winning the **2016 INFORMS Franz Edelman Award**. FedEx runs comparable route and network optimisation internally; ORION is simply the version that became public through the Edelman process, and its publicity should not be mistaken for FedEx lacking an equivalent.

## The Honest Caveat

**This is not American Airlines versus People Express, and treating it as a symmetrical story is a mistake.** Nobody in this fight went bankrupt. UPS is not a challenger that got crushed by an incumbent's superior computing, nor is FedEx a scrappy insurgent that toppled a giant — UPS *is* the giant, dating to 1907, and it had the balance sheet to buy its way into the same network topology FedEx had proven, rather than being structurally incapable of matching it the way People Express's simpler systems genuinely could not do yield management at all.

**What this fight actually demonstrates is a different lesson from the airlines one:** when the competitive advantage is a *network design* rather than a proprietary algorithm, a well-capitalised incumbent can replicate it by building the same shape of infrastructure, and the market settles into a duopoly instead of producing a corpse. Compare this to [[origins/airlines/the-fight|American Airlines versus People Express]], where the loser's systems could not have replicated the winner's approach at any price, because the constraint was capability, not capital.
