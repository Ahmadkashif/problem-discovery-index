# Package Carriers

**Layer:** Origin — a parent industry, not a prospect
**Primary Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-02-departmental-item-level|2 — Departmental & Item-Level]] → [[series/eras/wave-08-mobile-gps|8 — Mobile & GPS]]
**Founding event:** Federal Express founded 5 May 1971, operations began 17 April 1973 from a purpose-built Memphis hub; COSMOS tracking system live 1979; UPS ORION prototyped 2008–2011, rolled out from 2013, complete by ~2016–17
**Children in this vault:** last-mile-delivery, freight-brokerage, warehouse-3pl, owner-operator-trucking, ecommerce-sellers

## Profile

**What it is:** the scheduled physical movement of individually tracked parcels between named senders and receivers, at a guaranteed time, for a price set in advance.

**Who pays:** shippers, on a rate card that has grown more granular every decade — by weight, zone, speed, and now, increasingly, by the driver-hours and fuel a given stop actually costs to serve.

**The economics:** a package is not perishable in the way an airline seat is, but its route is: once a truck leaves the building, that day's sequence of stops is fixed, and every mile driven that didn't have to be is pure cost with no offsetting revenue. Package carriers scaled by attacking the two costs that dominate the business — the cost of knowing where a package is, and the cost of choosing the order to visit addresses in.

## Why This Is an Origin

Federal Express invented a *network topology* — hub-and-spoke — before it invented a tracking system, and the network is the less obvious achievement: routing every package through one central sort seems, on paper, like adding a detour. It multiplies the number of viable direct connections instead. **Every parcel network in this vault, and most of retail delivery logistics, still uses this shape.**

Decades later, this origin produced one of the best-documented applications of large-scale route optimisation in commercial history — and one of the most persistently mis-told, because a much older, much simpler heuristic gets credited with the newer system's results.

## The Contested Decision

> **Do you route each package the shortest way between its actual origin and destination, or force every package through one sorting point, every night, no exceptions?**

The second answer is worse for any single package and better for the network as a whole — the founding bet of this entire origin, and the reason a package from Miami to New Orleans has, for fifty years, quite possibly flown through Tennessee.
