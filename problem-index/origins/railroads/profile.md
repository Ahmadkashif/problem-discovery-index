# Railroads

**Layer:** Origin — a parent industry, not a prospect
**Primary Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-02-departmental-item-level|2 — Departmental & Item-Level]]
**Secondary Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-04-client-server-erp|4 — Client–Server & ERP]]
**Founding event:** Southern Pacific/IBM feasibility study, June 1960, developing into TOPS through the 1960s; British Railways adopted TOPS from August 1973
**Children in this vault:** freight brokerage, warehouse & 3PL, cold chain logistics, owner-operator trucking

## Profile

**What it is:** Common-carrier freight transport over a shared, fixed, capital-intensive network — track, yards, locomotives, crews — scheduled and re-scheduled continuously as tens of thousands of shipments with different origins, destinations and priorities move across it at once.

**Who pays:** Shippers, by the car or container, on contracts from single spot moves to multi-year commitments. The railroad owns the fixed asset every shipper's freight depends on and cannot substitute away from over the medium term.

**The economics:** Extraordinarily high fixed cost (track, yards, rolling stock) against comparatively low marginal cost per additional carload — closer to airlines' perishable-capacity problem than to retail banking's, except a freight car that misses its optimal path is not lost revenue, it is a car sitting in the wrong yard costing money every day it sits there.

## Why This Is an Origin

Railroads carry the **oldest continuously unsolved large-scale scheduling problem** in this series. Car-blocking, yard classification, crew scheduling and locomotive assignment across a shared, congested, priority-conflicted network is a stochastic integer scheduling and routing problem that remains, honestly, only partially solved — [[origins/electric-utilities/profile|electric utilities']] optimisation runs are mature and stable by comparison.

Railroads also teach a distinction FDEs routinely blur: **the difference between computerising visibility into an asset and actually optimising the network that asset moves through.** Railroads did the first in the 1960s and 70s. The second remains, in real and important ways, a management discipline rather than a solved algorithm.

## The Contested Decision

> **Do you run the network as a hub-and-spoke system — accumulate cars in a yard until there is enough volume to justify a train, however long that takes — or as a fixed, published schedule that runs whether the yard is full or not, the way passenger rail always has?**

That decision, made explicitly inside one company starting in 1993, reshaped the operating economics of North American freight rail, and the argument over what it cost is still active.
