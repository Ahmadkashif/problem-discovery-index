# The Fight: Precision Scheduled Railroading

**Origin:** [[origins/railroads/profile|Railroads]]
**Outcome:** Contested. Record profitability for the railroads that adopted it; a live, unresolved argument over what that profitability cost.

## The Model It Replaced

For most of the twentieth century, North American freight railroads ran on a **hub-and-spoke, wait-until-the-yard-is-full** model: cars accumulated at a classification yard until there was enough volume to justify assembling and running a train. This maximised each train's utilisation. It also meant a car's transit time depended on how quickly its yard filled up, which nobody controlled precisely.

## The Contest Over Who Gets Credit

**E. Hunter Harrison** joined Illinois Central in 1989 and became its CEO in **1993** — the year usually cited as **Precision Scheduled Railroading's** formal debut. Harrison traced the thinking further back, to ideas developed in the early 1980s at Burlington Northern.

**Flagged honestly:** the origin is disputed even among people close to it. Some credit Harrison's predecessor at Illinois Central, **Ed Moyers**, and the operating plan Moyers ran at the smaller **MidSouth** railroad, as an uncredited precursor. There is no clean, undisputed single-inventor story here, and an episode assigning sole credit to Harrison repeats an oversimplification the record does not fully support.

## What PSR Actually Changed

The core move: replace "accumulate until full" with **fixed, published point-to-point train schedules** — run the train on the timetable, the way passenger rail always has. That discipline cascaded into consolidating classification yards, cutting the locomotive and car fleet needed to move the same freight, and reducing headcount.

Harrison exported the model as he moved: **Canadian National (2003)**, **Canadian Pacific** (CEO from 2012), and finally **CSX (2017)** — he died on **16 December 2017**, shortly after arriving. At every stop, the immediate result was the same: **operating ratios improved sharply and profitability records followed**, which is why the model spread across the Class I railroads even as Harrison himself moved on or passed away.

## What It Is Now Blamed For

PSR is now widely — and this is contested territory — blamed for **reduced crew levels, longer and heavier trains, and deferred maintenance**, from labour unions to congressional testimony to NPR and academic transportation-policy sources. The critique is most visibly attached to the **3 February 2023 Norfolk Southern derailment in East Palestine, Ohio**, where a defective wheel bearing overheated and caused a derailment that released hazardous materials.

**Do not resolve this.** PSR's financial results are not in dispute. Whether that improvement came at an acceptable safety and service cost is an active, unresolved public argument, and East Palestine is cited as evidence by critics rather than established by an investigation as a *direct, sole consequence* of PSR policy at Norfolk Southern.

## What Survives

A scheduling discipline enforced by management will beat an unenforced optimum, because the hub-and-spoke model was never actually optimal, it was merely unmanaged. And the same discipline that removes slack from a system removes the slack that used to absorb its failures — fewer spare locomotives, fewer spare crews, less schedule buffer, all of which were also, unglamorously, safety margin.
