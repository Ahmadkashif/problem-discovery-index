# Origin Story: Instinet Before NASDAQ

**Origin:** [[origins/exchanges-market-makers/profile|Exchanges & Market Makers]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-01-mainframe-batch|1 — Mainframe & Batch]]

## The Myth This File Kills, First and Loudest

**"NASDAQ was the first electronic stock exchange" is the single most repeated wrong fact in this industry's popular history, and it is wrong on both halves of the claim.**

NASDAQ was not first, and for its first thirty-five years it was not, legally, a stock exchange at all.

## What Came First

**Instinet** — Institutional Networks Corp — went live in **December 1969**, founded by Jerome Pustilnik and Herbert Behrens. It let institutional investors — banks, mutual funds, insurers — trade directly with each other over a computer network, without going through an exchange floor or a dealer. That makes Instinet the first **electronic communication network (ECN)**, a category name it effectively created, and it predates NASDAQ by more than a year.

## What NASDAQ Actually Was

**NASDAQ launched 8 February 1971**, built by the National Association of Securities Dealers with technology from Bunker-Ramo. It is almost always described as an electronic stock exchange from that date. **It was not one.**

What NASDAQ actually was, in 1971, was a **quotation system**: a network of dumb terminals broadcasting the bid and ask prices that over-the-counter market makers were willing to trade at. Seeing a quote on a NASDAQ terminal told a broker what a dealer claimed to be willing to do. **Executing the trade still meant picking up the telephone and calling that dealer.** The matching and execution — the part that makes something an exchange in the legal and functional sense — happened by voice, exactly as it always had.

NASDAQ did not become a **registered national securities exchange** under the Securities Exchange Act until the SEC approved its application on **13 January 2006**, with NASDAQ beginning to operate as a registered exchange in its listed securities on **1 August 2006**. For thirty-five years, the network everyone called "the first electronic exchange" was, in the SEC's own regulatory language, something else.

## Why the Distinction Is Not Pedantry

The gap between "everyone can see the price" and "anyone can act on the price without calling a human" is the entire subject of this origin. Instinet solved execution — actual electronic trading between counterparties — from 1969. NASDAQ solved *visibility* of price from 1971, and left execution as a manual, dealer-mediated bottleneck for over three decades.

That bottleneck was not neutral. A dealer who quoted a price and then had to be telephoned to trade on it retained the ability to hesitate, requote, or simply not answer — a form of friction that, as [[origins/exchanges-market-makers/the-fight|the fight]] shows, protected dealer spreads for years after the technology to remove that friction already existed.
