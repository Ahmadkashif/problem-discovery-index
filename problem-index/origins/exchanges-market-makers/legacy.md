# Legacy: What Exchanges & Market Makers Bequeathed

**Origin:** [[origins/exchanges-market-makers/profile|Exchanges & Market Makers]]

## The Direct Inheritance

| Child | What it inherited |
|---|---|
| [[industries/crypto-exchanges|Crypto Exchanges]] | The exact same fight, replayed with none of the sixty years of regulatory scar tissue already built in. A crypto exchange choosing whether to run a continuous order book, how to define price-time priority, and whether to sell faster market data access to some participants than others is re-litigating decisions equities markets settled — sometimes badly — between 1971 and 2007, largely without the equivalent of a Christie-and-Schultz moment forcing early correction. |
| [[industries/robo-advisors|Robo-Advisors]] | A cost structure that only exists because the spread-compression war was won. Sub-basis-point trading costs, one-cent tick sizes and near-instant electronic execution are the precondition for a product that can profitably manage a small account for a low flat fee — none of that was available to a retail investor before decimalization (2001) and the ECN-driven collapse of dealer spreads that preceded it. |
| [[industries/wealth-management-rias|Wealth Management RIAs]] | The consequence of the same cost collapse, at the human-advice layer. Once execution and access were commoditised to near-zero cost, the fee an advisor could justify charging shrank to whatever was left over for judgement and planning rather than for moving money — a direct, traceable value migration away from the exact function this origin's founders were fighting each other over. |

## The Pattern That Repeats Underneath All Three

The nineteen-seventies-to-2007 fight in this origin was fundamentally about **who gets to see and act on a price first, and how much of that advantage the rules will let them keep.** Every child industry above is a business built entirely downstream of how that fight resolved: crypto exchanges are fighting it again from scratch, robo-advisors and RIAs are pricing their entire business models against the fact that it mostly resolved in the investor's favour.

**That is worth stating plainly, because it cuts against a comfortable narrative.** It would be easy to tell this origin's story as "technology made markets fairer and cheaper," full stop. The more accurate version, documented across [[origins/exchanges-market-makers/the-fight|the fight]], is that markets got fairer and cheaper **because investors, regulators and litigation forced dealers to give up rents they were extracting through avoidable friction** — the odd-eighth quoting, the phone call a customer had to make to act on a quote they could already see. The technology made the correction possible. It did not make the correction automatic, and it did not make it welcome to the incumbents losing the rent.

## What an Episode Should Take From This

1. **"The market got more efficient" is true and is also doing a lot of quiet work to hide who fought to make it true, and who they beat.** A billion-dollar antitrust settlement sits behind the tightened spreads that every downstream fintech now treats as a fact of nature.
2. **A regulation aimed at protecting investors from one specific harm (trade-throughs) can create an entirely unintended commercial arms race (latency) as a side effect, without mandating any part of that race directly.** See [[origins/exchanges-market-makers/the-mechanism|the mechanism]] for how completely a $300M physical asset can be obsoleted once that race is on.
3. **A new venue (crypto exchanges) inheriting none of an incumbent's regulatory history does not inherit its lessons either.** It has to relearn them, in public, usually at some cost to its own users — and an FDE evaluating a crypto-exchange opportunity should ask which of this origin's sixty-year-old lessons the venue in front of them has actually absorbed.
