# Legacy: What Online Travel Agencies Bequeathed

**Origin:** [[origins/online-travel-agencies/profile|Online Travel Agencies]]

## The Direct Inheritance

| Child | What it inherited |
|---|---|
| [[industries/short-term-rentals\|Short-Term Rentals]] | The OTA distribution-and-commission model, applied to a supply side (individual hosts) with none of a hotel chain's negotiating leverage. Airbnb and Vrbo occupy the exact structural position Expedia occupied over independent hotels twenty years earlier. |
| [[industries/hotels-boutique\|Boutique Hotels]] | The "OTA tax" — commission running 15–25% of a booking — as the modern equivalent of the travel-agent commission the industry spent the 1990s trying to eliminate. | [[industries/online-marketplaces\|Online Marketplaces]] | The general template: aggregate fragmented supply, own the customer relationship and the search box, and extract a toll regardless of who wins any given transaction. Every two-sided marketplace that displaced a category's traditional middleman is running the OTA playbook, usually without knowing its lineage traces here. |

## The Duopoly That Resulted

By the 2010s the sector consolidated hard. **Booking Holdings** (Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, Kayak — acquired 2012–13 — and OpenTable) and **Expedia Group** (Expedia, Hotels.com, Vrbo, Orbitz, Travelocity, plus a majority stake in Trivago) became the two firms that mattered. Booking Holdings won decisively on profitability and market capitalisation, substantially on the strength of Booking.com's high-margin hotel-commission model described in [[origins/online-travel-agencies/origin-story|the origin story]] — a reminder that within a consolidating platform layer, the specific unit economics of the acquired asset can matter more than the acquirer's original brand recognition.

## The Re-Intermediation Pattern

The central lesson of [[origins/online-travel-agencies/the-fight|The Fight]] is not specific to travel. **An incumbent that removes one intermediary to reach its customer directly frequently creates the conditions for a new, better-capitalised intermediary to occupy the same position** — because the thing that made the old intermediary valuable (aggregating comparison and reducing the customer's search cost) does not stop being valuable just because the seller wanted to skip it.

This is the same shape as the screen-bias problem [[origins/airlines/legacy|Airlines bequeathed]] one layer down, and it recurs in this vault wherever a platform sits between a supplier and a customer: retail media networks charging suppliers to be found inside a retailer's own marketplace, app stores charging developers to be found on a phone the developer does not control, programmatic ad exchanges charging publishers and advertisers alike to find each other (see [[origins/ad-holding-companies/profile|Ad Holding Companies]]).

## What an Episode Should Take From This

1. **The GDS was infrastructure; the OTA was a business built to exploit it.** Twenty-five years separated Sabre's nationwide rollout from Expedia's founding — a smaller gap than the twenty-five years between Sabre and yield management, but the same shape: infrastructure sits idle until someone builds the layer that monetises it.
2. **Disintermediation is a direction, not a destination.** Every layer removed clears space for a new layer, and the new layer usually has more data and more customer reach than the one it replaced.
3. **The acquisition that mattered was the unglamorous one.** Priceline buying a modest Dutch site in 2005 outperformed every flashier launch in this file's own history. Judge unit economics, not headlines.
