# The Fight: Disintermediation That Inverted Itself

**Origin:** [[origins/online-travel-agencies/profile|Online Travel Agencies]]
**Outcome:** Airlines won the fight they picked. They lost the war they didn't know they were starting.

## The Opening Move, Dated Precisely

Airlines wanted travel agents gone, or at least cheaper. The first documented move was **Delta Air Lines cutting base agent commissions from 10% to 5% on 9 February 1995**. Five more rounds of cuts followed across the industry over the following seven years. By **March 2002**, eight of the ten largest US airlines — every major carrier except Alaska and Southwest — had cut base commissions to **zero**.

The effect on the traditional travel agency was severe and measurable: ARC-accredited agency locations fell from roughly **36,000 at their 1995 peak to about 13,000**. This is, on its face, a clean disintermediation story — the internet let airlines sell direct, so they stopped paying a middleman to sell for them.

## The Part the Clean Story Leaves Out

**Airlines did not end up owning the customer relationship they were trying to reclaim. A new, more powerful intermediary stepped into the space they had just cleared.**

The old travel agent worked for a commission and had no leverage over the airline — it queried the GDS the airline's own consortium had built. The OTA that replaced it — Expedia, Travelocity, Orbitz — aggregated demand across every carrier simultaneously and gained the ability to compare and rank flights the airline did not control. Booking volume stayed high enough through this channel that GDSs found themselves **raising incentive payments to keep travel agents in business**, because the GDS made money on every segment booked through one, regardless of who cut the commission.

This is the same "screen-bias" pattern the vault's [[origins/airlines/legacy|Airlines legacy file]] identifies at Sabre's founding, replaying one layer up: whoever intermediates a market can advantage itself inside that market, and the incumbent's attempt to remove one intermediary created room for a new, harder-to-dislodge one.

**Airlines set out to disintermediate and ended up re-intermediated by a party with more market power than the one they fired.**

## The Honest Caveat

**"OTAs killed the travel agent" is an oversimplification, and some serious industry scholarship pushes back on it directly.** *Travel Weekly* and related trade coverage argue that full disintermediation never actually happened: independent and corporate travel agents did not vanish so much as **change their revenue model**, replacing supplier commission with **service fees paid directly by the traveller or the corporate account** — a business that persists today, particularly in complex corporate and luxury leisure travel where a human intermediary still adds value a search box does not.

The commission cuts are real, dated, and drove the collapse in ARC-accredited locations. The inference that the *function* of the travel agent disappeared is not supported to the same degree — it moved to a different payer.

## What an Episode Should Take From This

The transferable pattern is not "the internet killed the middleman." It is: **removing one intermediary's toll booth does not remove the toll booth — it just changes who is allowed to build the next one, and the next builder is frequently better positioned than the incumbent expected.** Retail media networks, app store commissions and programmatic ad exchanges (see [[origins/ad-holding-companies/profile|Ad Holding Companies]]) are later instances of the identical shape.

**Sources:** ASTA (American Society of Travel Advisors) and ARC (Airlines Reporting Corporation) historical commission and agency-location data as widely cited in travel-trade retrospectives; *Travel Weekly* commentary on agency revenue-model transition; Sabre and Amadeus corporate histories on GDS incentive structures.
