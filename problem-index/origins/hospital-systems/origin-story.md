# Origin Story: HITECH

**Origin:** [[origins/hospital-systems/profile|Hospital Systems]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-07-big-data|7 — Big Data]]

## What Was True Before

In 2008, **9% of US hospitals had even a basic electronic health record.** The other 91% ran on paper charts, faxed referrals and dictated notes transcribed by hand. A patient discharged from one hospital and admitted to another the same week generated two unconnected paper trails, and the second clinician frequently made decisions without the first clinician's data.

This was not a technology gap in the ordinary sense — spreadsheets and databases had existed for decades, and plenty of hospitals could afford them. It was an incentive gap. Digitising a hospital's clinical workflow is expensive, disruptive to the people doing the work, and captures most of its benefit for payers and patients rather than for the hospital that bears the cost. Nobody was going to solve that with a better product pitch.

## The Trigger

In February 2009, Congress attached the **HITECH Act** to the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act — the stimulus bill. HITECH did two things at once, which is unusual and is the whole mechanism: it paid hospitals and eligible professionals to adopt EHRs that met a federal certification standard, and it scheduled Medicare payment *penalties*, starting in 2015, for anyone who had not.

> **The dollar figure has no single authoritative number, and an episode should not invent one.** $19.2B, $27B, $30B and $35B all circulate in reporting, depending on whether the count is CMS EHR incentive payments alone, the whole HITECH title including ONC operations and regional extension centres, authorised appropriations versus actual disbursements, or some mix. What is solid: Medicaid EHR incentive payments alone totalled roughly **$12.6B by December 2017**, and individual eligible professionals could draw up to **$44,000 over five years through Medicare** or **$63,750 through Medicaid**, with hospital base payments scaling with discharge volume.

Adoption was staged as **"Meaningful Use"** — Stage 1 (structured data capture and sharing), Stage 2 (advanced clinical processes), Stage 3 (improved outcomes) — measured against **ONC-Certified EHR Technology**, a federal certification a vendor's product had to hold before its use counted toward the incentive at all.

## The Curve

The resulting adoption curve is the fastest sector-wide digitisation this vault records anywhere:

| Year | Basic EHR | Any certified EHR |
|---|---|---|
| 2008 | 9% | — |
| 2010 | 15% | — |
| 2011 | 27% | — |
| 2015 | 84% | 96% |

"Comprehensive" EHR — the deeper functional tier — moved from **1.6% of hospitals in 2008 to 40% in 2015.** No market force in this vault's other seventeen origins produced a comparable curve on a comparable timescale. Airlines took twenty-five years to go from SABRE to yield management. Hospitals went from 9% to 96% in seven.

## Why It Mattered, and Why It Was Incomplete

HITECH proved that a government can compress a multi-decade technology adoption curve into single digits of years by paying for the input and penalising its absence. It is the strongest evidence in this vault that adoption speed is very often a financing problem, not an engineering one.

What it could not do by the same mechanism was force the *deeper* capability — clinicians' systems actually exchanging patient data across organisational boundaries — because that was never what the incentive measured. See [[origins/hospital-systems/the-mechanism|the mechanism]] for what "meaningful use" counted instead.
