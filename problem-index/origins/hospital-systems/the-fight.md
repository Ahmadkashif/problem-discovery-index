# The Fight: Epic against Cerner, for the Money the Government Was About to Spend

**Origin:** [[origins/hospital-systems/profile|Hospital Systems]]
**Outcome:** Epic leads on share; Cerner was acquired by Oracle in 2022.

## The Set-Up

HITECH did not create a fight between hospitals. It created a fight between the vendors selling hospitals the thing the government was about to pay them to buy. From 2009 onward, every hospital in America had a deadline and a subsidy, and a handful of EHR vendors — Epic, Cerner, Allscripts, Meditech — competed to be the system that deadline got spent on.

This is a different shape of fight to the ones elsewhere in this vault. American Airlines beat People Express by solving a problem People Express could not attempt. Epic and Cerner were not competing on capability so much as on **who could get certified, installed and live across the largest hospital network fastest**, while a clock the government had set was running.

## The Two Strategies

**Epic**, privately held and controlled by its founder Judy Faulkner throughout, sold a single, tightly integrated, self-built platform and generally required buyers to adopt Epic's way of running a hospital along with the software. It never took venture money, never went public, and grew primarily through direct sales to large health systems rather than acquisition.

**Cerner** grew partly by acquisition and pursued a broader mix of inpatient, outpatient, population-health and government contracts (including large federal EHR modernisation programmes for the VA and DoD), competing on breadth and price where Epic competed on depth and control.

By **2020**, Epic held roughly **31% of US hospitals (1,713)** and Cerner roughly **25% (1,354)** — the two of them accounting for the majority of the market the HITECH incentive dollars had been spent chasing.

## The Resolution

Cerner did not lose to Epic in the sense of being outcompeted into irrelevance — it remained the number-two platform for over a decade. It lost in a different sense: **Oracle acquired Cerner for $28.3B, a deal that closed 8 June 2022**, and rebranded it Oracle Health, betting that cloud infrastructure and a large-model rebuild could do to healthcare records what neither Cerner nor Epic had done on their own. Epic remains independently held and has not been acquired or gone public.

## The Fight Nobody Won: Interoperability

Meaningful Use Stage 2 required hospitals to demonstrate health information exchange, and the honest record is that this requirement was satisfied far more thinly than the adoption numbers suggest. Vendors built certified products; certified products were not the same thing as products that shared data freely with a competitor's certified product. The practice of a vendor or provider making data exchange difficult or expensive enough to discourage it acquired its own name — **information blocking** — and Congress addressed it separately, in the **21st Century Cures Act (2016)**, seven years after HITECH.

**The honest reading of this fight is not "Epic won, Cerner lost."** It is that the government-funded race to get *a* certified EHR installed everywhere was run and won decisively, in seven years, and the race to make those systems talk to each other was a different contest that HITECH's incentive structure did not fund and did not settle.
