# The Fight: Scale Against Scale

**Origin:** [[origins/retail-banking/profile|Retail Banking]]

## The Contest

Banking has no product differentiation worth the name. A dollar on deposit is a dollar on deposit. The competitive ground is **cost per transaction and reach**, and computerisation moved both at once.

The fight was never bank-versus-bank over a clever feature. It was **large banks against the fixed cost of automation**, and the outcome was structural: the cost of a core banking system is roughly independent of how many accounts run on it, so every account added lowers the average. That is a consolidation engine, and it ran for forty years.

## Who Won

**The large banks, decisively — and then the technology vendors.**

The second half is the more interesting part. Most banks could not build an ERMA. What emerged instead was a vendor layer — core banking providers selling the same system to hundreds of institutions — and that layer ended up with more durable pricing power than most of its customers.

This vault's [[industries/credit-unions|Credit Unions]] notes document the surviving condition precisely: institutions whose competitive strategy is member relationship, running on core platforms they do not control, cannot easily leave, and cannot extend without vendor cooperation. The core provider is a more important determinant of what a credit union can offer than the credit union's own strategy.

**That is what losing a build-versus-buy decision looks like fifty years later.**

## The Second Fight: who touches the customer

Once the rails were electronic, a new contest opened over **who stands between the customer and their money** — and the incumbent's advantage turned out to be weaker than it looked.

The bank owned the account. It did not necessarily own the interface, the credit decision, the payment experience or the merchant relationship. Every one of those was eventually contested by someone who did not need a banking licence to compete:

- [[industries/payment-processors|Payment Processors]] took the merchant relationship.
- [[industries/neobanks|Neobanks]] took the interface, frequently renting the licence from a small partner bank.
- [[industries/bnpl-providers|BNPL Providers]] took the point-of-sale credit decision — the highest-margin lending moment — by being present at checkout.
- [[industries/lending-marketplaces|Lending Marketplaces]] took origination.

In every case the incumbent still holds the balance sheet and the regulatory burden, and has lost the part of the transaction the customer actually experiences.

## The Honest Caveat

It is tempting to narrate this as legacy banks being out-innovated. The more accurate account is that they were **out-positioned while carrying obligations their challengers did not have.** A neobank renting a charter is not beating a bank at banking; it is competing in the profitable segment of banking without the compliance surface, capital requirements or branch network that the charter-holder carries.

An FDE should notice this, because it recurs everywhere: *the incumbent's disadvantage is frequently its obligations, not its competence.*
