# Credit Bureaus

**Layer:** Origin — a parent industry, not a prospect
**Primary Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-01-mainframe-batch|1 — Mainframe & Batch]]
**Founding event:** Fair Credit Reporting Act enacted 26 Oct 1970, effective 25 April 1971; Fair, Isaac and Company founded 1956, first scorecards sold to lenders 1958; bureau-wide FICO Score launched with Equifax 1989, all three national bureaus by 1991
**Children in this vault:** lending-marketplaces, bnpl-providers, collections-agencies, mortgage-brokers, credit-unions, independent-insurance-agents

## Profile

**What it is:** the collection, storage and sale of an individual's payment history to lenders deciding whether to extend credit — a business that sells neither a product nor a service to the person its records are actually about.

**Who pays:** lenders, landlords, insurers and employers, who buy reports and scores; the consumer pays only indirectly, in the interest rate, the security deposit or the job offer the score determines, usually without seeing the number that decided it until they ask.

**The economics:** enormous fixed cost to build and maintain a national, disputable, continuously updated file on hundreds of millions of people, against near-zero marginal cost per report sold once that file exists. That cost structure is a consolidation engine, and this origin's history is substantially the story of that consolidation.

## Why This Is an Origin

Credit bureaus computerised the first population-scale prediction problem in this vault: not "will this specific transaction succeed" but **"will this specific person repay,"** answered the same way for hundreds of millions of strangers using the same handful of variables. Every underwriting model in this vault's fintech industries is downstream of the decision made here — that repayment risk should be a number, not a local reputation.

## The Contested Decision

> **Should a lender's decision rest on what a local agent believes about a person's character, or on a statistic computed from that person's own payment record — one that doesn't know their name?**

The industry answered "statistic," decisively, over about thirty years — and in doing so traded one set of failures (a subjective judgement that varied by whom you knew) for a different set (a number blind to context, silent about its own reasoning, and, per this vault's contested-facts discipline, still argued over as to whether it reduced discrimination or automated it).
