# The Fight: Two Thousand Bureaus Become Three

**Origin:** [[origins/credit-bureaus/profile|Credit Bureaus]]
**Outcome:** Total consolidation. Over 2,000 local bureaus became, by most accounts, roughly five regional networks and then three national ones — Equifax, Experian and TransUnion — a pattern this vault's retail-banking origin describes independently for core banking, arrived at here by a similar mechanism.

## The Ground the Fight Was Fought On

Nobody was competing on a better character judgement — that was precisely the product under legal and public attack. The competition was over who could afford to run a national, computerised, FCRA-compliant file operation, and that is a game with economies of scale so strong it does not stay fragmented for long.

**Equifax** traces to the **Retail Credit Company**, founded in Atlanta in **1899** by Cator and Guy Woolford — making it, by a wide margin, the oldest lineage of the three. Retail Credit was also, by the early 1970s, the specific target of the congressional and press scrutiny into character-file abuses described in this origin's origin story; it renamed itself **Equifax in 1976**, after that scrutiny, a fact the company's own histories tend to understate. **TransUnion** was created in **1968** as a holding company by the Union Tank Car Company — a railway-equipment leasing firm, not a credit business — which acquired the Credit Bureau of Cook County in 1969 and then assembled national coverage gradually through regional acquisitions, not reaching all-US coverage until **1988**, nearly two decades later. **Experian's** lineage runs through Credit Data Corporation, acquired by TRW Inc. in 1968 and renamed TRW Information Services; TRW sold the unit in **November 1996** to Bain Capital and Thomas H. Lee Partners, who took it to market as Experian.

## Why It Consolidated This Fast

The same structural fact as retail banking's core-banking systems: **the cost of running a compliant, computerised credit file operation is largely independent of how many consumers it covers**, so every bureau absorbed by a larger one lowered the average cost for the survivor. FCRA's compliance burden — audit trails, dispute resolution infrastructure, standardised formats — accelerated a consolidation that computerisation alone had already made likely. By the mid-1980s the roughly 2,000-bureau landscape had become, functionally, three.

## The Honest Caveat

**"Only three bureaus" reads, in hindsight, like an obviously bad outcome for consumers, and it is tempting to narrate the consolidation as the villain of this story.** That is too simple. The character-file system it replaced was arguably worse for the people it judged — unaccountable, undisclosed, and shaped by a local agent's prejudice rather than a documented, disputable record. **The honest version is that consolidation solved one problem (accountability, standardisation) while creating another (concentration risk)** — and the second problem did not fully reveal itself for another thirty years. See [[origins/credit-bureaus/legacy|Legacy]] for what happened when it did.
