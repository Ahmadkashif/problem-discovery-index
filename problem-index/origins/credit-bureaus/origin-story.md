# Origin Story: From Character Files to a Number

**Origin:** [[origins/credit-bureaus/profile|Credit Bureaus]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-01-mainframe-batch|1 — Mainframe & Batch]]

## What Was True Before

Through the 1960s, credit reporting in the United States was done by **more than 2,000 local credit bureaus**, most of them small, independent, and running on paper files. A file did not only record whether bills were paid. It routinely recorded a local agent's subjective assessment of the person — remarks on drinking habits, marital difficulties, "lifestyle," cleanliness of the home, sometimes sexual orientation — collected by neighbourhood canvassers and door-to-door investigators and passed to lenders, insurers and employers as fact. **This is not a retrospective exaggeration; it is what drove the law that ends this section.**

## The Statistical Alternative Arrives First, and Separately

**Fair, Isaac and Company was founded in 1956** in San Rafael, California, by engineer Bill Fair and mathematician Earl Isaac. They began selling statistical credit-scoring algorithms directly to individual lenders in **1958** — more than a decade before the character-file system above came under legal challenge. This is worth holding as its own fact: **statistical scoring existed as a bespoke, lender-by-lender tool for thirty years before it became the bureau-wide standard product it is today.** It did not arrive to fix the character-file problem; it existed alongside it, largely unconnected, until regulation forced them together.

## The Law That Forced the Reckoning

The **Fair Credit Reporting Act** was enacted **26 October 1970** and took effect **25 April 1971** — the first federal law giving consumers the right to see their own credit file, dispute what was in it, and receive the specific reasons behind an adverse decision. It was passed directly in response to the abuses of the character-file era: Congress had heard testimony describing exactly the kind of subjective, unverifiable, undisclosed judgement above.

FCRA did something beyond creating consumer rights, and this is the mechanism that actually reshaped the industry: it required **disputable, standardised, machine-readable records** at the exact moment bureaus were shifting from paper ledgers to computerised files. Meeting that requirement — auditable data, correction workflows, retention rules — was expensive, and **only well-capitalised bureaus could afford to comply at scale**. The consolidation this forced is covered in [[origins/credit-bureaus/the-fight|The Fight]].

Four years later, the **Equal Credit Opportunity Act (1974)** made race- and sex-based denial illegal, pushing lenders further toward scoring models that could be defended as "neutral" — a defensibility argument that, as this file's mechanism note discusses, is itself contested.

## The Bureaus Adopt the Score

Fair, Isaac's algorithms and the bureaus' file infrastructure remained two separate things for another fifteen years after FCRA. The bureau-wide **FICO Score launched with Equifax in 1989**; TransUnion and Experian's predecessor followed, with **all three national bureaus using FICO scores by 1991**. For the first time, a lender pulling a report from any of the three got the same kind of number, built the same way, rather than each bureau (and each lender, in-house) running its own bespoke scorecard.

> **Note the two separate "founding" dates this file has to hold at once.** Statistical credit scoring is a 1956–58 story; the *bureau-wide, standardised* credit score most consumers mean when they say "my credit score" is a 1989–91 story. Conflating the two — treating FICO's founding as the moment credit scores began — erases thirty years in which scoring existed but wasn't yet the industry standard.
