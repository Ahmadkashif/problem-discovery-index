# Auto OEMs

**Layer:** Origin — a parent industry, not a prospect
**Primary Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-02-departmental-item-level|2 — Departmental & Item-Level]]
**Founding event:** Joseph Orlicky formulates MRP at IBM c.1964; Black & Decker first production user, same year; Orlicky's *Material Requirements Planning* published 1975; Oliver Wight's MRP II, 1983. Running in parallel and unrelated: Taiichi Ohno's kanban inside Toyota from 1953, company-wide by 1963.
**Children in this vault:** contract-manufacturing, metal-fabrication, electronics-contract-mfg, auto-dealers-independent, auto-repair-shops, food-manufacturing

## Profile

**What it is:** the design, engineering and final assembly of motor vehicles from tens of thousands of purchased and fabricated parts, sequenced onto a moving line at a rate the whole supply base must match exactly.

**Who pays:** eventually the buyer of the finished vehicle, but the more interesting customer of everything in this file is working capital itself. A part sitting in a warehouse is cash that has stopped moving; a line that stops for want of a part is worse — it is cash actively burning.

**The economics:** a vehicle has on the order of 30,000 parts sourced from thousands of suppliers. Get the timing wrong in either direction — too much inventory or too little — and the business loses money in a way that is entirely avoidable in principle and brutally hard in practice, because the plan has to account for every part, every supplier, every day.

## Why This Is an Origin

Auto OEMs are where two incompatible computerised answers to the same question were built within a decade of each other, on opposite sides of the Pacific, in near-total ignorance of one another — and the industry spent the following fifty years discovering which one it actually needed.

**One answer became a piece of software you could buy. The other stayed a shop-floor discipline you had to learn.** The software spread first. The discipline turned out to matter more, and arrived in Detroit only after a joint venture forced two workforces to stand next to each other.

## The Contested Decision

> **Should tomorrow's production plan be computed centrally from a forecast and pushed at the factory floor, or should it emerge from what the next station actually pulls?**

Both are legitimate engineering answers to "get the right part to the right place at the right time." They are not the same answer, and treating them as one — as a great deal of business writing still does — is the single most correctable error available to anyone learning this history.
