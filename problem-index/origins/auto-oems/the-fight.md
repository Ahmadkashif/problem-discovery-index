# The Fight: Detroit Tries to Buy What Toyota Built

**Origin:** [[origins/auto-oems/profile|Auto OEMs]]
**Outcome:** Contested. Toyota's method was vindicated on the factory floor; Detroit adopted it only partially, only after a decade of trying the alternative, and the industry's dominant systems today are a hybrid of both lineages.

## The Opening

By the late 1970s the American car buyer could directly compare a Japanese import against a domestic equivalent, and the comparison kept going one way. **General Motors, Ford and Chrysler had by then been running MRP for over a decade** — computerised, forecast-driven production planning was not the missing ingredient. Something else was.

## The Weapon Detroit Chose First

General Motors, under chairman Roger Smith, answered with more computing rather than a different kind of computing. Over the 1980s **GM spent on the order of $90 billion** remaking its manufacturing base, including a 1981 robotics joint venture with Fanuc (GMF Robotics) that made GM briefly the world's largest robot manufacturer, with a stated ambition of moving from 302 robots in 1980 toward 14,000 by the decade's end and, eventually, "lights-out" factories run by machines alone.

It did not work. Robots welded doors shut. Painting robots reportedly painted each other. Several installations were removed shortly after going in. **The figure is widely cited across business-history sources and should be read as an order of magnitude for a decade-long, multi-programme spend, not an audited single invoice** — but even generous downward revisions leave the same conclusion: an enormous, computationally sophisticated capital programme failed to close the quality gap.

## The Weapon That Actually Worked

**NUMMI** — New United Motor Manufacturing, Inc. — opened in Fremont, California in **1984**, a joint venture between GM and Toyota at a GM plant previously so troubled it had been closed for poor quality and labour conflict. Toyota trained NUMMI's supervisors directly: 257 group and team leaders rotated through Toyota's Takaoka plant in nine sessions across 1984–85. **The workforce was substantially the same one GM had been unable to make competitive.** What changed was the system: kanban-disciplined pull production, andon cords that let any worker stop the line, small-team quality authority, and a production plan that let reality — not a forecast — set the pace. The first Chevrolet Nova came off the NUMMI line in December 1984, and the plant went on to match Toyota-level quality with far less automation than GM's flagship plants.

That comparison — **modest capital, disciplined system, better result versus enormous capital, no discipline, worse result** — became the empirical case for lean manufacturing in the US, formalised six years later when the MIT International Motor Vehicle Program published *The Machine That Changed the World* (1990) and gave the American management vocabulary the term it still uses.

## The Honest Caveat

It is tempting to tell this as a clean morality tale: Toyota's philosophy, vindicated, defeats Detroit's hardware. **That is too neat.** MRP and MRP II did not disappear — they became, and remain, the backbone of ERP systems (SAP, Oracle, Infor) running inside essentially every OEM and tier supplier in this vault's `contract-manufacturing`, `metal-fabrication` and `electronics-contract-mfg` notes. What actually happened is a **hybrid**: push-based MRP for planning and procurement, layered with pull-based kanban signals for shop-floor execution — "MRP for the plan, kanban for the floor" — which is neither system in its pure form and is what most modern manufacturing actually runs.
