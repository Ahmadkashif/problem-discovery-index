# Legacy: What Ocean Shipping Bequeathed

**Origin:** [[origins/ocean-shipping-ports/profile|Ocean Shipping & Ports]]

## The Direct Inheritance

Every logistics business in this vault still moves the same unit McLean's ships made standard.

| Child | What it inherited |
|---|---|
| [[industries/freight-brokerage\|Freight Brokerage]] | The container and the trailer as the unit of a deal — a broker still prices a lane by the box or the load, the direct descendant of a standard argued over from 1956 to 1968. |
| [[industries/warehouse-3pl\|Warehouse & 3PL]] | The container as the unit that arrives at the dock door, and the stowage-planning problem re-appearing one level down as pallet and slot optimisation inside the warehouse. |
| [[industries/customs-brokers\|Customs Brokers]] | The manifest as a machine-checkable document — only possible once cargo was described in standard, countable units rather than free text, which is the precondition for every electronic customs filing this industry now runs on. |
| [[industries/cold-chain-logistics\|Cold Chain Logistics]] | The refrigerated container (the "reefer"), the temperature-controlled special case of the exact same standardisation logic — a box with an identity, now also carrying a sensor reading. |

## The Deeper Inheritance: the unit before the model

The single clearest lesson this origin hands to every industry downstream is sequencing, not technology: **you cannot build a tracking, forecasting or optimisation layer over an asset that has not yet been standardised into a discrete, identical, addressable unit.** Freight brokerage, warehousing, customs and cold chain all inherited a *pre-solved* version of that problem — the container already existed as a unit before any of them needed to build software around it. Industries without their own container moment, where the core asset is still a "job," a "case," or a "load" of inconsistent shape, are structurally a decade or more behind these four, no matter how much software gets pointed at them.

## The Second Inheritance: adoption is a labour negotiation, not just a technology rollout

[[origins/ocean-shipping-ports/the-fight|The Fight]] recorded that containerisation's opponent was not a competitor but the displaced workforce, and that the workforce's negotiated settlement — wage guarantees and severance under the 1960 Mechanization and Modernization Agreement — delayed the technology's price benefit by roughly a decade even after the technology itself had won. Every child industry in this vault that automates a task currently done by skilled manual labour is re-running that negotiation in miniature, whether or not it is named as one.

## What an Episode Should Take From This

1. **Standardisation is the invention, and it is slow.** Twelve years passed between the Ideal-X and ISO/R 668. The box everyone recognises as "the shipping container" is a multilateral agreement, not a single company's product.
2. **A technology can win completely and still not show up in the price for a decade.** The labour side of a disruption gets to negotiate terms on the way out, and those terms show up as a lag between "the old way is obsolete" and "the price reflects it."
3. **Look for the unit before looking for the model.** Every child industry above only became a modelling opportunity once its core asset had a stable, countable identity. That is very often the actual bottleneck an FDE is being asked to solve, disguised as a request for a forecasting model.
