# Ocean Shipping & Ports

**Layer:** Origin — a parent industry, not a prospect
**Primary Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-02-departmental-item-level|2 — Departmental & Item-Level]] *(pre-computer trigger — see below)*
**Founding event:** SS Ideal-X sails April 26 1956, Port Newark → Houston, 58 containers; ISO/TC 104 formed 1961; ISO/R 668 published Feb 1968
**Children in this vault:** freight-brokerage, warehouse-3pl, customs-brokers, cold-chain-logistics

## Profile

**What it is:** The movement of freight by sea, and the ports that load and unload it. For most of its history this was a business of loose cargo, handled piece by piece, by hand.

**Who pays:** Shippers, by weight and by piece, at a rate that for a century mostly reflected how many hours of dockworker labour a given cargo required to move — not how far it travelled.

**The economics:** Before 1956, cargo-handling labour was the largest controllable cost in ocean freight, and it was almost entirely a function of how many individual items had to be lifted, carried and stacked by hand. The insight that ends this origin's founding decade is that the item being lifted did not have to be the cargo at all.

## Why This Is an Origin

Every other origin in this vault begins with a computer. This one does not, and the vault records that deliberately: **ocean shipping's founding event is a steel box.**

The container did something a mainframe could not have done on its own: it converted an unbounded variety of loose freight into one standard, stackable, countable, trackable unit. That standardisation — agreed internationally over more than a decade, not invented in a single stroke — is the precondition for manifests that could be read by a machine, freight tracked by an identifier instead of described in prose, and a supply chain that could eventually be modelled as data at all.

**Standardisation precedes digitisation.** That is the lesson this origin exists to teach, and why it earns a place beside the mainframes and reservation systems that open every other file in this series.

## The Contested Decision

> **Do you pay to move the cargo, or do you pay to move a box that happens to contain it?**

Answering "the box" destroyed a labour system built around loose cargo, took roughly a decade longer than the technology alone would suggest to show up in the price of freight, and created the standardised unit that every logistics business in this vault still moves.
