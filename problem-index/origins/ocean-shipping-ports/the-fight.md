# The Fight: The Shipping Lines Against the Longshore Labour System

**Origin:** [[origins/ocean-shipping-ports/profile|Ocean Shipping & Ports]]
**Outcome:** Containerisation won. The workforce it displaced was compensated, not defeated outright — and the compensation slowed the very cost savings the technology promised.

## The Opening

Containerisation's opponent was not a rival shipping company. It was the workforce whose entire trade the container made unnecessary: the longshoremen who loaded and unloaded break-bulk cargo by hand, organised into powerful unions on both American coasts — the **International Longshore and Warehouse Union (ILWU)** on the West Coast, the **International Longshoremen's Association (ILA)** on the East.

The hostility was entirely rational. A container that could be lifted by a single crane operator in minutes replaced a gang of longshoremen who had spent a full shift loading the same cargo by hand. Fewer people were needed, permanently, to move the same tonnage. In the New York/New Jersey port alone, the registered longshore workforce fell from roughly **35,000 in the early 1950s to under 10,000 by 1976**.

## The Negotiated Settlement

Rather than fight containerisation to a standstill, the ILWU — under the leadership of **Harry Bridges**, over the dissent of some of its own members — negotiated the **Mechanization and Modernization Agreement of 1960** with the Pacific Maritime Association. In exchange for accepting containerised cargo handling, the union secured wage guarantees and severance payments for workers who chose to leave the industry.

That is the shape of the fight: **not a market contest between competitors, but a negotiated transition between an industry adopting a new technology and the workforce being displaced by it** — resolved through compensation rather than confrontation, and resolved earlier on the West Coast than the East.

## The Geographic Casualty: Manhattan's Piers

Containerisation also fought a fight over geography, with a clear, visible loser. Handling containers requires space — for stacking boxes, for truck and rail access, for wide-reaching cranes — that the dense, historic piers of Manhattan and Brooklyn never had. As container volume grew through the 1960s and 70s, cargo activity shifted to where that space existed: **Port Newark and the new Port Elizabeth-Port Authority Marine Terminal**, which opened in 1962 as the world's first purpose-built container port, and later Staten Island.

By the early 1980s, almost all maritime cargo handling in the New York region had left Manhattan. A waterfront that had organised the city's working life for a century emptied out within two decades — not because the port lost business to a rival, but because its own geography could no longer host the unit its industry now moved in.

## The Honest Caveat

**"Containerisation instantly crushed shipping costs" is the tidy version, and it does not survive the record.** The unit-cost drop at the point of loading was real and dramatic — Levinson's $5.86-to-$0.16-per-ton figure is well supported. But the broader trade-cost and trade-growth effect that containerisation is credited with was **delayed by roughly a decade**. Academic work on the period (Bridgman et al.) finds that from 1970 to 1975, real total labour cost per ton in the New York/New Jersey port fell only about **7%**, despite productivity roughly doubling over the same years — because the unions that had accepted mechanisation also extracted fringe-benefit and job-security concessions, including guaranteed-annual-income funds, that captured much of the productivity gain rather than passing it through to freight rates.

**The technology won. The price signal it was supposed to send took ten years to arrive**, because the losing side of the fight had negotiated a share of the winnings on the way out.
