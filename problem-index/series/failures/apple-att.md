# Failure: App Tracking Transparency (2020–2021)

**Lesson class:** the-platform-changed-the-rules
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-09-programmatic|9 — Programmatic]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/programmatic-ad-platforms|Programmatic Ad Platforms]] · [[industries/marketing-attribution-vendors|Marketing Attribution Vendors]] · [[industries/customer-data-platforms|Customer Data Platforms]] · [[industries/app-marketing-firms|App Marketing Firms]]
**What was claimed:** A stable, observable device identifier (the IDFA) meant mobile advertising could measure, attribute and optimise spend on individual users the way desktop programmatic already did — the identifier was infrastructure, durable enough to build a whole discipline of user-acquisition science on top of.

## Why It Was Plausible

Believing the IDFA was durable infrastructure was not naive; it was the reasonable extrapolation of everything the previous fifteen years had taught the industry. Apple had shipped the Identifier for Advertisers itself, as a deliberate replacement for less privacy-respecting device fingerprinting methods, which made it look like an endorsed, sanctioned part of the platform rather than a tolerated grey-area workaround. Programmatic advertising's entire model — buying an audience rather than a placement, the reversal this vault's own Wave 9 history names as the defining shift of the era — depended on a stable cross-app identifier, and by the mid-2010s a genuinely sophisticated measurement science had been built on top of it: mobile measurement partners, lifetime-value modelling from a user's first days of app activity, and creative testing at scale, all engineered by people this vault's own hub note for app marketing firms rightly calls "some of the most sophisticated lifetime-value modelling in marketing." An industry does not build that much precision science on a foundation it expects to be pulled without warning.

Apple's own justification was also genuinely defensible on its face, not a pretext. Users had no visible mechanism to consent to or refuse cross-app tracking, and a persistent identifier following a person across every app on their phone is a real privacy exposure by any reasonable standard, independent of whether advertisers found it convenient. Apple previewed the change at WWDC in June 2020 and delayed enforcement to give developers time to adapt — behaviour consistent with a platform trying to manage a difficult transition responsibly, not one springing a trap.

## What Actually Killed It

**iOS 14.5, shipped 26 April 2021,** made IDFA access opt-in rather than opt-out, and adoption of the opt-in prompt was low industry-wide. This vault's own Wave 9 history calls it correctly: "the single largest disruption to mobile targeting and attribution to date." The mechanism the industry replaced it with, SKAdNetwork, is instructive about what was actually lost, because the loss was not merely "fewer identifiers" — it was a change in the *kind* of information available. Under SKAdNetwork a marketer receives a postback carrying a coarse conversion value encoded in a handful of bits the marketer must design themselves, delivered after a randomised delay, and suppressed entirely below a privacy threshold — precisely the volume range a new campaign starts in. This vault's own app-marketing-firms hub note is exact about the consequence: user-level attribution was replaced by "aggregated, delayed, sometimes suppressed entirely" signal, and the conversion-value schema — the design choice of what those few bits should encode — determines everything every downstream model can subsequently learn, and most teams picked a schema at launch and never revisited it. The industry adapted with real technical inventiveness — predictive lifetime-value modelling from a user's first hours, incrementality geo-testing — but the underlying constraint was imposed once, by one company's product decision, and every subsequent adaptation operates inside it rather than around it.

## What It Was Not

ATT is sometimes folded into the same narrative as the general decline of third-party cookies on desktop, as though it were one continuous "privacy crackdown" story with a single cause and timeline. It is worth being precise about the distinction this vault's own history file draws: ATT has a hard death date, 26 April 2021, and a single identifiable actor. Cookie deprecation, by contrast, has run on "the same story on a longer and far more erratic timeline," repeatedly delayed and revised by the party enforcing it. Treating the two as one event overstates how planned or coordinated the industry's broader identity collapse has been — ATT was decisive and sudden; the rest of the identity ecosystem's erosion has been slow, inconsistent, and is still not fully resolved. It is also not accurate to describe ATT as the origin of measurement uncertainty in this industry: the deeper and older problem, this vault's own history documents, is that programmatic's whole measurement layer trained on last-click — a convention inherited from 1996 cookie tooling, not chosen for its accuracy — long before Apple's decision made the identity layer itself unstable. ATT did not create the industry's measurement problem. It removed one input the industry had used to paper over a measurement problem that already existed.

## The Lesson That Transfers

An entire discipline — user acquisition, at $90B in global spend by this vault's own count — had built its central practice on an identifier it did not own, issued by a platform under no obligation to keep issuing it on the same terms. The identifier was never a contract; it was a product decision Apple could, and did, reverse, for a reason (user privacy) that outranked the industry's operational convenience in every reasonable ethical framing. The transferable diagnostic is not "will the platform eventually remove this capability" — for any capability a platform grants rather than contractually guarantees, the answer is effectively certain over a long enough horizon. It is: **does the business's core measurement or targeting function depend on something the platform can revoke unilaterally, and if the platform revoked it tomorrow, does the business still have a path to the outcome it needs, even a cruder one?** App marketing firms that had already invested in first-party data collection, incrementality testing, or aggregated modelling absorbed the shock; firms that had built exclusively on user-level device identity did not have a fallback to reach for. The same question applies to any business built on a platform-granted API, scraping allowance, or data-sharing agreement that carries no contractual permanence — a category this vault documents repeatedly across creator, gig and marketplace industries, always with the same shape: convenience granted unilaterally, at a cost of the recipient's own choosing, is not infrastructure. It is a favour, revocable on the granter's schedule.
