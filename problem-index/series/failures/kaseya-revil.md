# Failure: Kaseya VSA / REvil (2021)

**Lesson class:** concentration-became-systemic
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-06-cloud-saas|6 — Cloud & SaaS]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/it-managed-services|IT Managed Services]] · [[industries/cybersecurity-mssp|Cybersecurity MSSPs]]
**What was claimed:** One remote-monitoring-and-management agent, holding standing privileged access across every endpoint an MSP manages, would let a single technician support thousands of machines across hundreds of client businesses — the mechanism this vault's own history file credits with making flat-rate managed services viable in place of break-fix.
**Capital at risk:** not a funded venture at the time of the incident; omitted rather than guessed

## Why It Was Plausible

There is nothing exotic about the bet Kaseya, and every one of its competitors, made. An MSP serving hundreds of small businesses cannot afford to build bespoke tooling for each one, and a technician cannot physically visit every site to check whether a disk is filling up or a patch is missing. The entire economic case for managed services, laid out in this vault's own history file for the industry, rests on one mechanism: an RMM agent installed on a client's machines that reports continuously to a dashboard the MSP can watch from anywhere, and can act on remotely — patch, reconfigure, remediate — without a truck roll. That agent needs privileged, standing access to do its job. There is no version of remote monitoring and management that does not require broad, persistent reach into the systems it manages; the privilege is not a design mistake layered on top of the product, it is the product.

Kaseya, founded in 2000, was one of several vendors — ConnectWise, N-able, Datto/Autotask among them — that had built a two-decade-old, well-established category on exactly this trust model by 2021. Choosing a centralised RMM tool over hiring enough technicians to visit every client site by hand was not a reckless shortcut. It was, and remains, the only model that makes an MSP's flat-rate contract economically survivable, and tens of thousands of MSPs worldwide had made the identical choice by the time REvil found a way through Kaseya's specific implementation of it.

## What Actually Killed It

Security researcher Wietse Boonstra, working with the Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure, discovered a chain of vulnerabilities in Kaseya's VSA software beginning 23 March 2021, with an additional flaw found 2 April; DIVD notified Kaseya on 6 April 2021. Kaseya had not finished patching the full set of reported issues before the REvil ransomware group exploited an authentication-bypass vulnerability in VSA to push a malicious update through the software's own trusted channel, on 2 July 2021. Because VSA's entire purpose is to reach every endpoint an MSP administers, the malicious update propagated the same way a legitimate one would: to roughly **60 MSPs directly**, and through them to an estimated **800–1,500 downstream small and medium businesses** — most of which had never bought anything from Kaseya and had no way to have known their exposure depended on it. REvil demanded **$70 million** in bitcoin for a universal decryptor; Kaseya stated it did not pay.

A universal decryption key reached Kaseya on 23 July 2021 from what the company called an unnamed "trusted third party." A Washington Post investigation published two months later established that the FBI had already obtained the key — having penetrated REvil's own infrastructure — but withheld it for roughly three weeks to pursue a separate operation targeting the group, rather than releasing it immediately to the businesses still locked out of their own systems. That is a second, distinct concentration failure sitting inside the first one: the party positioned to end the harm quickest was not the party bearing it, and it made a resourcing choice about a covert operation that extended the downtime of businesses who had no visibility into that trade-off and no vote in it. REvil's own infrastructure disappeared from the internet around 13 July 2021; a Ukrainian national, Yaroslav Vasinskyi, was arrested in Poland on 8 October 2021 and later sentenced, on 1 May 2024, to just under fourteen years and ordered to pay $16 million in restitution for over 2,500 ransomware attacks; a named Russian co-defendant remained at large as of mid-2024.

## What It Was Not

The story that flattens this incident is "REvil hacked 1,500 businesses." It did not. REvil compromised Kaseya's VSA infrastructure and, through it, roughly 60 MSP customers who ran that software directly. The 800 to 1,500 downstream businesses were never individually breached — no attacker touched their networks, guessed their passwords, or exploited anything specific to them. They were encrypted because their MSP's monitoring agent, the same one that let a technician patch their machines without visiting, had exactly the reach a malicious actor needed once it controlled the update channel feeding that agent. That distinction matters: this was not a case of a thousand-plus organisations having weak security. It was sixty compromise points cascading through a trust relationship each of those organisations had extended for an entirely different, entirely reasonable purpose. It is also not, primarily, a story about ransom payment mattering — Kaseya says it did not pay, and the businesses still recovered, via a key law enforcement already held. The lesson is not "pay or don't get your files back." It is that the recovery path ran through an intermediary — the FBI — making its own calculation about timing, one more layer removed from the businesses actually losing money every day the systems stayed encrypted.

## The Lesson That Transfers

The party that chose the concentration was every MSP and every RMM vendor building the standard tooling of the category — a rational, well-precedented choice that made the entire industry's pricing model possible. The party that bore the loss was the roughly 1,500 downstream small businesses that never selected Kaseya, never evaluated its security posture, and had no seat at any table where that risk was assessed — plus, in a narrower but real sense, whichever affected businesses stayed locked out for the three extra weeks a law-enforcement operation's own priorities cost them. The transferable question for an FDE evaluating any vendor with standing privileged access reaching many downstream customers — an RMM tool, an identity provider, a CI/CD pipeline, a browser extension with broad permissions — is not "is this vendor's security good," but "how many organisations that never chose this vendor are nonetheless exposed to its failure, and do any of them know it."
