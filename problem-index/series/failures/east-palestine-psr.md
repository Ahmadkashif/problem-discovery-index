# Failure: Precision Scheduled Railroading and East Palestine (1993–2023)

**Lesson class:** software-failure-physical-harm
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-04-client-server-erp|4 — Client–Server & ERP]]
**Industries touched:** [[origins/railroads/profile|Railroads]] · [[industries/freight-brokerage|Freight Brokerage]]
**What was claimed:** Precision Scheduled Railroading — fixed, published point-to-point train schedules in place of accumulate-until-the-yard-is-full classification — makes a freight railroad dramatically more efficient without a corresponding cost to safety or service.

> **Wave note.** Railroads' primary wave is Wave 2; their secondary is Wave 4, which is used here because PSR's own origin (1993, Illinois Central) sits inside it. The event this file must also account for — the 3 February 2023 derailment — falls three decades later, in no wave the spine assigns to railroads at all. Flagged rather than forced.

> **What kind of "software failure" this is.** Unlike this class's other three cases, the proximate mechanical cause here is a physical bearing failure, not a code defect. The automated system in question is the **wayside defect detector** — a trackside sensor-and-threshold system that reads bearing temperature and triggers an alert once it crosses a set point. It functioned as designed. Whether it was designed, spaced, and thresholded well enough, and whether the operating model surrounding it left less margin to catch the fault earlier, is the contested question this file must leave open.

This file draws on `origins/railroads/the-fight.md` and `origins/railroads/profile.md` for the PSR history, and on direct NTSB and FRA material for the derailment itself.

## Why It Was Plausible

For most of the twentieth century, North American freight railroads ran on a hub-and-spoke, accumulate-until-the-yard-is-full model: cars sat in a classification yard until there was enough volume to justify assembling and running a train. This maximised each train's utilisation, but a car's transit time depended entirely on how quickly its yard happened to fill — nobody controlled that precisely. **E. Hunter Harrison**, who became Illinois Central's CEO in 1993 (though he traced the underlying thinking to ideas from the early 1980s at Burlington Northern; some credit his predecessor Ed Moyers with an uncredited precursor at the smaller MidSouth railroad), replaced that with fixed, published point-to-point schedules — running the train on the timetable, the way passenger rail always had, regardless of whether the yard was full.

The case for this was not speculative by the time it spread. At every railroad Harrison moved to — Canadian National in 2003, Canadian Pacific from 2012, CSX in 2017 — operating ratios improved sharply and profitability records followed. PSR is one of the plainer cases in this vault's origin research of a management discipline simply beating an unmanaged default, because hub-and-spoke was never actually optimal — it was unmanaged, not optimal, and PSR imposed discipline on it. Consolidating classification yards and cutting the locomotive and car fleet needed to move the same freight is a real, auditable cost reduction, not an accounting trick, and every Class I railroad that resisted PSR was competing against rivals who had already taken the operating-ratio gains. Adopting it was the financially rational decision at every railroad that made it.

## What Actually Killed It — What the Record Shows

On 3 February 2023, Norfolk Southern train 32N — roughly 150 cars, 141 of them loaded — derailed 38 cars on the east side of East Palestine, Ohio. The NTSB's stated probable cause is specific and mechanical: **the failure of an overheated wheel bearing on the 23rd railcar, which caused the axle to separate.** Wayside defect detectors along the route did function as designed: one alerted the crew shortly before the derailment, and the crew initiated emergency braking. The train could not stop in time. Eleven of the derailed cars carried hazardous materials, including four tank cars of vinyl chloride (with a fifth intact car also carrying it). Responders subsequently conducted a "vent and burn" of the vinyl chloride cars — a decision NTSB Chair Jennifer Homendy testified in June 2024 was **not necessary**: polymerisation was not in fact occurring, and the cars could have been left to cool.

What the NTSB's own published findings do not do is draw a causal line from Precision Scheduled Railroading to the bearing's failure or to the detector spacing that let it progress as far as it did. That argument belongs to a separate, parallel body of testimony: labour unions, congressional witnesses and transportation-policy researchers have argued, publicly and repeatedly, that PSR's cost discipline — fewer crew per train, longer and heavier consists, reduced inspection time, deferred maintenance — creates the conditions in which a defect like this one is more likely to progress undetected toward failure. **That argument is contested, not established.** The NTSB's probable cause is the bearing and the physics of its failure; it does not, in the material examined for this file, formally attribute the surrounding conditions to Norfolk Southern's operating model. Both things are true at once: the mechanical cause is not in dispute, and PSR's causal role in the conditions around it is not resolved.

## What It Was Not

**This was not a Positive Train Control failure**, and the two are worth separating carefully because they are constantly conflated in commentary about rail safety generally. PTC is a federally mandated collision-avoidance system — enforcing braking automatically to prevent train-on-train collisions and over-speed derailments — completed across 57,536 US route-miles by 29 December 2020 at a cost of roughly $15 billion. It has no bearing-temperature sensing function and would not have detected or prevented an overheating axle bearing; that is the wayside detector's job, an entirely separate system. PTC does not appear in the NTSB's East Palestine findings because it is not the relevant technology for this failure mode.

Nor was the vent-and-burn a safety measure the chemistry made unavoidable, however it was described in real time. The NTSB's own June 2024 testimony found the decision was made on limited temperature readings and one tank's pressure release, rather than on evidence that dangerous polymerisation was actually under way, and concluded the burn was avoidable. That finding is a separate, narrower correction about the emergency response — it does not resolve the PSR question above.

## The Lesson That Transfers

Two lessons sit side by side here, and should not be merged into one. The first is mechanical and undisputed: an automated monitoring system — the wayside detector — worked exactly as designed, alerted the crew, and still could not close the gap between "problem detected" and "train stopped" before the bearing failed catastrophically. A detection system that is technically functioning is not the same thing as a detection system that is fast enough, and the interval between those two states is where this failure actually lived — a variant of the same theme running through this whole lesson class, where a system that is not silently broken can still fail to act in time to matter.

The second lesson is the one this file must leave open rather than resolve: **a scheduling discipline that removes slack from a system removes the slack that used to absorb its failures** — fewer spare crews, fewer spare locomotives, less inspection time, all of which were also, unglamorously, safety margin. Whether that specific mechanism reached this specific bearing is an argument still running, in litigation and congressional testimony, as this file is written. Teaching it as settled in either direction — "PSR caused East Palestine" or "PSR had nothing to do with it" — claims more certainty than the record, including the NTSB's own findings, currently supports.
