# Failure: The Reinhart-Rogoff Spreadsheet Error (2010–2013)

**Lesson class:** the-measurement-was-wrong
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-03-pc-spreadsheet|3 — The PC & the Spreadsheet]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/accounting-firms-smb|SMB Accounting Firms]] · [[industries/bi-analytics-platforms|BI & Analytics Platforms]]
**What was claimed:** In "Growth in a Time of Debt" (2010), Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff found that once a country's gross public debt crosses roughly 90% of GDP, median and average real GDP growth fall sharply — a specific, actionable threshold policymakers could cite as evidence for fiscal consolidation.
**Capital at risk:** not a funded venture — omitted; the paper's consequence was sovereign fiscal policy, not private investment.

## Why It Was Plausible

Reinhart and Rogoff were not fringe figures producing a provocative result to attract attention. Rogoff was a former IMF chief economist; both authors had, the year before, published *This Time Is Different*, an eight-hundred-year, multi-country dataset of financial crises that was already treated as a landmark reference across the profession. "Growth in a Time of Debt" extended that same painstaking historical-data tradition to a specific, falsifiable claim, and it circulated for three years — presented at conferences, distributed as a working paper, cited in hundreds of subsequent papers — before anyone outside the two authors saw the underlying spreadsheet. Academic economics at the time did not routinely require data and code deposit alongside publication; a reader's only real option was to trust the authors' evident rigour and reputation, because replicating the finding meant rebuilding the entire historical dataset from scratch.

The timing mattered as much as the pedigree. The paper arrived in 2010, in the middle of the post-2008 sovereign debt crisis, exactly when policymakers in the United States and the eurozone were looking for empirical grounding for a fiscal direction many had already chosen politically. A specific number — 90% — attached to a specific, alarming consequence by two of the most credentialed economists working on exactly this question was precisely the kind of citable evidence a budget debate wants, and there was no competing empirical claim of comparable authority pointing the other way. Trusting it was not intellectually lazy; independently checking it required access nobody outside the authors possessed.

## What Actually Killed It

In April 2013, Thomas Herndon, a PhD student at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, requested the original spreadsheet as part of a class replication exercise, received it, and — working with his advisors Michael Ash and Robert Pollin — published "Does High Public Debt Consistently Stifle Economic Growth? A Critique of Reinhart and Rogoff." The critique identified three distinct problems, not one: a coding error in which an AVERAGE formula excluded five countries from the high-debt calculation (the vault's own file dates this precisely — `AVERAGE(L30:L44)` should have read `L30:L49`); the selective exclusion of postwar high-debt, high-growth data from countries including Australia, Canada and New Zealand; and an unconventional weighting method that treated a single year of one country's data as equal in weight to averages spanning many years for others. Correcting all three moved the headline figure for growth in countries above the 90% threshold from −0.1% to +2.2% — turning a sharp cliff into a much more ordinary number.

Reinhart and Rogoff acknowledged the coding error publicly but disputed the framing of "selective omissions," defending their data choices as methodologically reasonable, and continued to argue that a broader, more modest correlation between high debt and slower growth survives the correction even though the dramatic 90% threshold does not. That is the competing account genuinely left open by this episode: critics hold that the *policy-relevant* claim — a specific number with a specific, alarming consequence — depended on all three errors together and does not survive; the original authors hold that the *general* relationship between high debt and growth was never really about a sharp cliff in the first place, and that politicians overread a nuanced finding into a bright line. Neither position has fully displaced the other in the subsequent economics literature.

## What It Was Not

It was not, as the popular retelling has it, "one Excel formula that sank global austerity policy." Three separate problems produced the distorted result, and per Herndon, Ash and Pollin's own analysis, the coding error — the easiest of the three to explain in a single sentence, and the one nearly every retelling repeats to the exclusion of the other two — was not obviously the largest single contributor to the headline distortion. The selective exclusion of data and the unconventional weighting scheme mattered comparably, and a version of this story that reduces to "a spreadsheet range was off by five rows" understates how much of the error was a set of judgment calls rather than a single slip.

Nor was austerity policy invented by this paper. Reinhart-Rogoff was cited as supporting evidence by Paul Ryan's "Path to Prosperity" budget, by EU Commissioner Olli Rehn, and by UK Chancellor George Osborne — but eurozone fiscal consolidation and UK austerity had independent drivers that predated and outlasted this specific paper: bond-market pressure on peripheral eurozone sovereigns, EU treaty fiscal rules, and IMF programme conditionality among them. Removing the paper from the historical record would not have removed the policy it was cited to justify; it would have removed one piece of citable ammunition for a direction that had other, non-academic sources of political momentum. Nor did the correction settle the underlying economic question as cleanly as either side's public rhetoric implied at the time — corrected data still show some negative association between very high debt levels and growth in many specifications; what the correction actually killed was the sharp threshold, not the entire debate about debt and growth.

## The Lesson That Transfers

For three years, this paper's authority rested entirely on trust in its authors' reputations, because the actual computation behind the published conclusion was inaccessible to anyone positioned to find the error sooner. Peer review of a published conclusion is not the same act as replication of the underlying computation, and at the time, economics as a discipline did not require the latter as a condition of the former. An organisation — academic, corporate, or governmental — that treats "these are credible people" as a substitute for "I have rerun this calculation myself and reached the same number" has not verified anything; it has extended credit, and credit is exactly what failed here for three years running.

The narrower, vault-specific version of the lesson is one this file shares with the two spreadsheet failures beside it: a formula error of the precise shape Panko's field audits describe — a cell range off by a small number of rows — sat undetected inside one of the most cited economics papers of its decade, for the same reason 94% of audited operational spreadsheets carry undetected errors. Reputation, urgency, and the absence of anyone with both the access and the incentive to check the arithmetic combined to let a five-row range error shape a global policy argument for three years. An FDE evaluating any client decision that rests on a third party's proprietary model should ask, before treating that model's output as settled: has anyone with no stake in the answer actually rerun the calculation, or has everyone in the room simply agreed to trust the people who built it?
