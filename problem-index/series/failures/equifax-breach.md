# Failure: Equifax (2017)

**Lesson class:** concentration-became-systemic
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-01-mainframe-batch|1 — Mainframe & Batch]]
**Industries touched:** [[origins/credit-bureaus/profile|Credit Bureaus]] · [[industries/lending-marketplaces|Lending Marketplaces]] · [[industries/bnpl-providers|BNPL Providers]]
**What was claimed:** Consolidating a nation's credit history into a small number of well-capitalised, FCRA-compliant national bureaus would be more accountable, more standardised, and — because scale funds better security than fragmentation does — safer than thousands of local credit bureaus each keeping its own paper files.
**Capital at risk:** not a funded venture; omitted rather than guessed

## Why It Was Plausible

This vault's own origins file for credit bureaus already establishes the mechanism: the Fair Credit Reporting Act (1970), layered onto computerisation's economies of scale, is why roughly 2,000 local credit bureaus became three national ones by the late 1980s. That consolidation was not an accident of ambition. Three large, well-resourced, federally regulated companies are easier to hold accountable than two thousand ungoverned local ones, easier to audit, and — in principle — able to fund security operations no single local bureau could ever have afforded. A standardised national score, arriving in 1989, gave lenders a single comparable number instead of a patchwork of local reputations, and gave consumers one identifiable place to dispute an error rather than thousands. Believing that concentration made the system safer, not merely cheaper, was not naive in 1989. It was the same bet an entire generation of American financial infrastructure was making at the same time, largely with good results.

By 2017, Equifax was one of exactly three companies holding this position for the entire country. That is itself the plausible part of the story — the part worth taking seriously rather than mocking with hindsight. What is not plausible, and does not survive contact with the record, is any suggestion that the specific failure which followed required a sophisticated adversary or an unforeseeable flaw.

## What Actually Killed It

The vulnerability was **CVE-2017-5638**, in Apache Struts2, publicly disclosed with a patch available from Apache on **7 March 2017**. Equifax did not apply it to the specific system that mattered: a consumer dispute portal still running the vulnerable version months later. This was not an isolated lapse. A 2015 internal audit — two years before the breach — had already found that Equifax was not adhering to its own patching schedules, lacked a comprehensive asset inventory, did not prioritise patches by severity, and relied on what investigators later called an "honour system" for enforcement, without real accountability behind it. The vulnerability that mattered in 2017 was, in other words, a symptom of a process failure the company already knew about and had not fixed.

Attackers began exploiting the unpatched portal on **12 May 2017**. The intrusion ran undetected for **76 days**, discovered internally on **29 July 2017**, and was disclosed to the public on **7 September 2017** — a further 71-day gap the company has never fully explained. The result was the exposure of **approximately 147 million people's** names, Social Security numbers, dates of birth, addresses, driver's licence numbers, and around 209,000 credit card numbers — roughly 56% of American adults, most of whom had never chosen to have a relationship with Equifax at all, since lenders and businesses, not consumers, are the bureau's actual paying customers. Three executives, including CFO John Gamble, sold approximately $1.8 million in personal shares on 2 August 2017 — three days after the company internally confirmed an intrusion had occurred, and 36 days before the public was told. Equifax has said the executives had no knowledge a breach had occurred when they sold; the Department of Justice opened an investigation into potential insider trading on 18 September 2017, and no confirmed public outcome of that specific inquiry could be verified for this file. CEO Richard Smith retired on 26 September 2017; the CIO and CSO had already departed on 15 September. The settlement, finalised in July 2019, ran up to **$700 million**: an initial $300 million consumer relief fund (structurally expandable to $425 million if exhausted, which it was), $175 million to states and territories, and a $100 million CFPB civil penalty — the largest data-breach enforcement action on record at the time. In February 2020, the Department of Justice indicted four members of China's People's Liberation Army for the intrusion; this is an indictment, not a conviction — the defendants remain outside US custody, no trial has occurred, and China has denied the allegations. The attribution should be read as alleged, not established.

## What It Was Not

The story that keeps this breach comfortable is that a nation-state's cyber unit outmanoeuvred Equifax's defences — sophisticated attackers, an unwinnable fight. Even taking the DOJ's 2020 attribution at face value, that framing does not survive the timeline. The entry point was a publicly known vulnerability with a vendor-issued patch available more than two months before attackers used it, on a system inside a company whose own internal audit had already documented, in writing, exactly the process failures — no enforced patch cadence, no asset inventory, no severity-based prioritisation — that let a known, patchable flaw sit exposed on a public-facing portal. Whoever the DOJ says was on the keyboard did not need sophistication. They needed Equifax not to have applied a patch Apache had already published. Reaching for "advanced persistent threat" as the explanation lets the actual, mundane, previously-documented failure disappear behind the more flattering story that nobody could have stopped this.

## The Lesson That Transfers

The party that chose the consolidation was the credit-reporting industry itself, over decades, responding rationally to the FCRA's compliance burden and to computing's economies of scale — a choice that produced real, genuine benefits in accountability and standardisation for as long as the three resulting institutions kept their side of the bargain. The party that bore the loss when one of them didn't was 147 million individual people who were never Equifax's customers, never chose Equifax, and in the overwhelming majority of cases never interacted with Equifax directly in their lives. Equifax's shareholders absorbed a large settlement and a reputational hit; the people whose Social Security numbers are now permanently in circulation absorbed a risk with no expiry date and, per the settlement's own structure, a claims process capped most individual payouts far below what that risk is worth. An FDE evaluating any institution that has become the shared, authoritative record for an entire population — a bureau, a registry, a national identity system — should treat "we are large enough to secure this properly" as a claim to be checked against the institution's actual patch discipline and audit history, not accepted because the institution is large. Scale funds better security only if the institution actually spends the money and enforces the process; scale alone just makes the eventual failure bigger.
