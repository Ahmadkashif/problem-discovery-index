# Failure: Change Healthcare Ransomware (2024)

**Lesson class:** concentration-became-systemic
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-01-mainframe-batch|1 — Mainframe & Batch]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/medical-billing|Medical Billing]] · [[industries/healthcare-practice-software|Healthcare Practice Software]]
**What was claimed:** One shared clearinghouse, sitting between every provider and every payer, could clear the country's medical claims faster and more cheaply than each provider maintaining its own connections — and folding that clearinghouse into the country's largest insurer would make US healthcare's data exchange more efficient still.
**Capital at risk:** not a funded venture; omitted rather than guessed

## Why It Was Plausible

The clearinghouse layer this vault's own history file for medical billing already describes did not appear as a design flaw. It appeared because the alternative was worse. HIPAA's Administrative Simplification standards, phased in from 1996 through the 2000s, fixed the vocabulary (CPT, ICD) and the wire format (the 837 electronic claim) that every provider and every payer had to speak. No small billing operation could maintain direct, correctly formatted connections to every payer in the country, so an aggregation layer — a clearinghouse that validates a claim once and routes it to whichever payer needs it, in whatever variant of the format that payer requires — was the obvious and, for two decades, uncontroversial answer. This is the same aggregation logic this vault has already traced in credit bureaus and in core-banking vendors: fewer, larger, shared intermediaries genuinely are cheaper than thousands of bespoke connections, and cheaper usually means more reliable, not less, for as long as the intermediary itself is healthy.

Change Healthcare had grown into one of the largest such clearinghouses by the time UnitedHealth Group's Optum unit sought to acquire it. UnitedHealth's own argument for the deal, publicly stated, was efficiency: combining a payer, a data and analytics operation, and the clearinghouse that already touched a large share of the country's claims traffic would streamline exchange rather than duplicate it. The bet was not obviously reckless. Insurers, providers, and the acquiring company's own shareholders all had a plausible efficiency story to tell themselves, and the deal had a genuine, non-cynical logic behind it: one well-capitalised, well-secured operator running the shared plumbing should, in principle, be safer than a fragmented one.

It is worth being precise about who tested that bet before it was allowed to close. The Department of Justice sued to block the acquisition in February 2022, on antitrust grounds — its concern was competitive, that Optum would gain an unfair data advantage as both a payer and the clearinghouse operator, not cybersecurity. A federal court ruled against the DOJ, and the acquisition closed, per this vault's own history file, on 3 October 2022, reported at roughly $13 billion. The regulator that did intervene was worried about the wrong failure mode. Nobody with the power to stop the deal was arguing about what a single ransomware actor could do to a system this concentrated, seventeen months later.

## What Actually Killed It

The attack began, per CEO Andrew Witty's later congressional testimony, with a **Citrix remote access portal that lacked multi-factor authentication**. Attackers gained initial access on 12 February 2024, moved laterally through the network for nine days undetected, and deployed ransomware on 21 February 2024. The group ALPHV/BlackCat claimed responsibility; UnitedHealth confirmed on 29 February 2024 that the actor represented itself to the company as ALPHV/BlackCat. Reuters reported on 4 March 2024, citing a post on a hacker forum, that a payment of approximately **$22 million in bitcoin** had moved to a wallet associated with the group; UnitedHealth did not confirm the payment, saying only that it was focused on investigation and recovery.

The disruption was not contained to Change Healthcare's own systems. Because the clearinghouse sat in the claims and payment path for a large share of US healthcare, electronic claims submission, eligibility verification, and payment processing stopped for providers who had no direct relationship with Change Healthcare at all and no way to route around it. Providers reported losses running up to $100 million a day while the outage continued. UnitedHealth had advanced more than $6 billion in provider assistance by 16 April 2024 — advances, not gifts, structured to keep smaller practices and hospitals solvent through a crisis their own IT teams had no part in causing and no ability to fix. Witty testified before the Senate Finance Committee on 1 May 2024 and the House Energy and Commerce Committee on 2 May 2024, disclosing that attackers had exfiltrated approximately six terabytes of data over the nine days of lateral movement before the ransomware deployed; later reporting placed the number of Americans whose data was affected at approximately 190 million.

## What It Was Not

The tidy story is that this was a uniquely sophisticated attack on unusually hardened infrastructure — a cyber Pearl Harbor nobody could have stopped. It was not. The documented entry point was a remote access portal without multi-factor authentication, a baseline control absent on infrastructure sitting in the claims and payment path for a large share of the country's healthcare system. This is, structurally, the same shape of finding [[origins/credit-bureaus/legacy|this vault's credit-bureaus legacy file]] already reaches about Equifax's 2017 breach — a mundane, preventable lapse, not an unstoppable adversary — cited here as a prior finding of this project's own research, not as independent corroboration of this case. It is also not, primarily, a story about DOJ's antitrust theory being vindicated: the regulator that tried to block the merger was arguing about data advantage between a payer and a clearinghouse, and the failure that materialised was a security lapse that would have caused the same national disruption whoever owned the clearinghouse, provided it remained this concentrated. The deal's critics were right that the concentration was dangerous and wrong, on the public record, about which danger.

## The Lesson That Transfers

A clearinghouse — any shared layer that many independent parties rely on precisely so they do not each have to build and secure their own version of it — inherits the security posture of whoever operates it, and that posture becomes everyone's problem the moment it fails. The parties who chose the concentration were UnitedHealth's leadership and, by declining to block it, a federal court; the parties who bore the loss were providers with no seat in that decision and patients whose data moved through infrastructure they never knew existed. An FDE evaluating any vendor that has become "the" shared layer for an industry — a clearinghouse, a payments switch, an identity provider, an RMM tool — should ask not whether that vendor's security is good in isolation, but who is downstream of it, whether those downstream parties have any way to route around a failure, and whether anyone with the power to prevent that concentration was ever asked to weigh the risk that actually materialised rather than the one that was easiest to litigate.
