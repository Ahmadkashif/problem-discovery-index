# Failure: Vine (2013–2017)

**Lesson class:** the-platform-changed-the-rules
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-10-creator-platform|10 — The Creator Platform]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/creator-businesses|Creator Businesses]] · [[industries/ugc-video-platforms|UGC Video Platforms]]
**What was claimed:** A six-second looping video format, acquired by Twitter before it even launched, would become the next major short-form creator platform — and it produced a genuine, famous first generation of stars to prove the claim.

## Why It Was Plausible

Vine's premise was not a stretch in 2013. Twitter acquired the company before it had even launched, in October 2012, and released it in January 2013 — a signal that a platform already expert at distributing short bursts of content to a huge audience believed a short looping video format was the natural next format for that audience. The app grew fast and produced real stars: creators with millions of followers who used the six-second constraint inventively, building comedic and narrative styles that a longer format could not have produced. By any measure of product-market fit that a platform normally uses — daily active users, session frequency, imitators — Vine looked like a hit, and it looked like one at a moment when YouTube's Partner Program (2007) had already proven that a large platform audience could be turned into a revenue-sharing business for the people who built it. Believing Vine's stars were the vanguard of a comparable business was not naive. It was the same bet that had already paid off once, on a platform with more distribution than YouTube had in 2007.

## What Actually Killed It

The documented cause is not an algorithm change. It is that **Twitter never built Vine a creator payment mechanism at all.** YouTube's Partner Program existed from 2007; Twitch's subscription revenue split existed from 2011. Vine, launched in 2013, had neither an ad-revenue share nor any other route for a creator to earn directly from the platform in proportion to the audience they had built. Vine's biggest stars — reportedly earning real money, but earning it entirely off-platform, through brand deals negotiated independently — had every commercial incentive to go wherever a payment mechanism existed, and Instagram and, later, TikTok supplied one. This was reported publicly and repeatedly at the time as the core grievance: creators who had built Vine's audience said so directly, naming the absence of monetisation as the reason they were leaving.

Twitter's own strategic and commercial position compounded the gap rather than caused it. Twitter was under sustained pressure over its own growth and monetisation through the mid-2010s, and Vine was a subsidiary product inside a parent company fighting for its own commercial credibility — not a resourcing priority positioned to get the investment a creator-payments system would have required. Twitter announced Vine's shutdown in October 2016, with the app discontinued in January 2017. The timeline from "creators leaving because there is no way to get paid" to "shutdown announced" runs over roughly three years, not overnight — a slow strategic failure, not a triggered event.

## What It Was Not

This is the myth that most needs killing in this file, because Vine is routinely folded into the same story as the adpocalypse (`series/failures/youtube-adpocalypse.md`) or Facebook's pivot to video — "the platform changed the algorithm and killed the creators." **That is not what happened, and this vault's own spine research has already flagged and killed the conflation.** The adpocalypse and the pivot to video both have hard documentary evidence of a specific mechanism: YouTube's overnight, unappealable mass demonetisation by classifier in 2017, and Facebook's admitted, litigated overstatement of average video view time by 60–80%, settled for roughly $40M in 2019. Vine has no equivalent. There is no reported classifier retuning, no distribution-algorithm change identified as the proximate cause, no admitted metric fraud. What is documented is an absence — a payment mechanism that Vine's own owner never built — compounding a separate, unrelated strategic failure at the parent company.

The distinction matters because it changes what lesson the case teaches. "Platform changes the algorithm and creators lose income they were already earning" is a story about a asymmetric hold — a party with something to lose, blindsided by a rule change from a party with no obligation to warn them. Vine's story is closer to "platform never builds the infrastructure a comparable competitor already had, and creators migrate rationally to where the infrastructure exists." Those are different failures with different lessons, and treating Vine as a second data point for the first one manufactures evidence that does not exist. If a script needs a punchier version of "platforms killed my Vine career," the honest version is: *nobody* killed it, because nobody had built anything there to be killed. The audience left for a payment mechanism, not away from a penalty.

## The Lesson That Transfers

The transferable finding is not about algorithms; it is about the presence or absence of a revenue-sharing rail, and it generalises well past video platforms. A platform that aggregates a valuable audience but never builds a mechanism for the people who built that audience to capture value from it is exposed to defection the moment a competitor supplies one — not because the incumbent did anything actively hostile, but because it did nothing at all. This is the same logic that governs marketplace sellers choosing between platforms with different fee structures, or gig workers choosing between apps with different payout mechanics: the absence of a mechanism is itself a competitive decision, made by omission, and it is exactly as consequential as an active rule change.

For an FDE assessing any platform business built on aggregated third-party content or labour, the diagnostic question this case supplies is blunt and checkable: **does this platform have a working, funded mechanism for its most valuable contributors to earn in proportion to the value they create, and if not, why not yet?** An affirmative answer at a comparable competitor is a leading indicator of defection, well before any usage metric shows it — Vine's creators announced their reasons publicly, years before the shutdown, and the warning was ignored rather than hidden.
