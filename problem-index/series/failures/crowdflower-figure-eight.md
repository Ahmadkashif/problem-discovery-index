# Failure: CrowdFlower → Figure Eight (2007–2019)

**Lesson class:** thesis-died-company-lived
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-07-big-data|7 — Big Data]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/data-labeling-services|Data Labeling Services]] · [[industries/crowdsourcing-platforms|Crowdsourcing Platforms]]
**What was claimed:** A general-purpose crowdsourced-labour marketplace — capable of routing any short, priced, on-demand human task, from market-research surveys to catalogue cleanup to data annotation — was a horizontal platform business, not a vertical one, and horizontal reach was the moat.
**Capital at risk:** $58 million total venture capital raised across five disclosed rounds (2011–2017); acquired by Appen in March 2019 for $300 million — a return above invested capital, and not, on its own numbers, a failed venture.

## Why It Was Plausible

CrowdFlower was founded in December 2007 as Dolores Labs by Lukas Biewald and Chris Van Pelt, renamed and relaunched at TechCrunch50 in 2009, and built directly on the general-purpose crowd-labour infrastructure Amazon Mechanical Turk had proven viable since November 2005. This vault's `history/crowdsourcing-platforms.md` establishes that infrastructure's own plausibility case in detail: a continuous, priced, on-demand global labour market for short tasks, with quality control run on gold-standard questions and inter-annotator agreement, was a genuinely new capability with no precedent at that scale. CrowdFlower's specific bet — that a company built on top of this infrastructure, rather than Amazon's own platform, could serve enterprise customers who needed more workflow tooling, quality assurance and account management than Mechanical Turk's raw marketplace provided — attracted real, sophisticated enterprise partnerships across market research, catalogue management, and eventually machine-learning data: the company's client list came to include Autodesk, Google, Facebook, Twitter, Cisco, GitHub, Toyota and American Express, a roster that does not describe a company selling an unwanted product.

The horizontal thesis looked stronger, not weaker, as the 2010s progressed. **Fei-Fei Li's ImageNet project ran its image labelling on Amazon Mechanical Turk from July 2008 to April 2010**, drawing roughly 49,000 workers across 167 countries — proof that exactly the kind of general crowd-labour marketplace CrowdFlower had built a business around could be pointed at a specific, repeatable, high-value task (image annotation) at a scale no research lab could staff internally. CrowdFlower built its own business around precisely this shape before ImageNet's own labelling effort had even finished, positioning it to ride whichever vertical use case grew fastest — market research today, machine-learning training data tomorrow — without having to bet the company on which one would win.

## What Actually Killed It

The horizontal-platform thesis did not collapse; it was **overtaken by a narrower, purpose-built competitor entering later with a sharper focus.** Scale AI, founded in 2016 by Alexandr Wang and Lucy Guo through Y Combinator, built specifically for machine-learning training data from the outset rather than pivoting a general marketplace toward it. That focus let Scale ride the autonomous-vehicle annotation boom of the late 2010s and then the RLHF and reasoning-trace data boom of the 2020s more directly than a company still carrying market-research and catalogue-cleanup infrastructure and customer relationships could. CrowdFlower rebranded as **Figure Eight in 2017** — already a narrowing of positioning toward the machine-learning use case specifically — and was **acquired by Appen in March 2019 for $300 million**, folding the company's technology and workforce into a larger, longer-established data-and-linguistics business (Appen itself dates to 1996, a full decade before CrowdFlower, as a Sydney-based linguistics data company that pivoted into ML labelling as the market appeared around it).

Appen's own subsequent numbers describe an incumbent that did not sustain the position it absorbed Figure Eight to strengthen: revenue fell to US$273 million in 2023, headcount dropped to roughly 1,000 by 2024, and the company cycled through three CEOs in three years. Scale AI, by contrast, reached a $14 billion valuation in May 2024 on a round including Amazon and Meta — before Meta purchased a 49% non-voting stake in Scale AI for approximately $14.8 billion in June 2025, with founder Alexandr Wang moving directly to Meta and Jason Droege taking over as CEO. The category CrowdFlower had helped build was won, at each successive stage, by a company organised around a narrower and more specific version of the same idea.

## What It Was Not

**CrowdFlower's own outcome was not a financial failure, and this is the detail that keeps this case inside the "thesis died, company lived" class rather than a straightforward bankruptcy.** $58 million raised against a $300 million acquisition price is a return well above invested capital — early investors in CrowdFlower were not wiped out; they did reasonably well. What died was not the company's balance sheet but its horizontal-platform *idea*: that a single business, indifferent to which vertical use case eventually mattered most, could hold the category's centre of gravity by serving all of them adequately. The market's answer, delivered twice — once when Figure Eight was absorbed into the older, broader Appen, and again when the ML-native specialist that followed Figure Eight was itself substantially absorbed into its largest customer — is that this category rewards depth in one specific, high-value vertical over breadth across many, at least at the stage where the highest-value work (RLHF, reasoning traces, autonomous-vehicle annotation) demands purpose-built tooling and quality processes a general marketplace was never built to provide.

It is also not the case that the underlying measurement problem this vault's `history/data-labeling-services.md` documents — that the industry sells its most expensive product with its weakest quality signal, because consensus among annotators does not converge on truth for genuinely hard judgement calls — was solved by any of these transitions. Scale AI's ascent and Meta's absorption of it changed who runs the labour marketplace; it did not change the asymmetric hold `history/crowdsourcing-platforms.md` documents as a policy choice, not an engineering gap, nor did it produce a genuine replacement for consensus arithmetic at the expert tier. The company that won this round inherited the same unresolved problem CrowdFlower never solved either.

## The Lesson That Transfers

**A horizontal platform thesis is most vulnerable exactly when the market it serves discovers a single vertical use case valuable enough to fund a purpose-built competitor from scratch.** CrowdFlower's breadth across market research, catalogue work and early ML annotation was a genuine strength while no single use case dominated demand; once machine-learning training data became valuable enough on its own to justify a company built for nothing else, breadth stopped being an advantage and became, relative to a focused competitor, an unnecessary cost centre. An FDE evaluating a horizontal-platform business inside a category still finding its highest-value use case should ask which vertical, if it suddenly became dominant, would justify a purpose-built entrant — and should treat the horizontal incumbent's moat as contingent on no such vertical emerging, not as durable regardless of what happens next. CrowdFlower's absorption into Appen, and Appen's subsequent eclipse by Scale AI, is the same lesson delivered twice in twelve years.
