# Failure: Klarna's Revaluation (2021–2025)

**Lesson class:** thesis-died-company-lived
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-06-cloud-saas|6 — Cloud & SaaS]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/bnpl-providers|BNPL Providers]] · [[industries/payment-processors|Payment Processors]]
**What was claimed:** Klarna was not a consumer lender being priced like one; it was a hyper-growth checkout-infrastructure company, growing fast enough and diversifying far enough beyond credit risk to justify a software-multiple valuation rather than a bank's.
**Capital at risk:** roughly $39 billion of paper valuation evaporated between June 2021 ($46bn) and July 2022 ($6.7bn) — an ~85% fall inside thirteen months.

## Why It Was Plausible

By June 2021, when a SoftBank Vision Fund 2-led round priced Klarna at approximately $46 billion, the company was sixteen years into a genuine and well-documented technical achievement: real-time, checkout-embedded underwriting of a purchase for a stranger the merchant had never met, at a speed no branch-based instalment loan had ever approached. This was not vapourware. Klarna had operated in Sweden since 2005, expanded across Europe and into the US, and built an underwriting stack that used device signals, checkout behaviour and its own repayment history to score applicants who often had no usable bureau file at all — precisely the population traditional instalment credit could not reach economically. Pandemic-era e-commerce growth was real and sustained; a generation of BNPL-adjacent vendors (Plaid, MX, Sardine, Socure) had matured alongside it, making the underwriting layer cheaper to build than ever. Loss rates, moreover, were genuinely low: Federal Reserve research cited in industry retrospectives put BNPL account defaults at roughly 2% over 2019–2022, against roughly 10% for other consumer credit in the same window — a real, checkable number, not investor spin.

Against that backdrop, treating Klarna as a payments-infrastructure company rather than a subprime lender was not an obviously foolish read. 2021 was a year in which capital markets were pricing growth-stage fintech, broadly, at multiples that assumed durable hyper-growth continuing indefinitely — Klarna was an extreme instance of a market-wide mispricing, not an isolated one. A well-informed investor in June 2021 could point to real underwriting innovation, real loss discipline, and a real and growing merchant network, and conclude the multiple was aggressive but not absurd.

## What Actually Killed It

Two accounts compete here, and neither should be flattened into the other. The **market-repricing account** holds that 2022's broader collapse in growth-stock and fintech multiples — driven by rising interest rates making future cash flows worth less today — repriced every hyper-growth company simultaneously, and Klarna's 85% fall was simply this vault's `series/eras/`-documented macro shock landing hardest on the most extreme prior multiple. This account is well supported: Klarna itself attributed its July 2022 raise, at $800 million for a $6.7 billion valuation, to "the current environment," and paired it with layoffs of roughly 10% of its then-~7,000 staff, citing inflation and the war in Ukraine — external, macro causes, not an internal product failure.

The **thesis-specific account** holds something narrower and more durable: regardless of the macro trigger, Klarna was never actually a software company with software economics. It carries credit risk on its balance sheet, its revenue scales with gross merchandise volume and default rates rather than with seat licences, and its 2025 disclosed credit-loss provisions of 0.55% of GMV (Q1 2026) versus 0.54% (Q1 2025) describe a lending business managed the way lending businesses are managed — carefully, on thin margins, watched quarter to quarter — not a SaaS business compounding at 40% net revenue retention. On this account, 2022 did not manufacture a mispricing out of nothing; it corrected one that always existed, and would have been corrected eventually regardless of the specific macro trigger that happened to do it in 2022.

Both accounts can be true at once, and this file declines to pick between them: the timing was macro, but the magnitude — an 85% fall, larger than most fintech peers absorbed in the same window — reflects a valuation that had drifted furthest from what the underlying business actually was.

## What It Was Not

**This was not a bankruptcy, and it was not a fraud.** No customer lost a deposit, no regulator found deceptive lending, and no restatement of financials followed. Klarna kept operating, kept underwriting, kept growing its merchant network, and completed a US IPO on the NYSE on 10 September 2025 at $40 a share — a return to public capital markets that a company whose thesis had been fully discredited would not have been able to complete. The tidy story — "BNPL was subprime lending in disguise and Klarna's collapse proves it" — does not survive the loss data. A 2% default rate against roughly 10% for general consumer credit, sustained across 2019–2022, is not what a mispriced subprime book looks like; it looks like a genuinely differentiated underwriting product that was, independently and additionally, priced by growth-stage investors as though it were something else entirely. The correct target for scepticism is the 2021 valuation methodology, not the underwriting itself.

It is also not the case, per the vault's own `history/bnpl-providers.md`, that pay-in-four's regulatory position was ever settled. The CFPB's May 2024 interpretive rule classifying BNPL lenders as card issuers under Regulation Z remains contested — the Financial Technology Association sued to block it in October 2024, and enforcement priorities shifted again in 2025. Klarna's valuation crisis and BNPL's regulatory exposure are two separate, only loosely related risks that happened to sit inside the same company at overlapping times; this file keeps them apart rather than letting one stand in for the other.

## The Lesson That Transfers

**A company can be technically excellent at the thing it does and still be catastrophically mispriced for what investors believe it is.** Klarna's underwriting engineering was not the failure; the classification of the business — checkout infrastructure versus consumer lender — was. The tell was hiding in plain sight the whole time: a lender's revenue moves with default rates and merchant discount rates, not with the seat-expansion and net-revenue-retention metrics that justify a software multiple, and the two kinds of business are valued on entirely different bases for good reason. An FDE evaluating any fast-growing fintech should ask, independent of the growth rate: does this business carry credit, insurance, or inventory risk on its own balance sheet? If it does, its valuation ceiling is set by the economics of that risk, not by the elegance of the software wrapped around it — and the market, eventually, always finds this out, though rarely on a schedule anyone can predict. Klarna's own history is the clean version of this lesson because the company survived to prove the corrected valuation was closer to right than the peak one: the 2025 IPO priced a real, still-operating lender, not a corpse.
