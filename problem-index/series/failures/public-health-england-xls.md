# Failure: Public Health England's Dropped COVID Cases (October 2020)

**Lesson class:** the-measurement-was-wrong
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-03-pc-spreadsheet|3 — The PC & the Spreadsheet]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/healthcare-practice-software|Healthcare Practice Software]] · [[industries/bi-analytics-platforms|BI & Analytics Platforms]]
**What was claimed:** Public Health England's automated pipeline for ingesting daily positive-test results from commercial ("Pillar 2") laboratories into the national case-management system was a reliable, fully automated feed, processing every result it received through to contact tracing.
**Capital at risk:** not applicable — a government public-health data pipeline, not a funded venture.

## Why It Was Plausible

By October 2020, England's testing programme had scaled far beyond what it looked like even a few months earlier, and the pipeline moving results from disparate commercial lab IT systems into PHE's central case-management system had to absorb that growth without a redesign in the middle of a live pandemic response. Using Excel, including its legacy `.xls` format, as an interchange format between separate laboratory systems and a central public-health authority was not an exotic shortcut invented under pandemic pressure — spreadsheet-based data exchange between disconnected institutional systems was, and in many places still is, ordinary practice across the public sector, precisely because it requires no custom integration between systems that were never designed to talk to each other directly.

Nobody chose the specific 65,536-row ceiling that caused the failure; it is an artefact of a decades-old binary file format, `.xls`, that remained quietly supported and quietly still in use inside older reporting pipelines years after Excel's default interchange format had moved to `.xlsx`, with a limit more than fifteen times higher. A pipeline built when daily volumes were comfortably under that ceiling would have worked correctly, indefinitely, without anyone needing to think about the limit at all — which is exactly why nobody was monitoring for it. A silent truncation produces no crash, no error message, and no visibly missing chunk of a dashboard; it produces a number that is simply, quietly, too small, and a system that looks, from the outside, exactly like a system that is working.

## What Actually Killed It

On 2 October 2020, PHE discovered that a substantial batch of positive test results, dating back to 25 September, had never been transferred into the national case-management system or passed on to NHS Test and Trace. The cause was that the automated ingestion process used the legacy `.xls` format's hard cap of 65,536 rows per file; as incoming volume exceeded that cap, additional rows were silently dropped rather than triggering an error, a rollover to a new file, or any other form of alert. The vault's own figure for the consequence is precise and direct: 15,841 positive test results never reached contact tracers in time to act on them. Health Secretary Matt Hancock's public response — that the failure "should never have happened" — was widely reported at the time.

The harder number is contested rather than settled. A subsequent University of Warwick economic and epidemiological analysis estimated that the resulting delay in contact tracing may have contributed to more than 125,000 additional infections and roughly 1,500 additional deaths — but PHE publicly disputed those findings. Both figures belong in this file, and neither should be treated as more solid than it is: the 15,841 figure is a direct count of dropped rows, while the 125,000/1,500 figures are the output of an epidemiological model built on counterfactual assumptions about how those specific delayed contacts would otherwise have altered onward transmission — assumptions that cannot be directly observed and that PHE itself rejected.

## What It Was Not

It was not a collapse of PHE's entire COVID data infrastructure. This was one specific automated ingestion step, for one specific data stream — Pillar 2 commercial laboratory results feeding into the national case-management system — not a failure of testing capacity, genomic sequencing, hospital admissions reporting, or the wider public dashboard, all of which continued operating through the same period. It is also not a story about an obscure, unknowable technical limit: spreadsheet format ceilings are well documented and have been for decades. The actual failure was narrower and, in a sense, more mundane — nobody had audited whether the volume flowing through this particular pipeline had grown past a ceiling that had been perfectly safe when the process was first built, and no monitoring existed to catch a system quietly going from correct to wrong as its input scaled, with no code change and no visible error at any point in between.

The 125,000-infections, 1,500-deaths figure should not be repeated as an agreed fact; it is one modelling exercise's estimate, disputed by the agency it is used to indict, and it deserves the same caution this vault's marketing-attribution files apply to marketing-mix modelling more generally — a model's output is only as trustworthy as the counterfactual assumptions baked into its specification, which the reader of the headline number rarely sees argued. Nor is this a uniquely British institutional failure. The underlying mechanism — a legacy file-format ceiling silently truncating a growing automated feed that nobody re-monitors as volume scales — is a generic pattern, which is exactly why this vault's Wave 3 file groups it with Reinhart-Rogoff and the London Whale under the same failure class: what repeats across all three is the mechanism, not the domain.

## The Lesson That Transfers

A pipeline that has "been running fine" for years is not evidence that it has been checked against its *current* input volume — those are different claims that happen to produce identical-looking output, because a healthy pipeline and a silently truncating one both generate a dashboard that updates every day without complaint. The failure mode this case illustrates is specifically dangerous because it gives no warning signal of its own; the system does not degrade visibly, it simply stops being complete while continuing to look operational.

The transferable diagnostic for an FDE inheriting any legacy data pipeline feeding a governance-critical destination — billing, regulatory filings, compliance reporting, contact tracing — is not "does this still produce output" but "what happens, specifically, to the row, record or event immediately past whatever ceiling this format or protocol imposes: does it error loudly, does it roll over automatically, or does it simply vanish." A pipeline's continued operation is evidence about its uptime. It is not evidence about its completeness, and the two are routinely confused by everyone, including the people running it, until an external audit or a public failure forces the distinction.
