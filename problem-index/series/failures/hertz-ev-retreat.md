# Failure: Hertz's EV Retreat (2021–2024)

**Lesson class:** thesis-died-company-lived
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-08-mobile-gps|8 — Mobile & GPS]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/rideshare-fleet-operators|Rideshare Fleet Operators]] · [[industries/fleet-managers|Fleet Managers]]
**What was claimed:** A large rental fleet operator could electrify at scale by ordering tens of thousands of Tesla and other EVs, renting them to both retail customers and rideshare drivers, and profiting from lower fuel and maintenance costs while riding a wave of accelerating EV adoption.
**Capital at risk:** the October 2021 Tesla order (100,000 vehicles, predominantly Model 3s) — described at the time as the single largest EV purchase ever placed, with an undisclosed total dollar value — followed by further commitments of up to 65,000 Polestar vehicles (April 2022) and up to 175,000 GM EVs over five years (September 2022); roughly a third of the resulting EV fleet was put up for sale from January 2024.

## Why It Was Plausible

Hertz's Tesla order was placed in October 2021 by then-interim CEO Mark Fields, four months after the company had emerged from Chapter 11 bankruptcy (filed May 2020, citing the pandemic's collapse of travel demand; emerged July 2021; returned to Nasdaq trading in November 2021). Fields's stated reasoning — that Tesla was "the only manufacturer that can produce EVs at scale" — was not an eccentric read of the market in late 2021. Tesla had spent the prior several years proving out high-volume EV manufacturing in a way no other automaker had matched, EV adoption curves across most public forecasts were still pointing sharply upward, and a rental company able to offer a Tesla by the day, at scale, had an obvious marketing and press advantage over competitors still renting almost entirely petrol fleets. Hertz followed the Tesla order with an October 2022 arrangement to make up to 50,000 of the vehicles available specifically to Uber drivers — extending the bet from retail renters into the gig-economy driver population this vault's `history/rideshare-fleet-operators.md` describes as chronically unable to access a qualifying vehicle any other way. Two further manufacturer commitments, with Polestar (April 2022) and General Motors (September 2022, up to 175,000 vehicles), plus a memorandum of understanding with bp pulse for charging infrastructure, show this was not an isolated Tesla-specific gamble but a genuine, multi-manufacturer strategic bet on the rental category electrifying broadly and quickly.

A company emerging from bankruptcy with a fresh balance sheet, entering a growth category with genuine first-mover marketing value and a manufacturer that had already solved the production problem everyone else was struggling with, was a coherent turnaround strategy — not an obviously reckless one, and one several analysts credited at the time as a smart way for a recently-restructured company to differentiate itself from Avis and Enterprise.

## What Actually Killed It

By January 2024, Hertz announced it would sell roughly a third of its EV fleet through outlets including Auto Trader and EV.com, reinvesting the proceeds into petrol vehicles. CEO Stephen Scherr, who had by then replaced Fields, attributed the reversal to weak demand and high repair costs for the battery-powered vehicles specifically, not to EVs in general failing to find renters. Several distinct mechanisms compounded: **Tesla's own aggressive price cuts through 2023 forced Hertz into significant write-downs on the resale value of vehicles it had already purchased** — a rental fleet's economics depend on eventually reselling the asset at a predictable residual value, and Tesla repricing its own new-vehicle line undercut exactly that assumption for every used Tesla Hertz was carrying. Renters unfamiliar with EVs produced **higher accident rates** than the petrol fleet, raising repair frequency. Those repairs were also **more expensive**: Tesla parts and service were harder and costlier to source at rental-fleet volume than equivalent petrol-vehicle parts, and Tesla was, per Scherr's own account, reluctant to offer the kind of volume discounts a fleet buyer of this scale would normally expect. In February 2024, Hertz paused its Polestar purchase commitment as well, citing deteriorating EV resale values across the board, not a Tesla-specific problem.

## What It Was Not

**Hertz did not fail at electric vehicles generally; this was a fleet-economics retreat, not a verdict on EV viability, and the distinction matters.** Nobody went bankrupt over this decision — Hertz was already a large, operating rental business before the Tesla order and remained one afterward, selling roughly a third of an EV fleet inside a much larger overall vehicle portfolio. The company did not exit electrification entirely or repudiate EVs as a category; it characterised the move as a rebalancing toward a fleet mix its own economics could support, not an ideological reversal. This is a meaningfully different failure than, for instance, a startup betting its entire existence on EV-only operations and going under when the bet failed — Hertz absorbed the write-downs and kept operating.

The tidy wrong story — "Hertz proves rideshare drivers don't want EVs" — also does not survive the evidence Scherr himself gave. The stated causes were resale-value collapse (driven by Tesla's own pricing decisions, an external factor outside driver preference entirely), higher accident and repair costs among unfamiliar renters, and a parts-supply relationship that did not scale the way a fleet buyer needs it to. None of these is a statement about whether rideshare drivers, given a well-supported and well-priced EV, want to drive one — it is a statement about whether a specific fleet operator, buying at a specific moment from a specific set of manufacturers, could make the asset-residual-value arithmetic work.

## The Lesson That Transfers

**Renting a depreciating asset against a resale value you do not control, purchased from a manufacturer who can reprice the entire category downward at will, is an underwriting bet on someone else's pricing decisions — and it fails exactly when that other party moves the price, regardless of how sound the demand thesis otherwise is.** Hertz's core rental economics have always depended on predicting residual value accurately enough to buy today at a price that clears a profit after resale years later; EVs added a second, more volatile axis to that prediction, because the market-clearing price of a used EV is set substantially by what the same manufacturer charges for a new one, and Tesla changed that price repeatedly through 2023 for its own reasons entirely disconnected from Hertz's fleet plan. An FDE evaluating any capital-intensive rental or leasing business entering a category dominated by a small number of fast-moving manufacturers should ask, before modelling demand at all: who sets the resale value of this asset, and can that party reprice it out from under me without warning? Hertz's Tesla bet answered that question the hard way, in a business it otherwise understood extremely well.
