# Failure: NASDAQ's Odd-Eighth Quoting (1971–1998)

**Lesson class:** the-regulator-arrived
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-01-mainframe-batch|1 — Mainframe & Batch]] *(the practice this file examines runs across the full span this origin covers — see below)*
**Industries touched:** [[origins/exchanges-market-makers/profile|Exchanges & Market Makers]] · [[industries/robo-advisors|Robo-Advisors]] · [[industries/crypto-exchanges|Crypto Exchanges]]
**What was claimed:** NASDAQ dealer quotes clustering on even eighths of a dollar reflected natural trading convention in a genuinely competitive dealer market, not coordination to protect a wider-than-necessary spread.

## Why It Was Plausible

Quoting in round fractions was the market's inherited convention, and round-number quoting is a real, widely documented phenomenon in many markets absent any coordination — people and institutions gravitate to round prices for benign cognitive and operational reasons having nothing to do with collusion. No NASD or SEC rule required dealers to use odd eighths, and no single dealer's individual quote, examined alone, proved anything at all; the pattern was visible only in aggregate, across the whole market, over years — precisely the kind of finding no single customer, dealer or regulator looking at one firm at a time was positioned to see.

NASDAQ was, by regulatory design, a dealer market: multiple market makers competing to post two-sided quotes was the structure regulators themselves had built, on the theory that dealer competition alone would discipline spreads without needing a rule to dictate tick size. A wide, consistent spread could be — and was — defended as legitimate compensation for standing ready to buy or sell in any market condition, a genuine inventory-risk and adverse-selection cost that exists in any dealer market and that makes "the spread looks wide" alone insufficient evidence of wrongdoing. And the practice had run for roughly two decades inside a self-regulatory structure — NASD oversight, with SEC supervision above it — that had never previously flagged it. From inside the industry, that silence looked like tacit acceptance of the status quo, not an oversight quietly waiting to be corrected.

## What Actually Killed It

An academic study, not a regulator, started the sequence. William Christie and Paul Schultz published findings in 1994 showing that avoidance of odd-eighth quotes was too consistent across dealers in active stocks to be coincidental, and that the pattern was consistent with implicit coordination around a minimum $0.25 spread rather than the $0.125 a genuinely competitive market in eighths would allow. The story broke in national newspapers on 26–27 May 1994. What happened next is the single most important fact in this file: the day after the story ran, dealers in stocks including Amgen, Cisco and Microsoft sharply increased their use of odd-eighth quotes, and measured spreads fell by close to half — overnight, with no new technology and no new rule. That is direct, dated evidence that the spread had been a choice available to reverse immediately, not a technical constraint anyone needed time to engineer around.

Only after that public, voluntary correction did the formal apparatus arrive. A Department of Justice antitrust investigation produced a 1996 settlement requiring practice changes and, at many firms, the taping of trader phone calls. Separate private antitrust litigation produced a $1.03 billion class-action settlement, approved in November 1998, covering 37 brokerage firms — at the time the largest civil antitrust settlement in US history. The SEC's Order Handling Rules followed in 1997, requiring market makers to display customer limit orders priced better than their own quote, or any better price they had placed on an ECN, in the public montage — the regulatory opening that let Island ECN and others compete with NASDAQ dealers on equal visibility. Every one of these mechanisms documented, penalised and structurally locked in a change the market had already made on its own, days earlier, once the finding became public and undeniable.

## What It Was Not

Regulation NMS, adopted by the SEC on 9 June 2005 and fully compliant by 5 February 2007, is routinely blamed for causing the rise of high-frequency trading that followed it — that overstates what the rule does, and it is a full decade removed from this file's core event regardless. Rule 611 bars a venue from executing at a price worse than a protected quote displayed elsewhere; it does not mandate routing an order to whichever venue has the best price. It created an incentive to see and react to every protected quote fast enough to avoid a trade-through violation, and that incentive — not a routing mandate — is what made speed newly valuable across every linked venue at once. Reg NMS does not belong in this file's causal chain as the mechanism that fixed odd-eighth quoting; it postdates the fix by a decade and answers a different question entirely.

Nor did the SEC catch the collusion — two academic economists analysing public quote data did, and the market's own week-one behavioural response confirmed the finding before any enforcement body acted on it. And the DOJ settlement, the private litigation and the 1997 rules did not themselves close the spreads: they arrived after spreads had already fallen by roughly half under public and reputational pressure alone, which means they ratified a change the market had made rather than caused it. Finally, this is not proof that markets reliably self-correct without regulation — the same practice had run undisturbed for roughly two decades of NASDAQ operating as a dealer-quoted system before 1994, meaning nothing internal to the market produced the correction on its own; it took one external, credible, unambiguous public finding to trigger what the market could apparently have done at any point in the preceding twenty years.

## The Lesson That Transfers

This file's constraint was never technical, and — in the moment that actually mattered — it was not primarily regulatory either. It was informational and reputational, and it broke in a single news cycle once made public and impossible to plausibly deny. That is a genuinely different failure shape from the others in this batch: Done Global needed a federal prosecution and a jury; Wirecard needed its own auditor to finally refuse to sign; Small Smiles needed a hidden camera and years of litigation; IAB Europe needed a formal data-protection-authority decision. NASDAQ dealers needed one rigorous, publicly reported, statistically unambiguous measurement, and the market corrected itself overnight, before any formal mechanism had moved at all.

An FDE should take from this that the highest-leverage intervention against an entrenched, purely conventional industry practice is sometimes not a new rule, a new system, or a new technology — it is a sufficiently rigorous and sufficiently public measurement that removes every participant's ability to plausibly deny what the aggregate data already shows. The hard work of building enforcement infrastructure afterwards can matter far less than being first to make the underlying pattern undeniable.
