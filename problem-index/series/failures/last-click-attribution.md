# Failure: Last-Click Attribution (1996–present)

**Lesson class:** the-measurement-was-wrong
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-05-commercial-web|5 — The Commercial Web]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/programmatic-ad-platforms|Programmatic Ad Platforms]] · [[industries/affiliate-networks|Affiliate Networks]] · [[industries/marketing-attribution-vendors|Marketing Attribution Vendors]] · [[industries/retail-media-networks|Retail Media Networks]]
**What was claimed:** Crediting 100% of a conversion to the single most recent ad, link or click a customer touched before buying is a fair, industry-standard measure of "what worked" — solid enough to base agency billing, campaign optimisation, affiliate commission and real-time bidding algorithms on.
**Capital at risk:** not applicable — a measurement convention embedded across an industry's tooling, not a funded venture.

## Why It Was Plausible

In 1996, the technology available to measure an ad's effect could do exactly one thing reliably: a cookie — an invention barely two years old, first built by Netscape's Lou Montulli to solve a shopping-cart statelessness problem — could persist in a browser and let a server recognise the same visitor returning. DoubleClick, founded in February 1996, built its ad-serving business around that capability. What a cookie could not do, at any price, was store a visitor's entire history of exposures across publishers and sessions and join it back to a later purchase at internet scale. The storage, the cross-site identity infrastructure, and the compute to run that join in real time did not exist, and would not exist in a practical form for roughly two more decades.

Given that constraint, the choice on the table was not "a better attribution method versus a worse one." It was an instant, free, individual-level answer to which ad a converting visitor had most recently touched, versus no individual-level answer at all — only the aggregate lift studies and marketing-mix modelling CPG advertisers had run for years, which took weeks, cost real money, and could only say whether a campaign worked in general, never which exposure worked for which customer. Last-click was the only individual-level option the infrastructure could produce, and every party hit the identical constraint independently: Amazon Associates, launched in July 1996, adopted the same last-touch logic at the affiliate layer the same year, not by copying DoubleClick but because a cookie imposes the same ceiling on whoever holds it. Building on what could be measured cheaply and immediately, rather than waiting for a measurement science that did not yet exist, was the rational engineering decision available to publishers, advertisers and agencies alike. Nobody was weighing last-click against a worked-out multi-touch alternative and picking the cheaper option out of laziness. The honest alternative had not been invented yet.

## What Actually Killed It

Nothing has fully killed last-click even now; it survives as the default in dashboards, agency billing, and a meaningful share of bidding logic decades after the constraint that produced it began to lift. What changed is the belief that it was correct, and that erosion happened in layers. Agency practice through the late 1990s entrenched it in billing because it was already the number every tool could produce; after Google acquired DoubleClick in 2008, Google Analytics and AdWords defaulted to it for simplicity and low compute — an inherited constraint, popularised at scale, not a fresh decision about fair credit. Multi-touch vendors emerged to challenge the convention through the 2010s, and marketing-mix modelling made a documented comeback as cross-site cookie identity eroded, because MMM never depended on individual-level tracking. Incrementality testing — geo holdouts, randomised experiments — is the one method that actually establishes causation, and remains a supplement rather than a foundation because it is slow and expensive next to the instant number last-click still supplies free.

Two forcing events turned an abstract complaint into something the public could see. Apple's App Tracking Transparency (iOS 14.5, 26 April 2021) made cross-app tracking opt-in and broke the deterministic identity graph last-click had depended on, forcing a shift toward "modelled conversions" — estimates the measured platform makes about its own performance. Then, on 23 December 2024, an investigation by the YouTuber MegaLag alleged Honey, PayPal's browser extension, was silently overwriting other publishers' affiliate links at checkout, crediting itself regardless of whether it had contributed anything a shopper could not have gotten without it. PayPal's response to USA Today was not a denial: it stated that "Honey follows industry rules and practices, including last-click attribution" — confirmation, from the accused party, that the mechanism worked exactly as the industry's own convention says it should. Honey lost several million users within months, and Google amended Chrome Web Store policy in March 2025 to bar extensions from claiming affiliate commission without providing an actual discount. What these events killed was not the convention, which persists, but the ability to describe it in public as a neutral default rather than a rule a browser extension could, and did, turn into a weapon.

## What It Was Not

It was not "Google decided last-click attribution." This vault's own spine research has already flagged and killed that claim, worth restating inside the file about the industry the decision supposedly happened in: DoubleClick's cookie tooling predates Google's acquisition of DoubleClick by twelve years and predates Google's own founding by two. The mechanism was set by what was technically buildable in 1996, entrenched through agency practice and Amazon Associates' identical, independent adoption the same year, and only later scaled and popularised by Google after it acquired the company that built the tooling. Google defaulted to last-click for simplicity and low compute — inherited constraints, not an original judgement about what fair credit should mean.

Nor did a committee at a trade body sit down and author the current attribution rulebook on a fixed timetable. This file deliberately omits two claims that appear, unverified, in four other Phase 3 files: a quotation attributed to Greg Stuart, a former IAB president, calling last-click "a big mistake," and specific years — 2004 for impression guidelines, 2009 for click guidelines — for when the IAB codified click-based measurement. Both trace to a single trade-press page that returned HTTP 403 when re-fetched this session, and to no other locatable source; a Wikipedia article for Greg Stuart returned no result; IAB.com's insights listing and IAB Europe's site search, both fetched this session, named neither claim; WebSearch was unavailable, its budget exhausted. Absent a primary source — an IAB publication, a recorded interview, an archived version of the original page — both claims are unverified, and this file does not repeat them. What survives without them, and without any loss to the argument: the IAB, founded in 1996, is a trade body that has historically formalised existing practice into guidance rather than inventing new practice, so even confirmed dates would describe the IAB documenting a convention agency practice had already set, not originating it.

Finally, last-click is not uniquely a problem of the open-web auction, fine everywhere else. It recurs, materially worse, inside retail media, where — this vault's own history of that industry states plainly — "every fact needed to compute the honest number is already sitting in one company's own database." A retailer that shows the ad and rings the sale in the same system grades its own advertising with no external check — worse than the open-web case because the join between exposure and outcome is no longer technically missing there. It is simply not published.

## The Lesson That Transfers

A measurement convention adopted because it was what the available tooling could observe, cheaply, in real time, does not stay confined to the use case it was built for. It gets written into billing systems, then bidding algorithms, then a client's own KPI targets, and every layer built on top raises the cost of replacing it, independent of whether anyone still believes it is correct. This is the distinction this vault's own research draws between a missing join and a declined join: in 1996 the join between an ad exposure and its outcome was genuinely missing, because the technology to construct it did not exist. Inside a modern retail-media platform, that join is no longer missing — the retailer holds every fact required to compute the honest number — and the convention persists anyway, because unwinding decades of institutional dependence on a wrong number is a political cost now, not a technical one.

The diagnostic this case supplies an FDE evaluating a client's defence of an "industry-standard" measurement is historical rather than statistical: ask what tool was available when the convention was set, not whether it is defensible today. If the honest answer is "it's what we could measure in 1996, not what anyone believed was correct," the client already knows the number is wrong. The opportunity there is rarely a better model — better models for this exact problem have existed for a decade and have not displaced the convention. It is whatever lets a party profit from publishing the honest number without being the one forced to absorb the resulting invoice shrinkage.
