# Failure: Grubhub's Round Trip (2021–2025)

**Lesson class:** thesis-died-company-lived
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-08-mobile-gps|8 — Mobile & GPS]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/restaurant-tech-platforms|Restaurant Tech Platforms]] · [[industries/gig-delivery-platforms|Gig Delivery Platforms]]
**What was claimed:** Food-delivery marketplaces would consolidate into a small number of durable, globally-scaled platforms commanding lasting pricing power over both restaurants and consumers — and a $7.3 billion acquisition of a leading US player was the price of entry into that consolidation.
**Capital at risk:** $7.3 billion paid by Just Eat Takeaway in June 2021; sold for $650 million in January 2025 — a loss of more than $6.5 billion, roughly 91% of the purchase price, on a business that never stopped operating.

## Why It Was Plausible

Grubhub was not a speculative startup when Just Eat Takeaway bought it. Founded in Chicago in 2004 by Mike Evans and Matt Maloney as a digital alternative to the paper takeaway menu, it had already merged with Seamless in 2013, gone public on the NYSE in April 2014 at $26 a share, and by 2019 counted roughly 19.9 million active users across 115,000 restaurants in 3,200 cities — a real, cash-generating marketplace with more than a decade of operating history. The 2020–21 pandemic then produced the single largest demand shock the food-delivery category had ever seen: dine-in rooms closed, restaurants that had never taken an online order were suddenly dependent on one, and every major delivery platform posted growth rates that looked, from the outside, structural rather than cyclical.

Against that backdrop, a global consolidator paying a premium to acquire scale in the world's largest delivery market was a coherent bet, not an obviously reckless one. Just Eat Takeaway itself was the product of an earlier consolidation — Just Eat and Takeaway.com had merged in 2020 — and its thesis was that the category would resolve the way food delivery's fixed-cost, network-density economics suggested it should: into a small number of national or global champions extracting durable margin from both sides of the transaction, the way this vault's `history/restaurant-tech-platforms.md` describes marketplace commissions as a rate no individual restaurant sets or negotiates. Paying $7.3 billion in stock, announced June 2020 and completed June 2021, for the number-three US player at the height of pandemic-driven order volume was a bet that the category's economics — not just its momentary growth rate — justified the multiple.

## What Actually Killed It

The pandemic demand spike normalised. As dine-in reopened through 2021–22, delivery order growth slowed sharply across the category, and Grubhub's US market position — never first, consistently behind DoorDash and Uber Eats — did not improve under Just Eat Takeaway's ownership. This is the *demand-normalisation account*: the thesis assumed 2020–21 order volumes, or something close to them, would persist as a new baseline, and they did not.

A second, competing account is structural rather than cyclical: **the commission economics that make delivery marketplaces valuable to their owners are the same economics that make them precarious for the restaurants they depend on.** Litigation brought against the major platforms — the 2020 antitrust suit *Davitashvili v. Grubhub Inc.*, naming DoorDash and Uber Eats alongside Grubhub — alleged commissions of 13% to 40% of order revenue, against a restaurant industry running average margins of 3% to 9%. A marketplace extracting that much from a merchant base with that little room cannot raise take rates indefinitely without pushing restaurants toward direct ordering, third-party aggregators, or exit altogether — which caps the pricing power the consolidation thesis required indefinitely, not just during a demand trough. Both accounts likely operated together: a shrinking order base made an already-thin merchant relationship less tolerant of the platform's take rate, at the same moment competitors with deeper capital reserves (DoorDash, backed by continued US market leadership; Uber Eats, cross-subsidised by ride-hailing) could out-invest Grubhub on both marketing and driver supply.

## What It Was Not

**Grubhub did not fail because food delivery technology failed, and it is not a bankruptcy.** The dispatch, routing and menu-ordering technology this vault's `history/restaurant-tech-platforms.md` describes — the join between a POS system, several delivery marketplaces, a website and a kiosk — continued working exactly as designed throughout. Grubhub kept processing orders, kept paying couriers, and was still a functioning national marketplace at the moment of its January 2025 sale to Wonder Group for $650 million. What died was the acquisition's specific consolidation thesis: that owning Grubhub in 2021, at that price, bought durable category leadership. It did not buy leadership — Grubhub never overtook DoorDash or Uber Eats under JET's ownership — and the $7.3 billion price already assumed it eventually would.

Nor is this a story of one company's mismanagement in isolation. It is the same shape of failure this vault records for Klarna's 2021–22 valuation collapse in `series/failures/klarna-revaluation.md`: a capital allocator paid a 2021-vintage price for a business whose underlying fundamentals had not changed nearly as much as the price implied they had. Grubhub the company survived the round trip; the $6.5 billion difference between what JET paid and what it recovered is the size of the mistake made about what Grubhub, at that moment, actually was.

## The Lesson That Transfers

**A marketplace's most attractive quality to a strategic acquirer — its commission take rate — is also the ceiling on how much pricing power it can safely extract, because the take rate is bounded by the thinnest margin on the other side of the transaction.** Grubhub's technology stack owned the transaction and the interface completely; it never owned, and structurally could not own, the restaurant margin its commission was drawn from. An FDE evaluating a two-sided marketplace acquisition should treat the counterparty's own margin structure — not the acquirer's growth model — as the actual ceiling on the deal's terminal value, and should be specifically wary of any valuation built on a demand baseline set during an unrepeatable external shock. The $7.3 billion price assumed 2020–21 order volumes were the new normal for a category whose merchants were, the whole time, absorbing a commission close to the edge of what their own margins could bear.
