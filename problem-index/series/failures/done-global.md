# Failure: Done Global (2019–2025)

**Lesson class:** the-regulator-arrived
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-11-covid-dislocation|11 — The COVID Dislocation]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/telehealth-platforms|Telehealth Platforms]] · [[industries/medical-billing|Medical Billing]]
**What was claimed:** A telehealth platform could responsibly diagnose adult ADHD and prescribe controlled stimulants entirely by video, because the DEA's own pandemic order had suspended the requirement for an in-person exam first.

## Why It Was Plausible

Nothing about the underlying regulatory mechanism was invented or stretched by the company. In roughly two weeks in March 2020, the DEA suspended the Ryan Haight Act's in-person-exam requirement under its public-health-emergency authority, alongside CMS's Section 1135 waiver and OCR's HIPAA enforcement discretion — a cluster of federal actions, not a company's own interpretation of a grey area. That suspension has never lapsed and has never been made permanent either; it has been extended four times and currently runs through 31 December 2026. A founder building on it in 2019–2020 was building on a real, standing federal exemption, issued by the agency with authority to issue it.

The underlying clinical problem was real too. Adult ADHD is broadly under-diagnosed and under-treated, in-person psychiatric access collapsed during the pandemic, and Teladoc had already spent fifteen years proving that video-call medicine worked operationally at scale — it simply had never been allowed to serve this population at this volume. Done Global's founder, Ruthia He, came from a product-design background, not medicine, which fits a legitimate pattern in digital health: a UX-first team building distribution for a chronically undersupplied clinical service. Cerebral, founded the same year by different people with the same demand thesis, was not obviously distinguishable from Done at the outset, and treating the entire category as suspect from day one would have meant treating a wide swath of pandemic-era telehealth as suspect.

Market-level signals reinforced the read. For roughly two years, major payment processors, advertising platforms and pharmacies serviced these companies without public objection — a distributed vote of confidence that a single regulator's silence could not have provided alone.

## What Actually Killed It

The sequence matters more than any single event, and the actors arrived in a specific order — market gatekeepers before the state. CVS and Walmart, independently, stopped filling Done and Cerebral prescriptions in early 2022, a pharmacy-level judgement call made before any prosecutor had filed anything. Google and TikTok banned advertising from these platforms by mid-2024, a private platform-policy decision, also ahead of trial. Only then did the formal apparatus complete its work: the DOJ charged CEO Ruthia He, Clinical President David Brody and four others in June 2024, alleging distribution of more than 40 million Adderall pills for over $100 million in revenue. On 18 November 2025, a federal jury convicted He and Brody of illegally distributing controlled substances and conspiring to commit healthcare fraud, with He also convicted of conspiring to obstruct justice. Both face up to twenty years.

That is an established criminal conviction, not an allegation and not a settlement. It sits next to a materially different outcome for Cerebral, which halted most controlled-substance prescribing after a 2022 DOJ investigation and, in November 2024, agreed to pay $3.65 million to resolve it — a civil resolution, with no admission of the criminal conduct alleged against Done's leadership. Same regulatory environment, same suspended rule, two different outcomes. That difference is itself evidence against treating this as one undifferentiated telehealth-ADHD story.

## What It Was Not

This is not evidence that COVID permanently changed telehealth's regulatory boundary, and it should not be scripted that way. This vault's own history of the industry documents that thesis failing on its own terms: HIPAA enforcement discretion expired in August 2023, interstate licensure waivers mostly expired by May 2023, Medicare payment parity survives only on repeated short-term congressional patches, and the DEA's prescribing flexibility — the exact rule Done Global is convicted of exploiting — has never lapsed and never been made permanent. Utilisation settled around 13–17% of visits, roughly 55% below the April 2020 peak. Done Global was prosecuted for conduct inside a suspension that, at time of writing, is still neither closed nor law.

Nor is this proof that telehealth prescribing of controlled substances is inherently a pill mill. Teladoc's pre-pandemic model, built for a world where the old rules held, continued operating unaffected. The distinction the prosecution ultimately drew was about volume and encounter design — a fixed, brief consultation engineered to end in a prescription at industrial scale — not about the modality of care itself. And it is not a story about a hidden crime uncovered by a vigilant regulator: pharmacies and advertising platforms moved first, on their own commercial judgement, well before the DOJ's case reached a jury.

## The Lesson That Transfers

A temporary regulatory suspension is not the same instrument as a new law, and a business plan whose entire viability depends on that suspension persisting is a bet on an administrative and political process that nobody at the company controls and that carries its own expiry risk, however repeatedly renewed. The diagnostic question an FDE should ask before anything else is whether the revenue model survives the reversion of the suspended rule to its pre-emergency form — Teladoc's did; Done Global's did not, because the suspension was not incidental to the business, it was the business.

The order of arrival matters as much as the outcome. Industry counterparties with no regulatory mandate at all — a pharmacy chain, an ad platform — moved against the volume pattern roughly two years before the criminal justice system produced a verdict. They were positioned to see the same signal (implausible per-visit throughput at population scale) that took a federal investigation years to convert into a courtroom finding. An FDE evaluating a business built on an emergency waiver should model the base case as the waiver's reversion, not its indefinite extension, and should watch what the ecosystem's non-regulatory gatekeepers are quietly doing about volume — they often notice before anyone with subpoena power does.
