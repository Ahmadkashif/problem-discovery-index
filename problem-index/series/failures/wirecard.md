# Failure: Wirecard (1999–2020)

**Lesson class:** the-regulator-arrived
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-06-cloud-saas|6 — Cloud & SaaS]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/payment-processors|Payment Processors]] · [[industries/payment-fraud-vendors|Payment Fraud Vendors]]
**What was claimed:** Wirecard was a genuinely fast-growing, professionally audited global digital-payments company, and the profitable Asian third-party-acquiring business at the centre of years of press allegations was real.
**Capital at risk:** €1.9 billion in funds Wirecard reported holding in Philippine escrow accounts, which turned out not to exist; the stock lost 72% of its value in the two trading days following the 18 June 2020 disclosure.

## Why It Was Plausible

Wirecard was audited annually for over a decade by EY, a Big Four firm, without a qualified opinion on the figures now known to be fraudulent. In September 2018 it was promoted into the DAX, Germany's blue-chip thirty-company index — a step that, on its face, should have invited more institutional scrutiny, not less, since index inclusion pulls in passive-fund ownership and additional sell-side coverage. Its reported growth also had real comparables: this was the era this vault's own spine records as Wave 6, cloud and SaaS-adjacent fintech scaling quickly and legitimately across many companies, so implausible-looking growth curves were not, on their own, disqualifying.

The most consequential reason it was plausible is institutional, not commercial. Germany's own financial regulator, BaFin, spent years treating the sceptics as the problem. After the Financial Times published its first detailed allegations from 2015, and after a 2016 short-seller report, BaFin banned short selling of Wirecard shares from 18 February to 18 April 2019, and Bavarian and Munich prosecutors opened criminal investigations into short-sellers and, separately, into FT journalist Dan McCrum, for suspected market manipulation and securities-law violations respectively. For an institutional investor watching from outside, a national securities regulator actively investigating a company's critics — rather than the company — was a strong, entirely rational signal that the criticism, not Wirecard, was the outlier. And Wirecard's European business was not fictional: it was the real operating company Santander bought out of the insolvency for €100 million months later. A genuine business anchored the inflated one, which is exactly what made the inflated part easy to wave off as a rounding dispute over one regional segment.

## What Actually Killed It

The FT's reporting, running from 2015, documented specific accounting irregularities for years against the company's aggressive denials and legal threats, without on its own forcing a resolution. The proximate mechanism was narrower and more mundane: under mounting pressure, Wirecard commissioned a special audit from KPMG in late 2019. In March 2020 Wirecard publicly claimed KPMG had found "no discrepancy"; on 28 April 2020 KPMG stated it had not received sufficient documentation to address several of the allegations at all — a materially different, unresolved finding that Wirecard's own spin had misrepresented, and shares fell 26% on the correction. Weeks later, EY — the long-standing statutory auditor, not the special auditor — declined to sign off on the 2019 annual accounts and sought direct confirmation of the escrow funds from the two Philippine banks named as holding them. On 18 June 2020, Wirecard disclosed that the €1.9 billion probably did not exist; the named banks, BDO Unibank and BPI, confirmed they held no such funds. CEO Markus Braun resigned 19 June and was arrested 22 June. The company filed for insolvency on 25 June 2020. All of this is established — the company's own disclosure, the banks' confirmation, the insolvency filing — not alleged.

## What It Was Not

This was not a competitive failure in the People Express or Amazon-versus-Barnes-and-Noble sense: nobody out-executed or out-computed Wirecard, because the profit that mattered did not need to be won against a rival — it needed to be invented, and inventing it required no competitor at all. Nor is "the audit eventually worked, just late" an honest reading. EY's unqualified opinions across the preceding decade, delivered against publicly reported red flags since 2015, are a serious, unresolved failure of statutory audit in their own right, not evidence the system functioned with an acceptable lag. And BaFin's failure was not simple inaction — it took the affirmative step of banning short-selling and referring the reporters and sceptics for criminal investigation, a materially more culpable posture than a regulator who merely failed to look. The German parliament announced a full inquiry into the supervisory failure in September 2020, which is itself the tell that this was recognised, after the fact, as an active regulatory misdirection rather than a passive gap. Finally, this should not be read backward into payment-processor audit risk generally: this vault's own industry history frames Wirecard as the payments industry's instance of a declined join — a reconciliation nobody built, in an industry where the technical capability to check a reported cash position against reality has existed since Wave 1 — and that framing is specific to this case, not a general indictment of the sector's accounting.

## The Lesson That Transfers

When the institution whose job is to be sceptical — a national securities regulator — instead acts to suppress the sceptics, that reversal is a stronger and more legible signal than the underlying allegations themselves, and it should be weighted as a red flag in its own right rather than as noise around the "real" story. A Big Four signature is evidence of a process having been followed, not proof of an underlying fact, particularly for reported cash and receivables domiciled in a jurisdiction the auditor does not independently control and must instead take a counterparty's word for. And profit that has never been stress-tested by an actual competitive fight — nobody had to beat Wirecard at anything to make its numbers look strong, because there was no fight generating the number — deserves the same scrutiny that dentistry's documented absence of a competitive fight deserves for the opposite reason: ask what mechanism is actually producing the figure in front of you, because sometimes, as here, the honest answer is that nothing was.
