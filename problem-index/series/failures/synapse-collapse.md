# Failure: Synapse Financial Technologies (2014–2024)

**Lesson class:** concentration-became-systemic
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-06-cloud-saas|6 — Cloud & SaaS]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/neobanks|Neobanks]] · [[industries/bnpl-providers|BNPL Providers]] · [[industries/embedded-finance-platforms|Embedded Finance Platforms]]
**What was claimed:** One shared ledger and banking-as-a-service middleware layer could let more than a hundred fintech apps offer FDIC-insured banking to an estimated ten million end customers, without any of those apps ever becoming a chartered bank themselves.
**Capital at risk:** $51 million raised from investors, including Andreessen Horowitz

## Why It Was Plausible

Founded 14 April 2014 by Sankaet Pathak and Bryan Keltner, Synapse solved a real coordination problem the same way this vault has already seen solved elsewhere: a small number of chartered banks, and a much larger number of fintechs that want to offer banking-shaped products, cannot each build a bespoke integration with every counterpart. A shared middleware ledger — one system of record that a fintech's app writes to and a partner bank's compliance team can, in principle, read from — collapses that many-to-many integration problem into something tractable, the same logic that produces clearinghouses in medical billing and card networks in payments. [[series/eras/wave-06-cloud-saas|Wave 6]]'s general mechanism made this cheap to build: cloud infrastructure and a stack of specialised vendors for identity, fraud, and card issuing let a company assemble the operational appearance of a bank without owning a charter, which no software shift has ever made cheap on its own.

The sponsor-bank model this middleware served is not a Synapse invention and is not disreputable on its face — this vault's own history file for neobanks documents Chime, Dave, Cash App and others openly built on the identical structure, still operating without incident. A shared ledger connecting a hundred fintechs to a handful of banks was, on the same logic that makes any clearinghouse attractive, cheaper and more standardised than each of those hundred fintechs negotiating its own bespoke reconciliation process with each bank. Raising $51 million from investors including a16z to build that layer was a bet on a real efficiency gain, not a speculative story with no operating logic behind it.

## What Actually Killed It

Synapse filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in **April 2024**. What followed was not a bank failure in the conventional sense — no bank on either side of the relationship was declared insolvent by a banking regulator. It was a reconciliation failure: former FDIC Chair Jelena McWilliams, appointed bankruptcy trustee, reported in May 2024 an estimated **$65–96 million gap** between Synapse's own ledger and the records kept by its partner banks, meaning nobody could establish with confidence which of Synapse's ledger entries corresponded to money actually held at a bank. Evolve Bank & Trust, one of Synapse's partner banks, froze customer withdrawals for roughly five months while reconciliation was attempted. Data from a single downstream fintech, Yotta, illustrates the scale of what remained unresolved: by November 2024, 13,725 of its customers had received only $11.8 million in refunds against $64.9 million in deposits. Class-action lawsuits against the partner banks were still active as of the most recent reporting available for this file, and this vault cannot verify a full resolution or final accounting as of the date of writing.

It matters here, more than in the other files in this batch, to be precise about what is established fact and what is not. **No criminal charges against Synapse or its founders have been documented in the sources checked for this file.** The trustee's finding is a reported ledger discrepancy of a specific size, not a court finding of fraud or theft; the class actions target the partner banks over fund mismanagement, and those claims remain allegations, not adjudicated findings, as of the most recent public reporting this file could verify. Reporting elsewhere has attributed part of the trigger to a commercial dispute between Synapse and Evolve and a subsequent, contested migration of ledger data to a different partner bank in 2024 — this file could not verify that account against a primary source and flags it as unconfirmed rather than asserting it.

Separately, and concurrently, the Federal Reserve issued a formal enforcement action against Evolve Bank & Trust in June 2024, citing deficiencies in anti-money-laundering programmes, risk management, and — specifically — failure to manage fintech-partnership risk. This vault's own history file for neobanks documents that action and a parallel FDIC cease-and-desist order against Cross River Bank in April 2023; both are cited here as this vault's own prior findings, not independently re-verified in this file.

## What It Was Not

The comfortable version of this story is "a bank failed and depositors lost their FDIC insurance." That did not happen, and the distinction is the entire lesson. No bank in this arrangement was closed by its regulator, and no FDIC insurance claim was triggered, because the underlying banks did not fail — deposits were genuinely held, in aggregate, in real FDIC-member institutions. What failed was the middleware layer's own bookkeeping: Synapse, not a bank and not itself FDIC-insured, was the party whose ledger was supposed to say which of those aggregated dollars belonged to which end customer, and when Synapse went bankrupt, that ledger's authority went bankrupt with it. Consumers who believed their money was "in a bank" were not wrong about where the dollars physically sat; they were exposed to a layer they had never heard of and could not audit, which sat between them and that bank the entire time. It is also not established that Synapse's founders committed fraud — the record to date, checked for this file, supports a documented reconciliation failure of a specific and serious size, and unresolved litigation against the banks, not a proven criminal act. Treating this as a settled fraud case overstates what has actually been established, in either direction.

## The Lesson That Transfers

The party that chose the concentration was Synapse's founders and investors, betting on middleware as a real efficiency layer, and the fintechs that built consumer products on top of it without building or owning the ledger themselves. The party that bore the loss was ordinary end customers of apps like Yotta — many of whom had never heard the word "Synapse" and had no reason to know a third company, invisible to them, sat between their banking app and their money. Evolve, the bank left holding a five-month freeze and a separate Fed enforcement action, arguably bore a disproportionate share of the operational fallout for a dispute over whose ledger was correct. The transferable question for an FDE evaluating any "banking, but digital-first" product is not only who holds the charter — this vault's own neobanks history file already asks that question — but who owns the ledger of record between the charter and the app, whether that ledger owner is itself audited or insured in any way, and what happens to every customer's claim on their own money if that specific company, rather than the bank, is the one that goes bankrupt.
