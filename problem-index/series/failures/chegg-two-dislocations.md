# Failure: Chegg's Two Dislocations (2020, 2023)

**Lesson class:** thesis-died-company-lived
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-11-covid-dislocation|11 — The COVID Dislocation]] (2020 demand shock) and [[series/eras/wave-12-transformers|12 — Transformers]] (2023 supply-side shock) — deliberately two waves, three years apart, and this file's entire purpose is to keep them separate.
**Industries touched:** [[industries/online-tutoring-platforms|Online Tutoring Platforms]] · [[industries/online-course-platforms|Online Course Platforms]]
**What was claimed:** Chegg's homework-help "answer lookup" subscription business — students paying to see a worked solution to a specific assigned problem — was a durable, structurally-moated education product, not merely a business that happened to be convenient before something better and cheaper existed.
**Capital at risk:** reported to have reached a peak valuation in the region of $12 billion around 2021 (a widely-cited figure this session could not trace to a single authoritative primary source, and is flagged accordingly); stock fell 38% in a single day in May 2023; 2024 revenue was $618 million against a net loss of $873 million; roughly two-thirds of the workforce was cut across 2025.

## Why It Was Plausible

Chegg was founded in July 2006 as a textbook-rental business — a real, working answer to an obviously overpriced product category — and went public in November 2013 at $12.50 a share, raising $187.5 million at a $1.1 billion valuation. It grew a "homework help" answer-lookup product on top of the textbook business over the following decade, and by the late 2010s that product, not textbook rental, was the company's actual growth engine. None of the underlying marketplace infrastructure this vault's `history/online-tutoring-platforms.md` describes — matching, video delivery, per-session or per-subscription billing — was a COVID invention; Chegg, like Wyzant (2005) and Preply (2012), had built durable technical and operational capability years before the pandemic reached it.

What COVID did, starting in 2020, was produce the largest demand shock education technology had ever seen. UNESCO's own monitoring recorded more than 1.6 billion students and youth affected globally by full or partial school closures at the pandemic's peak. Every existing online-learning product absorbed a wave of students who had no functioning classroom alternative, and Chegg's subscriber growth accelerated sharply through 2020–21 as a direct result. A reasonable investor in that period could look at accelerating subscriber growth, a product with real retention, and a company with fifteen years of operating history, and conclude the growth was evidence of a durable structural shift in how students studied — not merely a temporary substitute for a closed classroom. That the underlying product was, specifically, a fast way to retrieve a correct answer to an assigned problem did not look like a vulnerability in 2021; it looked like the company's sharpest competitive edge.

## What Actually Killed It

The first shock and the second shock must be kept separate, because they came from different waves for entirely unrelated reasons and conflating them is the single most common error this file exists to correct.

**The 2020 event was a demand shock, not a business-model failure.** Mass school closure temporarily inflated demand for every remote-learning product, Chegg's included, and that inflation was always going to partially reverse once classrooms reopened — a normalisation, not a collapse, and one every pandemic-boosted platform in this vault's Wave 11 cohort experienced to some degree.

**The 2023 event was a genuinely new, unrelated dislocation: generative AI made getting a worked answer to a specific problem free.** Chegg told investors in May 2023 that ChatGPT was displacing its core homework-help product; its stock fell 38% in a single day, and the decline did not reverse. 2024 revenue was $618 million against an operating loss of $737 million and a net loss of $873 million. CEO Dan Rosensweig stepped down in June 2024 after fourteen years; his successor Nathan Schultz launched an AI product of Chegg's own ("Cheggmate") that did not reverse the trend, and further layoffs followed — 248 employees (22% of the workforce) in May 2025, then 388 more (45% of what remained) in October 2025, before Rosensweig returned as CEO that same month. A board-led strategic review with Goldman Sachs, examining a sale or a take-private transaction, concluded in October 2025 that Chegg would remain independent and public.

These are two different mechanisms. Demand normalisation is cyclical and expected. A free, instantly available substitute for the exact product being sold is structural and does not reverse when conditions change, because the underlying reason to pay — scarcity of the answer — is permanently gone.

## What It Was Not

**Generative AI did not kill online tutoring, and this is the tidy wrong story that must be killed explicitly.** Preply — founded 1 November 2012 by Serge Lukianov, Kirill Bigai and Dmytro Voloshyn, with nothing to do with COVID and built on human, one-to-one, conversation-based language tutoring — raised a $150 million Series D in January 2026 at a $1.2 billion valuation, becoming a unicorn nearly fourteen years after founding, in the same period Chegg was cutting two-thirds of its staff. The distinction is precise, not incidental: a marketplace built around matching a student with a person for live conversational practice is a poor product for a chatbot to substitute, because the value is the human relationship and real-time exchange, not the correctness of an answer. A product built around retrieving a correct answer to a specific, gradeable question is exactly the task a large language model performs better and for free. **Human-relationship tutoring survived what killed answer-lookup tutoring** — the same wave, hitting the same broad industry, produced opposite outcomes for two segments built on different value propositions.

It is also not established that either shock, on its own, explains the underlying learning outcomes it is sometimes blamed for or credited with fixing. The 2022 NAEP results recorded the largest math score decline since testing began in 1990, but NAEP's own longer-run reporting frames 2015–2025 as a decline "not only during the pandemic" — a slide already underway before school closures began. Chegg's product was never instrumented to measure whether a student who used it actually learned anything, only whether they rebooked or resubscribed; this vault cannot honestly credit or blame homework-help products for a learning trend they never measured their own effect on.

## The Lesson That Transfers

**A product built around scarcity of an answer has a valuation ceiling set by the cost of the cheapest available substitute for that answer, and that cost can fall to zero on a schedule set by a completely different wave than the one that grew the business.** Chegg's moat was never the tutoring relationship or the classroom — it was retrieval speed against a paywall, and a free, instant, general-purpose retrieval tool destroyed that moat in a single product-market shift with no warning specific to Chegg's own execution. An FDE assessing any education, information-retrieval, or answer-brokering business should separate the demand curve (which a shock like COVID can move up or down without changing the underlying product's durability) from the substitutability curve (which a shock like generative AI can collapse to zero, permanently, regardless of demand). The first kind of shock is survivable by definition. The second is not, unless the product's actual value sits somewhere a substitute cannot easily reach — as Preply's did, and Chegg's answer-lookup core did not.
