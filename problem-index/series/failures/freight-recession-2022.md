# Failure: The 2022–2023 Freight Recession

**Lesson class:** the-whole-market-repriced
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-08-mobile-gps|8 — Mobile & GPS]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/freight-brokerage|Freight Brokerage]] · [[industries/owner-operator-trucking|Owner-Operator Trucking]] · [[industries/warehouse-3pl|Warehouse & 3PL]]
**What was claimed:** The historic 2020–2021 surge in e-commerce demand and trucking-capacity shortages was a durable shift, not a temporary pandemic dislocation — justifying a step-change in trucking capacity, freight rates, and the valuations of the software and brokerage layer built on top of it.
**Capital at risk:** no single figure applies — this is a market-wide repricing across an ~$800B-plus US trucking sector rather than one funded venture; specific casualties are dated individually below.

## Why It Was Plausible

2020–2021 produced a genuinely historic freight demand shock: e-commerce volume surged, warehouse and carrier capacity could not keep pace, and — for the first time in the industry's history — the tightness was visible continuously and quantitatively rather than anecdotally. The **ELD mandate**, fully phased in by **December 16 2019** just months before COVID (`history/owner-operator-trucking.md`), meant every truck's hours and location were now electronically legible, and services built on that data layer — DAT's rate indices, FreightWaves' SONAR platform — gave brokers, carriers and investors alike a real-time read on capacity that had never existed in a previous cycle. A tight market you can measure continuously looks less like a speculative bubble and more like an efficient market pricing a real, observable scarcity.

Every actor with money at stake was responding rationally to the same data feed: carriers bought trucks, private equity rolled up small fleets, and brokers raised capital at record valuations — Convoy's $3.8B Series E in April 2022 (covered in this series' own `series/failures/convoy.md`) is the cleanest example, but it was one data point among many pricing the same apparent trend. Freight had cyclical downturns before — FreightWaves CEO Craig Fuller has described a "typical three-year trucking cycle," and 2019 alone produced carrier bankruptcies at Celadon, Falcon and NEMF — so nobody was pricing this market as though cycles didn't exist. What was harder to see in real time was that this particular upcycle was driven by a pandemic-era consumption shift rather than durable freight-mile growth, and was therefore a larger and more temporary anomaly than the cycles that had come before it. Diesel and equipment financing were both cheap through 2020–2021, so entering the market — buying a truck, launching a new brokerage authority — carried a low visible cost exactly when rates were at their highest, which is the textbook rational response to a price signal, not an irrational one.

## What Actually Killed It — the repricing, dated

FreightWaves CEO Craig Fuller published **"Why I believe a freight recession is imminent" on March 31 2022**, citing the tender-rejection rate — a real-time measure of truckload supply-and-demand tightness, tracking actual electronic load requests — falling from **18.7% in early March to 13.90%** by the end of the month, in what should ordinarily have been one of the strongest weeks of the year for carriers. Within two weeks of that publication, this vault's own `history/freight-brokerage.md` records that **dry van spot rates fell 24.1% between January 13 and April 13 2022** — the same collapse Convoy's Series E was priced into at the very top of the cycle. FreightWaves later described the resulting downturn as **"one of the most protracted freight recessions on record,"** with depressed conditions persisting into 2025.

The most visible single casualty of the period was not a startup but a 99-year-old incumbent. **Yellow Corporation filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on August 6 2023**, three weeks after ceasing operations on July 30 2023, affecting roughly **30,000 employees** (22,000 of them Teamsters) and carrying **$1.3 billion in debt due in Q4 2024**, including **$729 million owed to the US Treasury** — the balance of a **$700 million 2020 CARES Act loan** made in exchange for a 29.6% federal equity stake, a loan a June 2023 congressional probe concluded should not have been made on national-security grounds. It matters for this file's next section that Yellow's proximate triggers were a Teamsters dispute over a missed $50 million benefits payment and an unresolved integration failure dating to its 2003 Roadway and 2005 USF acquisitions — not the freight recession by itself. Yellow had posted only three profitable quarters since 2009, long before this cycle began.

## What It Was Not

**Kill "the freight recession proves digital freight brokerage or trucking technology failed."** That is a distinct and separate finding from `series/failures/convoy.md` in this same batch, and conflating a company's capital-cannot-buy-it failure with a market-wide cyclical repricing is precisely the error this vault's research discipline exists to prevent. Convoy's underlying business model — no switching costs, no network effects in commodity load matching — was likely to fail on a longer timeline regardless of this specific downturn's timing. The recession itself, separately, hit financially sound owner-operators and asset-based carriers who had done nothing wrong except buy a truck near the top of the cycle; those two failure mechanisms are adjacent, not identical, and belong in different files for exactly that reason.

It is also worth stating precisely what could not be confirmed here. A commonly repeated claim holds that small-carrier bankruptcies and authority revocations hit a record during this period; this file could not independently verify a specific count or an authoritative source for that figure this session, and it should be treated as directionally plausible but unquantified rather than cited with precision.

## The Lesson That Transfers

A demand and capacity signal generated by an emergency will be treated by rational capital as a durable trend if it persists long enough and if the market has continuous, granular data confirming it in real time — but continuous visibility into a market condition measures the present accurately without saying anything about whether the present is temporary. This is Wave 8's core inheritance (knowing where the workforce and the capacity are, continuously) applied to a question it was never built to answer: not "where is the truck right now" but "will this level of demand still exist in eighteen months." The same instrumentation that makes a boom look undeniable will, symmetrically, make the bust look equally undeniable on the way down. An FDE should treat "we now have real-time data proving X" as evidence about the quality of the measurement, not as evidence about the durability of the thing being measured — and should read this file alongside `series/failures/dot-com-crash.md` in this same batch, where an unrelated industry, two decades earlier, mistook a real but temporary cost collapse for a permanent one by exactly the same reasoning.
