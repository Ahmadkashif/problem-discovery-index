## Why It Was Plausible

Boeing's 737 MAX re-engining programme, launched in 2011 largely in response to Airbus's A320neo, mounted larger, more fuel-efficient CFM LEAP engines further forward and higher on the airframe than previous 737 generations. That change altered the aircraft's pitch behaviour at high angles of attack, tending to push the nose up in a specific part of the flight envelope, unlike its predecessor. Boeing's engineering response was not reckless on its face: build a flight-control augmentation — the **Manoeuvring Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS)** — to nudge the nose back down automatically in that regime, so the aircraft's handling matched the previous generation closely enough that airlines' existing 737 pilots would not need a new type rating or full simulator retraining. Avoiding a new type certificate was not a minor convenience; it was central to the MAX's commercial case, because a shared type rating let airlines add the MAX to an existing 737 fleet without the cost and schedule risk of separately certifying pilots on what would otherwise be a new aircraft type.

There is nothing exotic in principle about a control law that standardises handling across variants of an airframe — pitch- and stability-augmentation systems are unremarkable, common engineering on large commercial aircraft, and Boeing's own account frames MCAS internally as a handling-qualities system rather than an anti-stall system, tuned for a narrow corner of the flight envelope pilots would rarely encounter. Regulators, airlines and Boeing shared a rational commercial interest in the type-rating continuity holding: airlines saved training cost, Boeing sold aircraft faster, and the FAA's Organization Designation Authorization (ODA) programme — which delegated substantial certification work to Boeing's own engineers, a longstanding and, at the time, unremarkable arrangement industry-wide — accepted the resulting documentation as sound.

## What Actually Killed It

MCAS was engineered to act on a reading from a **single angle-of-attack sensor** at a time, even though the aircraft carried two — a design choice meaning a fault in that one sensor gave the flight-control system a bad input with no independent basis to reject it. On **Lion Air Flight 610 (29 October 2018)** and **Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 (10 March 2019)**, a faulty AoA sensor caused MCAS to repeatedly command aggressive, automatic nose-down trim. The pilots had not been told MCAS existed: Boeing had removed references to it from flight manuals and training materials during certification, and the system was first disclosed publicly on **10 November 2018 — twelve days after Lion Air**, and only after the first crash forced the disclosure. Crews facing runaway trim they had no documented reason to expect fought a system they did not know was there, using procedures written for a different failure mode. The two crashes killed **346 people: 189 on Lion Air 610, 157 on Ethiopian 302.**

This mechanism is documented finding, not advocacy: Boeing's own subsequent public acknowledgment, the FAA's post-crash technical review, and the U.S. House Transportation Committee's investigation all name MCAS's single-sensor architecture, the certification-era decision to omit it from pilot-facing documentation, and the degree of self-certification permitted under the ODA programme as the operative failures.

## What It Was Not

**This was not simply "pilot error" or two isolated, unrelated incidents** — a framing Boeing's early public response leaned toward before the second crash made it untenable. Two independent crews, on two different airlines, encountered the same automated system behaving the same undocumented way; that pattern points at the system and its certification, not at two unrelated crews making the same mistake by coincidence.

**Nor was MCAS a safety feature that malfunctioned by unlucky accident, separable from the commercial reasoning behind it.** It existed specifically to avoid the cost of a new type certificate, and the single-sensor architecture and the decision to keep it out of the manuals were both choices made in service of that trade-off, not oversights independent of it. The failure cannot be cleanly separated from the commercial goal that shaped the design.

## The Lesson That Transfers

The fleet was grounded worldwide — China first, on 11 March 2019, the FAA following on 13 March after most of the world's regulators had already acted — and did not fly commercially again until December 2020, after the FAA lifted its order on 18 November 2020: a 20-month grounding. Boeing's direct costs are estimated at roughly $20 billion, with a further ~$60 billion in indirect cost from around 1,200 cancelled orders; in January 2021 Boeing agreed a $2.5 billion Department of Justice settlement (a $243.6 million criminal penalty, $1.77 billion to airline customers, $500 million to a crash-victim fund) under a deferred prosecution agreement, and the FAA revoked the scope of Boeing's self-certification authority under the ODA programme that November.

The transferable lesson generalises cleanly past aviation: **a system that makes a consequential decision on a person's behalf is a hazard the moment that person does not know it exists**, regardless of how sound the system's logic is in the cases its designers tested. MCAS's single-sensor design was a real engineering weakness, but the disqualifying failure was that the pilots had no mental model of the system fighting them — they could not diagnose, still less override, a system whose existence they had not been told about. That is the same structure this lesson class returns to across every case in this set: FirstEnergy's operators had no alarm; Natanz's operators were shown a false one; MCAS's pilots had no idea there was a system to distrust in the first place. Whoever builds the layer that acts on a person's behalf carries an obligation to disclose that it exists and how it can fail — an obligation this case's own record treats as the actual root cause, ahead of the sensor, the software, or the certification process that let it through.
