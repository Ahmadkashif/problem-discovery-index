# Failure: Simple, the Neobank BBVA Bought and PNC Ended (2009–2021)

**Lesson class:** thesis-died-company-lived
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-06-cloud-saas|6 — Cloud & SaaS]]
**Industries touched:** [[industries/neobanks|Neobanks]] · [[origins/retail-banking/profile|Retail Banking]] (origin parent)
**What was claimed:** A software-first company could rebuild the entire consumer banking experience — the interface, the budgeting tools, the customer relationship — without becoming a bank itself, by partnering with a chartered institution to hold the regulatory exposure while Simple owned the product.
**Capital at risk:** $10 million raised in August 2011 (IA Ventures, Shasta Ventures, 500 Startups); acquired by BBVA on 20 February 2014 for $117 million; shut down entirely on 8 May 2021, seven years after acquisition and with no resale or spin-off value realised for the product itself.

## Why It Was Plausible

Simple — founded in Brooklyn in 2009 by Joshua Reich, Shamir Karkal and Alex Payne as "BankSimple," raising its first meaningful capital in August 2011 and launching to a limited beta in 2012 — arrived at a moment when consumer banking software genuinely was a decade or more behind the interfaces people used for everything else in their financial lives. This vault's `origins/retail-banking/origin-story.md` traces the industry's core computing infrastructure to the same postwar decade that produced ERMA and MICR; by 2009, that infrastructure had not meaningfully modernised its customer-facing layer even as mobile computing was transforming every adjacent category. Opening an account still meant a branch visit and a signature; nothing about a checking account's day-to-day interface had absorbed the previous decade's advances in software design.

Simple's structural choice — never seeking a charter itself, instead partnering first with U.S. Bancorp and later with BBVA USA for FDIC-insured accounts — was not evasive engineering the way this vault's `history/bnpl-providers.md` describes pay-in-four's deliberate positioning outside Regulation Z. Chartering a new bank has always required years of federal or state application process and substantial reserve capital before a single deposit is taken; no software company, however well-funded, clears that bar quickly, and every neobank founded in Simple's wake — Chime, Varo, Current, Dave — has made the identical structural choice for the identical reason. A sponsor-bank partnership was, and remains, simply how a software company enters retail banking at all. BBVA's decision to acquire Simple outright for $117 million in February 2014, having already backed its earlier funding round, reads as the plausible next step for an incumbent bank recognising it could not build this interface layer internally as quickly or as well: buy the team and product that already had.

## What Actually Killed It

**Simple was formally closed on 8 May 2021, weeks before PNC Financial Services completed its acquisition of BBVA USA on 1 June 2021.** Public reporting does not establish that PNC required Simple's shutdown as an explicit condition of the deal; what is documented is that Simple's charter host was sold, and Simple ceased to exist as an operating product almost simultaneously. This vault's `history/neobanks.md` treats the inference connecting the two events as the industry's own plainest lesson rather than an invented one, and this file does not go further than that: the timing is exact, the causal mechanism BBVA or PNC used internally to reach the shutdown decision is not part of the public record this session could verify.

What is verifiable and sufficient on its own: Simple's product, customer base, and app quality were never the failure point. Customers used real, working features — a round-up savings tool the company reported had saved users $1.2 million in under four months as of February 2020 — and nothing in the record suggests declining usage or product quality drove the shutdown. The company's fate was decided entirely by a transaction between two much larger institutions, neither of which Simple's customers had ever chosen or, in most cases, heard of.

## What It Was Not

**Simple did not fail because its technology was bad, its product was unloved, or its underlying business model was unprofitable in a way that forced a shutdown — and the tidy story that a fintech app "failed" the way a startup normally fails does not survive the timeline.** BBVA paid more for Simple in 2014 ($117 million) than the company had raised in venture capital ($10 million in its one disclosed major round), which is not the shape of a business investors had given up on; it is the shape of an acquisition an incumbent believed was worth a premium. Nor is this a fraud or a ledger-reconciliation failure of the kind this vault's `history/neobanks.md` documents for Synapse Financial Technologies in 2024 — no customer money went missing, no gap opened between what Simple's systems said and what the underlying bank held. Simple's failure is a different, quieter mechanism entirely: **the charter relationship the company depended on for its entire regulatory existence was an asset it never owned, and when the owner of that asset sold it to someone else, the product built on top of it had no independent right to keep existing.**

This is also not a story about Simple's specific two-charter history (U.S. Bancorp, then BBVA USA from 2016) being unusually fragile compared to peers. Every neobank operating today, per this vault's own hub note, runs the identical sponsor-bank structure — Chime through Stride Bank and The Bancorp Bank, and others through similarly small, deliberately-below-threshold chartered partners. Simple is not an outlier case; it is the industry's founding case, and its ending is the clearest illustration available of a risk every other neobank in the category still carries, largely unpriced, into whatever the next sponsor-bank consolidation turns out to be.

## The Lesson That Transfers

**A company can rebuild every part of a regulated product's interface and operations and still not own the one asset that determines whether it survives — and the asset it does not own is rarely the one its own roadmap is focused on.** Simple's engineers spent a decade improving budgeting tools, notification design, and customer support; none of that mattered once the institution actually holding the charter changed hands. An FDE evaluating any "X, but rebuilt in software" business inside a regulated category should identify, before anything else, which licensed or gate-kept asset the company rents rather than owns, and who the landlord is. That landlord's own strategic decisions — a sale, a merger, a regulatory enforcement action — sit entirely outside the product roadmap, and no amount of product excellence insulates the company from them. Simple's app worked until the day it did not, and the day it stopped had nothing to do with the app.
