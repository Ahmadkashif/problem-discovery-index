# Wave 8 — Mobile & GPS (2007–2016)

**Trigger:** iPhone on sale June 29 2007; Uber founded (as UberCab) March 2009; DoorDash 2013
**What went to ~zero:** the cost of **knowing where the workforce is**
**Failure class produced:** the **asymmetric hold** — and this wave is where it originates

## What Was True The Day Before

A dispatcher knew where a driver was because the driver radioed in. Between check-ins, the field was dark. Every field business — trucking, delivery, service, security, inspection — ran on a schedule made in the morning and a series of phone calls to find out how wrong it had become.

The worker's position was **self-reported**, and self-reporting is negotiable. The gap between the schedule and reality was absorbed by the worker's judgement, and absorbing it was a large part of the skill.

## The Trigger

A GPS receiver, a data connection and a payment mechanism, in every pocket, cheap. Uber is usually told as a consumer story; mechanically it is a **dispatch** story. The innovation was continuous position for supply and demand simultaneously, which turns allocation from a phone call into a computation.

## What Became Possible

Real-time matching against a moving supply pool. Route optimisation against live conditions instead of a printed manifest. Proof of service without a signature. Paying by the task rather than the hour, because the task could now be observed end to end.

That last one is not a technical consequence. It is a **commercial** one, and it is the whole story of this wave.

## The Competitive Fight

**Liquidity.** In a matching market the value of the network to each side rises with the density of the other, so the fight was to reach critical density in a geography first and hold it. That logic justified subsidised pricing, which justified enormous capital, which set the labour terms: if the take rate is compressed by competition, the adjustable variable is what the worker receives.

The competitive structure of the market determined the labour model. Not the technology.

## What It Broke

**The measurement became one-directional, and that is the defining fact of the modern platform economy.**

The platform now knows, continuously: where the worker is, how fast they drive, how long they idle, whether they accept, how they are rated, how long the handoff took. The worker knows: what they were offered, and what they were paid.

They do **not** know how the offer was composed, why this job and not that one, what the platform earned on it, how the algorithm weighted them, or why they were deactivated. The instrumentation is total in one direction and absent in the other.

This is not the missing join — nothing is missing, the platform has both halves. It is not quite the declined join either, because the measurement *is* made; it simply is not shared with the party it governs. It is a third thing:

> **The asymmetric hold: the party holding the measurement withholds it from the person whose livelihood it determines.**

The vault's Phase 2 sweep found this shape independently across gig delivery, rideshare, crowdsourcing, freelance marketplaces, talent assessment and creator platforms — and in several of those notes the honest conclusion was that this is a policy question with a model in a supporting role, not an engineering problem. Those notes say so rather than engineering around it, which is the right call and worth preserving in any episode.
