# Wave 2 — Departmental & Item-Level (1964–1980)

**Trigger:** Joseph Orlicky formulates MRP at IBM c.1964, publishes *Material Requirements Planning* 1975; **UPC first commercial scan June 26 1974, 8:01am** — a 10-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit, 67¢, Marsh Supermarket, Troy, Ohio; first ACH live 1972, NACHA founded June 20 1974
**What went to ~zero:** the cost of tracking physical things one at a time
**Failure class produced:** the missing join, now between the physical world and the record of it

## What Was True The Day Before

Wave 1 computerised the *ledger*. The things — the parts, the cans, the cars — were still counted by walking around with a clipboard. A factory knew what it had built. It did not know, on any given Tuesday, what it had.

Inventory was therefore a guess with a safety margin, and the safety margin was the business's working capital sitting on a shelf.

## The Trigger

Two unrelated events, four hundred miles and ten years apart, did the same thing.

**MRP** let a factory explode a build schedule down through a bill of materials into a dated list of what to order and when. Orlicky's insight was that dependent demand should be *calculated*, not forecast — if you know you're building 400 units in March, you don't forecast how many bolts you'll need, you compute it.

**The UPC** gave every retail item a machine-readable identity. As with MICR in Wave 1, the breakthrough is the handle, not the computer.

## What Became Possible

Item-level truth. A supermarket learned what it actually sold, by item, by store, by hour — and could therefore reorder automatically, price by velocity, and let the shelf itself become a measured asset. A factory could hold less inventory without stopping the line.

Scanner data also created an industry that had no prior existence: **selling the data back**. IRI was founded in 1979 to do exactly this. Every retail-media and category-analytics business in this vault is a descendant of that move.

## The Competitive Fight

**Working capital.** Whoever held less inventory for the same service level had more cash, and cash bought stores and lines. Lean beat fat, and computation was how you got lean.

## What It Broke

Two things, both still live.

**The scanner saw the sale and not the shopper.** Item-level data with no identity attached — which is precisely why loyalty cards arrived in the 1990s, and why retail media is the biggest business in the vault's adtech category today. The join between "what was bought" and "who bought it" was missing for twenty years and is still being monetised.

**MRP is push, and reality is lumpy.** MRP computes a plan from a forecast and pushes material at it. When the forecast is wrong the plan is confidently wrong, at scale.

> **Do not confuse MRP with Toyota's JIT.** They are opposed lineages. MRP is US/IBM computerised *push* scheduling. TPS/JIT is Japanese *pull*-based, computer-independent shop-floor discipline — Ohno's kanban was running from 1953, two decades before Orlicky's book, and it works by *removing* the central plan. Pop-business retellings conflate them constantly.
