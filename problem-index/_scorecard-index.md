### 65. Tenant Screening Data & Scoring — 54/60

### 112. Reserve Study Firms — 51/60
**Industry:** [[industries/hoa-management|HOA Management]] · **Position:** Specialist advisory · **Insight function:** 20-150

A reserve study is a life prediction repeated a hundred times: for every component an association owns, a remaining useful life and a replacement cost, compounded over thirty years into what every homeowner pays monthly. States increasingly mandate the study on a cycle, and Florida's structural integrity reserve study requirement made it statutory across much of the coastal condominium stock.

Meanwhile the firm has inspected thousands of communities, many repeatedly across decades, and frequently knows the year a component was actually replaced and what it actually cost. The competitive context makes both urgent: statutory mandates have pulled price-competing entrants into this market, and a thirty-year archive is the only durable advantage an established firm has.

### 119. Admission Testing Organizations — 51/60

Underneath that, the most valuable thing an experienced inspector produces — trades absent two months running, a site too clean for its claimed stage, a superintendent changed twice — reaches the report as optional narrative, so a report with no adverse comment cannot be distinguished from an inspector who wrote nothing.

### 136. Subcontractor Prequalification Data — 50/60
**Buyer:** VP of Product / Chief Risk Officer. **Who:** TradeTapp, Highwire, Avetta and the qualification platforms.

The direct institutional answer to a problem Pass 1 names explicitly — general contractors' knowledge of which subs show up and do quality work is informal and lost when a superintendent leaves — and it currently answers half the question. Qualification covers financial capacity and safety record because those are documentable from statements and federal data; project performance is collected by nobody, despite the platform sitting between many general contractors and the same subcontractor population, which makes it the only party that could ever aggregate it. Second gap: qualification runs annually because statements are annual, while contractor risk moves quarterly — a sub qualified in March on last year's statement is being awarded work in September on that assessment, and mid-project collapse is almost always visible beforehand to somebody who is not looking.

### 137. Unemployment Claims Management — 50/60
**Industry:** [[industries/hr-consultants|HR Consultants]] · **Position:** Payer & intermediary layer · **Insight function:** 200-1,500

An employer has seven to fourteen days to contest an unemployment claim or forfeit the right entirely. These firms do that across fifty state systems at enormous volume, often priced on outcome — and the decision on each claim is made in minutes by an analyst applying intuition about how that state tends to rule.

The firm holds every claim it has ever handled: separation facts, documentation available, position taken, state, determination, appeal, outcome. Millions of rows, labelled unambiguously by an outcome that arrives within weeks. It is one of the best-shaped prediction datasets in this index and it is used to bill. Nothing estimates win probability before the position is chosen, nothing measures which documentation actually moves a determination — the firm chases documents from employers constantly and has never established which requests are decisive and which are ritual — and nothing compares analysts making the same call differently.

The deadline layer is a genuine buy gap too: statutory forfeiture is not an SLA, and the counting conventions, tolling rules, and filing proof semantics differ by state and must be versioned so the firm can show which rule it applied. The most common cause of a late filing is the employer's own response time, which is in the data and is not used to schedule the work backward from the deadline. And what wins a hearing — how a particular referee weighs a final incident, what evidence they reject — lives entirely in representatives whose turnover is high in a high-volume, modestly paid role.

### 138. Protective Coatings Consulting & Failure Analysis — 50/60
**Industry:** [[industries/painting-contractors|Painting Contractors]] · **Position:** Specialist advisory & valuation · **Insight function:** 50-400

Not residential painting — the consultancies that tell an owner whether a bridge, a water tank or a refinery vessel needs recoating now or in five years. The answer is a report, it moves millions of dollars of capital, and the firm holds something no other party in the coatings industry has: forty years of field observations of how real systems performed on real structures under known exposure.

Coating service life is quoted as a convention that traces back to manufacturer literature and salt-fog chambers everyone agrees correlate poorly with the field. The only body of real-world durability evidence that exists sits in these firms' report archives, one asset at a time, never assembled. Repeat-cycle clients mean the same asset is surveyed again and again, so the archive is a genuine interval-censored survival dataset about a question the industry answers with a single number and no interval.

Two further gaps. Condition ratings — per cent breakdown, blistering grade, rust grade — are assigned by eye against coarse reference photographs, and inspector-to-inspector variance is never measured despite driving the deterioration trend that capital plans are built on; the survey photographs that could measure it are filed as evidence. And the remaining-life estimate, the actual deliverable, is never scored: the firm holds the prediction and, five years later, holds the outcome, and nothing joins them. Deferrals — owners who ignore the recommendation — are the cleanest experiment available and are not recorded as such.

### 139. Roadside Assistance Network Analytics — 50/60
**Industry:** [[industries/towing-companies|Towing Companies]] · **Position:** Payer & intermediary layer · **Insight function:** 300-1,500

The party an independent tow operator works for without quite realising it — sitting between the insurers, manufacturers and motor clubs who promise roadside help and the operators who deliver it, taking the call, triaging the problem, choosing the provider, quoting the arrival time and paying the claim.

The core prediction is explicit, the label is exact, and it arrives within the hour — and it is not modelled. Tens of millions of times a year the network tells a stranded person when help will arrive, then records when it did. The estimate comes from rules: stated coverage time, a distance calculation, a weather adjustment. Performance is reported as the share of events inside a service level threshold, which measures compliance rather than accuracy, so no network can say whether the times it quoted were right. There is no per-event distribution, so nothing flags the promises that are unusually uncertain — exactly when a promise needs a hedge — and the dispatch choice between two providers, which is a comparison of arrival distributions and acceptance probabilities, is a lookup.

Two more, both with free labels. Triage runs on scripted decision trees applied to a distressed caller's description, and sending the wrong equipment means a second truck; the corpus pairing the caller's own words and the vehicle's details with what the provider actually found runs to millions of examples, with every re-dispatch arriving as a labelled error within hours. And provider scorecards — which drive dispatch priority, rates and network membership — rest substantially on arrival times the providers type in themselves, after the fact, against a threshold they know they are scored on, with automatic reporting concentrated among the largest operators, so measurement quality tracks operator size rather than operator quality.

### 140. Underground Utility Locating & Damage Prevention — 50/60
**Industry:** [[industries/utility-contractors|Utility Contractors]] · **Position:** Suppliers selling into the industry · **Insight function:** 500-3,000

The same question at street level and at enormous volume: every excavation in the United States must legally ask what is buried where, and this layer answers it. Hundreds of millions of tickets a year, screened against facility maps, dispatched to technicians with a statutory forty-eight-hour window. Miss a line and someone hits a gas main.

Locator hours are the binding constraint and are allocated by geography and clock rather than by risk — though damage probability varies by orders of magnitude and is predictable from the ticket's work type and extent, the corridor's congestion and records vintage, and the excavator's own history, with the outcome label arriving within days. Quality assurance re-checks, the main control in the system, are sampled at random rather than aimed where the model is least certain.

The screening step compares a dig area described in a caller's free text against facility geometries of wildly varying positional accuracy, using hand-tuned buffers generous enough to be safe — which guarantees the over-notification that consumes much of the industry's field capacity, and which probabilistic screening with modelled records uncertainty would fix on both sides at once.

And the largest discarded dataset found anywhere in this sweep sits here. On a large fraction of tickets the locator discovers the record is wrong — the line offset, deeper, a different material, or absent from the map entirely — marks the ground correctly and closes the ticket. The discrepancy reaches nothing. Facility operators then run expensive periodic records improvement programmes to learn what their own contractors established years ago.
