## The Problem That Came First

A restaurant has an address. A truck has to tell people where it is, every day, again.

That is the whole economic difference. A kitchen on wheels trades rent for mobility, and pays for it in discovery: the customer who liked Tuesday's lunch cannot find Wednesday's unless something carries the news. For most of the trade's history the answer was a fixed route — the same factory gate at the same hour — which made the truck predictable by making it immobile.

## What Got Built

No founding artefact. Nothing in the trade's history was built *for* a food truck and adopted across it. The trade assembled itself from other industries' tools:

| Year | Tool | Builder | Place | What it was for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1866** | Chuck wagon — an ex-Army wagon fitted with shelving and drawers | Charles Goodnight | Texas | Feeding cowhands on a cattle drive |
| **1872** | Lunch wagon — a covered wagon with windows cut in the side | Walter Scott | Providence, Rhode Island | Selling sandwiches, pie and coffee to newspaper pressmen at night |
| **1880s** | Manufactured lunch wagons with stoves, sinks and refrigerators | Thomas H. Buckley | Worcester, Massachusetts | Selling the wagon itself to vendors |
| **1974** | Taco truck — a converted ice-cream truck | Raul Martínez Sr. (King Taco) | Los Angeles | A kitchen without a lease; a restaurant followed within six months |

| **2010** | Square Reader | Square | United States | Taking a card on a phone, without a terminal contract |
| — | The commissary-and-permit regime | local health departments, not one builder | each jurisdiction | Licensing a kitchen that has no fixed address |

The one row that belongs to this trade more than any other is Kogi's. Newsweek called Kogi "America's first viral eatery"; the thing that went viral was not the short-rib taco but the fact that you could follow the truck.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Twitter was not built for food trucks. Kogi's founders were the ones who noticed that it solved their specific problem, and the reason is structural rather than clever.

A post that says *here, tonight, until 2am* is exactly the message a mobile kitchen needs to send and a fixed restaurant does not. A restaurant's address is printed once; a truck's changes daily. A broadcast channel that reached a self-selected following, instantly, for nothing, turned mobility from a cost into a draw — the chase became part of the product.

Every row in the table was built for a *different* problem — a cattle drive, a night shift, a card without a terminal — and the truck borrowed it because the constraint matched.

## What It Cost

Borrowing the channel meant not owning it. The truck's customer relationship lives on a platform whose reach, rules and audience the operator does not control; the follower count is not a list the business holds. And posting daily is one more job for an owner who is also the cook and the driver.

The table's last row is the other cost. The trade's discovery tools got cheap; its permission regime did not. Each city licenses the truck, the commissary and the spot separately, and no single artefact ever unified them.
