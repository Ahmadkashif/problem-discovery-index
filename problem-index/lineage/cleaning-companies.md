# Lineage: Cleaning Companies

**Industry:** [[industries/cleaning-companies|Cleaning Companies]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-06-cloud-saas|6 — Cloud & SaaS]]
**The tool:** The Official ISSA Cleaning Times — a published table of standard times per cleaning task, used to "workload" a building before a janitorial contract is priced
**Builder:** ISSA
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

A janitorial contract is a price for labour that nobody has done yet.

The contractor sells so much a month to clean a building. What it buys is hours: cleaners walking corridors, emptying bins, running machines across floors. Those hours are the cost, and they are decided the day the bid goes in, by someone who has walked the building once.

So the estimator had to turn a building into minutes. How long to vacuum a thousand square feet of carpet? To scrub a restroom with four fixtures? Every firm answered from its own experience, and privately. A buyer comparing three bids could not tell which was lean and which had simply left something out.

## What Got Built

A book of times.

The Official ISSA Cleaning Times lists cleaning tasks and gives each one a standard time per unit of work. The estimator measures the building, lists the tasks it needs and how often, multiplies, and gets labour hours. Staffing and price follow from that. The industry calls this **workloading**.

ISSA's current description says the guide has given the industry "times and tasks" **since the 1960s**. It gives each asset or area its own identifier so a time can be tied to it, and it now includes times for specialised settings such as hospitality, correctional facilities and dormitories.

ISSA says the guide covers "the latest cleaning technologies" and includes times sent in by manufacturers. That suggests entries tied to specific equipment, so a faster machine would show up as a shorter time. I could not look inside a copy to confirm this.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

A distributors' association, not a cleaning firm.

ISSA began in **July 1923** as the National Sanitary Supply Association. Its first meeting was in St. Louis, with seven sanitary-supply distributors. It was founded by Alfred Richter. Its members sold chemicals, paper, mops and machines to the people who cleaned buildings. They did not do the cleaning.

That explains the book's shape. A contractor's own times are part of what it competes on, so no firm had a reason to publish them. A supplier had the opposite need. A neutral, shared scale of minutes lets a supplier show a customer that a machine costs less than the hours it saves. It also gives the supplier's customer a defensible way to price the job. And an association can gather times from many members without any one of them giving away its figures. ISSA's page says the current edition includes "updated times submitted by end users and manufacturers". That is still the mix: the people who clean, and the people who sell to them.

I could not find the name of the first compiler, the first edition's year, or whether the association was "National" or "International" at the time.

## What It Cost

**A standard time describes an average building on an average night.** The book cannot see clutter, the number of occupants, a floor's condition or a crew's skill. Its times are only as good as the conditions they assume. A bid built from them carries every one of those assumptions, and the estimator is the person left to adjust them.

Once a building is workloaded, the table's minutes become the pace a cleaner is expected to keep. That makes the table a source of physical strain, and a source of complaints about quality whenever the real building takes longer than the book allows.

## What You Still Touch

Every janitorial bid still starts from square feet and frequency multiplied by a time. When a price comes in wrong, the error is almost always in how the book's averages were applied to this building. Correcting that is the gap an estimator's gut still fills.
