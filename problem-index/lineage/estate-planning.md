## The Problem That Came First

An estate plan is an argument made in numbers, and in the early 1980s the numbers would not hold still.

The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 removed the limit on the marital deduction, so nothing passing between spouses was taxed. It also raised the amount an estate could pass tax-free in annual steps: **$225,000 for deaths in 1982, $275,000 in 1983, $325,000 in 1984, $400,000 in 1985, $500,000 in 1986, $600,000 from 1987.**

That created the planner's central sum. Leaving everything to the surviving spouse cost nothing at the first death and could waste the first exemption at the second. Showing a couple the difference meant computing tax at two deaths, under two plans, against a table that changed every year. Split interests added a layer: from **December 1 1983** a life estate or remainder was valued at a flat 10 percent, replacing a 6 percent table in force since 1971.

**The constraint was recomputation.** Martin Shenkman's account of how the tool began is exactly that: Leimberg, "having completed a complex set of calculations, … was given a change in facts and had to recalculate the numbers yet again."

## What Got Built

A calculator organised as a catalogue of techniques. It now computes QPRTs, GRATs, charitable remainder and lead trusts, private annuities and "many more" techniques. An ACTEC reviewer in 2017, after more than ten years' use, listed what she used it for: amortising notes, present values, split interests "for trusts such as QPRTs, GRTs, CLTs and CRTs", and "estate, gift and state inheritance taxes". The life-estate calculation, she noted, lives under a menu called **"Tools of Estate Planning/Factors."**

A search summary attributes to the current vendor's page the claim that the first version was "based on VisiCalc". That page refused to load, so this note does not rely on it.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Stephan Leimberg and Bob LeClair, in 1984, and the menu name says why.

Leimberg was the creator and principal author of the *Tools & Techniques* series, published by the National Underwriter Company, and taught at The American College, now the American College of Financial Services. LeClair was an associate professor of finance at Villanova. **Leimberg had already sorted estate planning into named techniques, each with its own arithmetic** — a software menu waiting to be written. Shenkman says a conversation with "a colleague" led to the programming; he does not name the colleague, and this note does not assume it was LeClair.

The program was sold to "estate, business and financial planners", not to estate lawyers alone. The trust and the life policy that pays its tax are designed in the same meeting, and both sides need the same number. **That is the likely reason it came from an author-educator and not a law-practice vendor** — an inference from the stated market, not a documented decision.

## What It Cost

**A calculator is only as current as its tax table.** NumberCruncher's version numbers are years — 2014.01, 2017.00 — and its update log records changes such as a report "updated to reflect the latest worksheet for IRS Form 709." Whenever Congress moves a number, the program is reissued.

The plans computed with it are not. From **May 1989** split interests were valued at the §7520 rate, 120 percent of the federal midterm rate, reset monthly. The exemption went from $600,000 in 1987 to **$15 million in 2026**, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act signed July 4 2025. The printout in a client's file carries the law of the day it ran.
