## The Problem That Came First

A grant writer's first question is who has money for this. In the early 1950s, nobody outside a foundation could reliably say.

There were compilations. The Russell Sage Foundation had published bibliographies and directories of American foundations since **1915**; its 1946 volume, *American Foundations for Social Welfare*, described 505. But nothing obliged a foundation to report what it funded. The tax code did not even define "private foundation" until 1969.

Then Congress asked the same question from the other side. The **Cox Committee (1952)** examined whether tax-exempt foundations were using their money "for un-American activities and subversive activities"; its report found "little basis for the belief" that foundation funds were being diverted from their intended use. The **Reece Committee (1954)** concluded the opposite, that major foundations "have directly supported 'subversion'."

**The constraint was that giving was private information.** The applicant could not see it, and neither could the congressman.

## What Got Built

A public library about foundations, and a book indexing them.

The Foundation Library Center opened in New York on **November 26 1956**. From 1959 it placed depositories in other libraries, the network now called the Funding Information Network. **The first *Foundation Directory* appeared in 1960.** Later data came from IRS Form 990-PF returns, annual reports and questionnaires sent to the foundations themselves.

The directory then followed the computer. A CD-ROM edition, *FC Search*, is dated by a search summary to 1996; Foundation Directory Online followed. The Center became The Foundation Center in 1968, and on **February 5 2019** merged with GuideStar to form Candid.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The foundations built it, and they built it for Congress.

The leaders included John Gardner and **F. Emerson Andrews**, a Russell Sage Foundation man since 1928, co-author of the 1946 directory and the Center's director and president until 1967. The board chair, Russell Leffingwell of the Carnegie Corporation, gave the Center its motto: **"We think that the foundation should have glass pockets."** Candid's own history places the founding in the McCarthy-era scrutiny of foundation spending.

That is why the builder was the funder and not the seeker. **The investigated party had the strongest reason to publish** — a public record of what foundations did was the defence against the charge that they were hiding it. Russell Sage supplied forty years of directory craft. Applicants got the directory as a by-product of foundations defending their tax exemption. That last step is this note's inference from the timing and the motto; no founding document read here states the applicant as the intended user, and none rules it out.

## What It Cost

**A directory written for Congress lists; it does not rank.**

It describes each funder from what the funder files and reports — the vault lists giving history, board composition and 990 data — and arranges the answers by funder. It tells a writer who exists and what they say they fund. It does not say whether this applicant, with this track record, has a chance. That judgement was left to the reader, where it still sits.

The glass pockets also acquired a price. The vault's hub note puts Foundation Directory Online at "$2,000+/year", and some public libraries offer it for in-library use only — the 1959 depository model, now a terminal.
