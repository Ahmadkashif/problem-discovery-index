**Builder:** Jubitz Corporation

## The Problem That Came First

A driver with an empty truck and a broker with a load could not find each other except by standing in the same room.

The room was a truck stop. At the Jubitz Truck Stop in north Portland, Oregon, brokers left handwritten notes on a bulletin board, and drivers finishing a meal and a shower lingered by it hoping a load would appear. The board is where the phrase *load board* comes from. It worked, badly: it held what fit on it, it served only the drivers physically present, and the people running the truck stop fielded the questions it generated.

Discovery, in other words, was local and slow. A load in Portland was invisible to a driver in Sacramento.

## What Got Built

**A television monitor where the corkboard had been.** Available loads were listed on screen at the truck stop; a driver who wanted one paid a fee for the phone number of the company that had it, and called to make the deal. One driver later remembered it plainly: "We'd call the number and if we could make a deal we'd take the load. It was pretty slick."

The service, **Dial-A-Truck**, launched on **April 3 1978**. The model was a listing and a toll: brokers and shippers supplied loads, drivers paid to reach them, and nobody in the middle took the freight itself.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The **Jubitz Corporation**, the family company that owned the truck stop, through a subsidiary founded by **Al (Albin) Jubitz**, son of the truck stop's founder Moe Jubitz.

The why-them is geography. A truck stop is the one place both sides of the market already came to, on their own schedule, many times a year. The Jubitz family did not need to recruit drivers; the drivers were at the counter. They did not need to invent a matching process; the notes were already on the wall. What they had, and a broker or a carrier did not, was the venue — and with it a way to charge the side of the market that was waiting.

The company's own history adds the practical motive: Al Jubitz noticed drivers hanging around hoping for loads and, by DAT's account, was tired of the interruptions. Automating the board took the questions off his staff.

It then scaled the way only a venue network could. Nine other stops along Interstate 5 added monitors; **by 1985 there were more than 200 truck stops in 42 states**. Dial-A-Truck became **DAT Services in 1989**, and the Jubitzes sold it to **TransCore in 2001**.

## What It Cost

**The board made loads visible and left everything else invisible.**

It told a driver a load existed. It did not say whether the broker paid, whether the carrier calling was who it claimed to be, or what the lane should cost. Every one of those judgements stayed with the phone call the fee bought.

And publishing loads to anyone who paid created the conditions for its own fraud. A board open to all comers lets a party take a load it never intends to haul and re-broker it — double-brokering — because the listing cannot tell a carrier from an impostor with a phone.

The subtler cost fell on the brokers. Once loads and later rates were visible across the market, a broker's margin could no longer rest on the shipper not knowing the going price.

## What You Still Touch

Every broker who posts to DAT or a rival board today is posting to a descendant of that monitor, and the gaps are where the monitor left them: price, trust and the call itself.
