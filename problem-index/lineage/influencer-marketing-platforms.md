# Lineage: Influencer Marketing Platforms

**Industry:** [[industries/influencer-marketing-platforms|Influencer Marketing Platforms]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-10-creator-platform|10 — The Creator Platform]]
**The tool:** the material-connection disclosure rule of the FTC Endorsement Guides — 16 CFR 255.5, revised on 5 October 2009 to treat a paid or gifted blogger's post as an endorsement — and the "#ad" it produced
**Builder:** Federal Trade Commission
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

A recommendation is worth something only if the audience believes it is unpaid.

That is the entire value of an influencer to a brand: a person whose opinion reads as their own. It is also the exact thing a payment destroys. A brand that sends a product, or a fee, to a blogger buys an opinion the audience will read as independent — and the more convincing the post, the more the audience is misled about who is speaking.

Advertising law already had an answer for celebrities in television spots, where the audience could see it was an advertisement. It had none for a blog post, a tweet or an Instagram caption, where the commercial relationship was invisible by default. The FTC's endorsement guides had not been updated since 1980.

## What Got Built

A one-sentence rule, and a vocabulary of hashtags.

On 5 October 2009 the Commission voted 4–0 to publish revised Guides Concerning Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising. The release said it plainly: "The post of a blogger who receives cash or in-kind payment to review a product is considered an endorsement." The operative text, **16 CFR 255.5**, reads: where a connection between endorser and seller "might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement, and that connection is not reasonably expected by the audience, such connection must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously."

The guides made advertisers, not only endorsers, potentially liable — which is what turned a rule about bloggers into a brand's compliance problem.

The practical form arrived as staff guidance. On 19 April 2017 FTC staff sent **more than 90 letters** to Instagram influencers and marketers, noting that mobile viewers "typically see only the first three lines of a longer post unless they click 'more'" and that `#sp` and "Thanks [Brand]" were unclear. *Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers* (November 2019) named what works — "#ad," "#sponsored," "advertisement" — and what does not: `#sp`, `#spon`, `#collab`.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The Federal Trade Commission, because deception in advertising is its statute and no one else's.

No platform had an incentive to label its users' posts as advertising; no brand wanted its endorsement discounted; no creator wanted to look paid. Every commercial party gained from the ambiguity. Only a regulator whose mandate is the audience would write the rule.

The shape follows from what the FTC is. The guides are **administrative interpretations, not binding law** — the 2009 release says the Commission bears the burden of proof in any enforcement action. So the FTC could not specify a label or a format. It could only set a standard, "clearly and conspicuously," and then describe, in letters and brochures, what meets it. The hashtag vocabulary is the market's reading of that standard.

## What It Cost

The rule made disclosure a per-post judgment rather than a system property.

Because "clear and conspicuous" depends on placement, wording and platform, every sponsored post became something a brand had to review before it went live. The 2023 revision, voted 3–0 on 29 June, went further and warned that "a platform's built-in disclosure tool might not be an adequate disclosure" — so even the platforms' own labels do not discharge the duty.

That is the origin of the approval queue: a brand liable for a caption it did not write has to see the caption first.

## What You Still Touch

Every "#ad" at the start of a caption, and every brief that makes a creator submit drafts for sign-off, traces to a 2009 sentence about bloggers.
