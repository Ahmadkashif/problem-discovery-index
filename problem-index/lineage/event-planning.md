## The Problem That Came First

A conference is many small events in one building, and each has to be described to someone else.

The planner owns the event; the hotel, caterer, AV company and decorator each own a slice. Before any of them can act, the planner must write down every function — each breakout, meal and reception — with its room, times, headcount, set-up and billing. Planners called that document a **résumé**; some called it a staging guide. The venue answered function by function with its own work orders, the best known being the **Banquet Event Order**.

Neither half had a common format or even a common dictionary. When the industry finally wrote a standard, its first job was to define its nouns: an *event* is "an organized occasion such as a meeting, convention, exhibition, special event, gala dinner, etc.", and a *function* is any one of the occasions that compose it. **The constraint was translation.** Every planner–venue pairing rebuilt the same information in a new shape, and every late change travelled by hand.

## What Got Built

A 27-page template. **The APEX Event Specifications Guide**, approved by the Convention Industry Council on **September 30 2004** and updated June 2005, has four parts: the Narrative; the Function Schedule; a Function Set-up Order for each function; and an exhibitor version for each booth.

Every function gets a number, and "when a function in sequence is cancelled, the function number should not be reassigned." Revisions are dated and highlighted. The organiser sends the ESG to every facility and vendor at least four weeks out; they respond with "completed orders [production schedules, Banquet Event Orders (BEOs), etc.]" at least two weeks out.

It is candid about its medium. **"Stage I – The form will be a word processing file and be completed manually."** Stage II, automated exchange, was left to a future in which software would reference "the APEX data map."

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The Convention Industry Council, because it was the table at which both sides already sat.

It was founded in New York in **1949** as the Convention Liaison Council by four associations: the American Society of Association Executives, the American Hotel and Motel Association, the Hospitality Sales & Marketing Association International and the International Association of Convention and Visitor Bureaus — the buyers of meeting space and its sellers in one federation. It had created the profession's credential, the Certified Meeting Professional, in **1985**. It became the Convention Industry Council in 2000, when APEX leadership formed; the work ran from 2001 in seven panels, one of them "résumés and work orders".

**A format both a planner and a hotel will use cannot come from either.** A planners' association's spec could be ignored by hotels; a hotel chain's could be ignored by its rivals. A federation of both could publish a standard that cost no member a competitive position — which is also why it could only be voluntary. That is this note's inference from the membership, not a documented statement of intent.

## What It Cost

**Voluntary meant optional.** In 2008 Meetings Today reported adoption still incomplete, with many professionals working in their own formats, and hotels receiving 30 to 40 RFPs a day in varying formats.

It also covers only half the exchange. The ESG goes out in APEX form; what comes back is each property's own BEO, which the template itself names as the reply. The planner reconciles the two.

And it was written for meetings. Its Event Type menu offers sixteen named choices, from Board Meeting to Video Conference, plus Other. None is a wedding, and a wedding's florist, DJ and baker have no function numbers.
