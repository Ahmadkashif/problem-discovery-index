## The Problem That Came First

A magazine on the web had costs and no cover price.

In 1994 *Wired* had a print business that worked the ordinary way: readers paid a little, advertisers paid a lot, and an advertiser bought a page whose value was established by audited circulation. The company wanted a web publication with its own staff and original writing. Readers of the early web did not expect to pay for pages, and there was no payment system cheap enough to charge them per article if they had.

Jonathan Steuer, who worked on it, later put the question plainly: how do we come up with money to pay for this thing on the internet? The answer had to be advertising. The difficulty was that a web page had no circulation audit, no rate card, and no agreed unit an advertiser could buy.

## What Got Built

A rectangle at the top of a page, linked to somewhere else.

**HotWired** launched on **27 October 1994** as a commercial web magazine, and carried what is widely credited as the first banner ad: an AT&T placement reading "Have you ever clicked your mouse right HERE? → YOU WILL," which led to an online tour of museums. Accounts say HotWired opened with advertisers from around a dozen brands, including AT&T, MCI, Volvo, Sprint, IBM and Zima.

The banner was priced by analogy to print. Steuer's recollection is that a print page cost about **$10,000, so HotWired charged $10,000 a month**. It was a flat fee with, in Jonathan Nelson's words, "no traffic commitments whatsoever."

The second half of the tool was the report. There was no analytics software. Andrew Anker: "We parsed our website logs for page views and then emailed the advertisers." What passed for web analytics, Steuer said, was a count of lines in the server log file.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**HotWired**, the online arm of Wired Ventures. Andrew Anker, *Wired*'s vice-president and CTO, wrote the business plan; on its approval in April 1994 he became HotWired's first CEO. Louis Rossetto, *Wired*'s founder, dismissed objections to commercial content online.

HotWired was not the first to sell a clickable ad — O'Reilly's GNN had sold one earlier to a law firm. What HotWired had that others lacked was **a print advertising sales force and advertiser relationships already in place**. It could sell web space as a line item beside a magazine page, to the same buyers, at a price they recognised. The banner's shape is the shape of that sale: a bounded, ownable unit of space, bought like a print insertion.

The pageview was not chosen as a measure of value. It was the only thing a 1994 web server recorded that could be counted and sent to an advertiser.

## What It Cost

The flat fee did not last. Within roughly two years, secondary accounts say, portals were pricing banners per thousand impressions, and the unit that had been a courtesy report became the invoice. Once the page was the thing sold, the page was the thing produced.

What the log never recorded was a person. A server line says a file was requested; it does not say whether the requester came back tomorrow, or next month, or paid. The measure that made the web fundable was designed to answer an advertiser's question about exposure, not a publisher's question about loyalty. For advertising publishers the two were close enough for twenty years.

They are not close any more. A subscriber is made by repetition over months, and the unit HotWired's server logs produced cannot see repetition at all.
