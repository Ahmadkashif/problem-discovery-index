## The Problem That Came First

A trade-in has to be priced before the deal closes, and the price has to hold for three parties at once: the dealer who will resell the unit, the customer, and the lender financing the new one.

A number the dealer asserts is a negotiating position. A number from a book the lender also uses is closer to a fact. That was the need a value guide met for cars, and an RV sharpens it: the unit is a vehicle, a house and a set of appliances, and — as this vault's hub records — a hidden roof leak can turn a $30,000 trade into a $5,000 wholesale unit.

## What Got Built

**A book of prices for models, not units.**

The *N.A.D.A. Recreation Vehicle Appraisal Guide* — "the official recreation vehicle guide of the National Automobile Dealers Association" — was published by N.A.D.A. Appraisal Guides of Covina, California. One library catalogue records the serial as beginning with 1980. Editions carried long runs of model years; one 2007 edition covered 19 years of values, 1989 to 2007.

Its successor, under J.D. Power, gives three prices: a suggested list price for new units, a **low retail** price for used RVs "in below-average condition or with high mileage", and an **average retail** price serving "as a baseline for negotiations." Mileage, upgrades and optional equipment are adjusted for; condition is a separate step the user performs.

The dealers' own trade body, the Recreation Vehicle Dealers Association, sells the printed J.D. Power RV appraisal guide in its store.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**NADA, and the asset was authority rather than arithmetic.**

NADA was founded in 1917, in its own words, "when a group of dealers set out to change the way Congress viewed automobiles." In 1922 it began to study used-car values "as a service to members." In December 1933 those studies became the *N.A.D.A. Official Used Car Guide* — 388 pages, 21 regions, about 40,000 copies — "carrying the U.S. government's endorsement as the nation's authority on used-car prices." Subscriptions rose from 28,000 in 1945 to 50,000 a year later.

That history is the business case. A value guide is only useful if the other side of the table accepts it, and by 1980 NADA had spent nearly half a century making its book an accepted reference. Producing an RV edition needed a new table of prices, not new credibility — and a dealers' association was the party whose members both generated trade-ins and needed them financed. When J.D. Power announced its purchase of the NADA Used Car Guide on May 28 2015, the business described itself as a source of "retail, loan, trade-in and auction" values.

## What It Cost

**The book prices the model; the loss sits in the unit.**

A guide keyed to make, model and year cannot see a soft floor. Condition enters only as a choice between price bands, and the user supplies it. This vault's own niche analysis states the limit directly: the guides provide base values and cannot account for the condition variables that decide an RV's real worth — water damage first among them.

So the shared number that lets dealer, customer and lender agree deliberately leaves out the most expensive fact about a used RV: whether its roof leaked. The appraiser's walkaround is the correction, and it is done by eye, one unit at a time.

## What You Still Touch

An appraiser pressing a moisture meter against a sidewall, then looking up the book, is reconciling two instruments: one built for lenders' agreement, one for the unit in front of them.
