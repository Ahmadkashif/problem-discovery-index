# Lineage: Acupuncture Practices

**The tool:** Medicare National Coverage Determination 30.3.3, *Acupuncture for Chronic Low Back Pain* — effective January 21 2020, capping cover at 12 visits in 90 days, 8 more on improvement, 20 a year
**Builder:** Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

An acupuncturist's income depends on who is allowed to be paid for a needle, and for most of the profession's American history the answer from the largest payer was nobody.

The work is simple to describe: needles placed, retained, sometimes re-inserted or wired to a stimulator. What was expensive was getting anyone other than the patient to pay for it. Private insurers covered it patchily, workers' compensation and auto carriers case by case, and Medicare — the payer that sets the pattern others copy — did not cover acupuncture at all.

Every claim a practice did file was a negotiation over whether this treatment, for this condition, by this licensee, counted.

## What Got Built

A coverage rule with numbers in it.

NCD 30.3.3 says Medicare will pay for acupuncture for one condition only: **chronic low back pain**, defined as lasting 12 weeks or longer, nonspecific, not associated with surgery, and not associated with pregnancy. Cover runs to **up to 12 visits in 90 days**, a further **8 sessions** only for patients showing improvement, and **no more than 20 treatments a year**. Treatment must stop if the patient is not improving or is regressing.

Then the sentence that matters most: **acupuncture, including dry needling, for any other condition is non-covered.**

The rule also names who may deliver it. Physicians may, under state law. Physician assistants, nurse practitioners and clinical nurse specialists may if they hold a master's or doctoral degree in acupuncture or Oriental medicine from a school accredited by ACAOM and a current, unrestricted acupuncture licence. Everyone else — which in practice means the licensed acupuncturist who does not also hold one of those titles — can only work as **auxiliary personnel under supervision.**

The claim itself is written in the acupuncture CPT codes 97810–97814 — an initial 15 minutes of one-on-one contact, each additional 15 with re-insertion, with or without electrical stimulation. The NCD does not cite them.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

CMS, and it opened the question itself.

The national coverage analysis, CAG-00452N, was **opened on January 15 2019 and internally generated** — no outside requestor is recorded. The proposed decision followed on **July 15 2019** and the final on **January 21 2020**.

The rationale was opioids. The decision memo cites that up to 66% of patients with chronic low back pain are prescribed opioids and that over 17,000 people in the United States died in 2016 in circumstances involving prescription opioids. Acupuncture was evaluated as a non-drug alternative for the single condition driving the most prescriptions.

**That is why the rule is shaped around one diagnosis and a session count rather than around the profession.** CMS was buying a substitute for a pill, for a defined population, with a stop-loss. It was not recognising acupuncturists: the memo states plainly that acupuncturists are not recognised by CMS as Medicare providers and are not eligible to bill. Changing that is a matter of statute, which CMS cannot write.

## What It Cost

The profession got cover and did not get paid for it directly.

A licensed acupuncturist with a Medicare patient must work under a supervising physician, PA, NP or CNS who bills. The fee flows to someone else's number, and the structure the rule rewards is an integrative group that employs acupuncturists, not a solo clinic that bills. (How far practices have actually reorganised around it was not measured here.)

The condition list costs more. Everything an acupuncturist treats that is not nonspecific chronic low back pain stays cash, and private payers that key their policies to Medicare inherit the same single-diagnosis frame.

## What You Still Touch

A patient's thirteenth session in 90 days needs documented improvement; the twenty-first in a year is not covered. Clinics billing Medicare count visits against a clock set by an opioid memo.
