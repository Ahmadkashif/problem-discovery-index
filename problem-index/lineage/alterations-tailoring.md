## The Problem That Came First

A made-to-measure garment has nothing to alter: the tailor measured the body and cut the cloth to it. Fit was part of manufacture.

The alteration shop as a volume business exists because that link was cut. Once clothes were made in advance for bodies nobody had measured, somebody had to close the gap after the sale, one hem at a time.

So the question is not which tool the trade built — it built almost none — but which tool *created* its work. The answer is the standard size.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The size standard was built for sellers who could not see the buyer.

Mail-order companies drove the standardisation effort, because they shipped garments to bodies described by a number. A shop with a fitting room can absorb a bad size chart; a catalogue cannot.

O'Brien and Shelton found women's bodies too varied for one measurement and proposed a nine-size scheme combining girth and height. Industry rejected it. The Bureau of Standards' CS 215-58 then keyed size to bust alone, with modifiers. It was unpopular, made voluntary in 1970 and withdrawn in 1983; ASTM's 1995 successor was never widely adopted, and sizes drifted into brand-by-brand catalogue sizes and vanity sizing.

**None of these builders was a tailor.** The standard's job was to let a manufacturer cut thousands of identical garments; every body the number did not describe was left for someone downstream.

## What It Cost

The standard traded fit for scale. A single-axis size cannot encode a rounded shoulder, a rotated hip or one leg a centimetre shorter than the other — precisely the asymmetries the vault's fit-assessment note says a master tailor reads in thirty seconds. The survey's own finding, that one number was never enough, was the first thing dropped.

The subtler cost is the drift after 1983. A "size 8" now means whatever a brand decides, so the counter cannot predict the error from the label; every garment is diagnosed from scratch, by eye.

## What You Still Touch

Every hem you take in is the gap between a size chart and your body. Online buying widened it, and the diagnosis still lives in an ageing tailor's head.
