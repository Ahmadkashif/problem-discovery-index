# Lineage: Corporate Training

**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-06-cloud-saas|6 — Cloud & SaaS]]
**The tool:** the Kirkpatrick four levels — Reaction, Learning, Behavior, Results — the four-rung evaluation ladder a training programme is still reported against
**Builder:** Donald Kirkpatrick
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

A training department sells something whose effect happens somewhere else.

The course runs in a classroom on a Tuesday. The thing the course was bought to change — a supervisor's handling of a grievance, a salesperson's close rate — happens weeks later, on a shop floor or in a client's office, mixed in with every other thing that changed that quarter. The trainer never sees it.

So what a training director could actually show a budget holder in the 1950s was the only thing that happened in the room: who attended, and whether they liked it. The expensive part of the question — *did it work?* — had no agreed shape. Every department that tried to answer it invented its own measure, and none of them could be compared.

## What Got Built

A ladder with four rungs, each asking a narrower-to-wider question:

1. **Reaction** — did the participants like it?
2. **Learning** — did they acquire the knowledge or skill?
3. **Behavior** — do they do anything differently on the job?
4. **Results** — did the organisation's outcome move?

It is not software, not an instrument and not a statistic. It is a *vocabulary* — which is exactly why it travelled. It gave a training director four words to put at the top of four columns, and a clear admission that the first two are cheap and the last two are what the money was spent on.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Donald L. Kirkpatrick (1924–2014), later Professor Emeritus at the University of Wisconsin and a past president of the American Society for Training and Development.

The framework grew out of his **1954 Ph.D. dissertation** and reached practitioners through a **1959 series of articles in the society's own journal** — the publication Wikipedia cites under its later title, *Training and Development Journal*. A book-length treatment, *Evaluating Training Programs*, did not follow until **1994**.

Why him and not a vendor or an employer? Because the problem was a *profession's* problem, not a firm's. A company could build its own evaluation; it could not make its numbers mean anything next to another company's. What the field needed was a shared frame published in the place every training director already read — and the person positioned to supply it was an academic embedded in that profession's association, not a consultant with a proprietary method to protect. The model's shape is the shape of that position: generic, non-proprietary, easy to repeat at a conference.

## What It Cost

**The ladder named the hard questions without answering them.** Levels 3 and 4 say *measure behaviour* and *measure results*; they say nothing about how to separate a course's effect from a price change, a new manager or a good quarter. That is a causal-inference problem, and the model is silent on it.

The practical consequence was predictable. Departments measure where measurement is cheap. Level 1 "smile sheets" became universal because they are handed out before anyone leaves the room; Level 4 stayed aspirational because it needs data the training department does not own. Later additions — a fifth "ROI" level proposed by others — added a column without adding a method.

The subtler cost: a vocabulary this successful becomes the reporting format. Learning platforms now ship dashboards organised by level, which makes the unmeasured rungs visible as empty cells rather than as a research problem.

## What You Still Touch

The feedback form at the end of every compliance module is Level 1, and it exists because it is the only rung the system can fill on its own. The LMS completion report is the same record with a date on it.
