## The Problem That Came First

YouTube had an audience and no way to pay the people who filled it.

By 2006 the site was carrying enormous volumes of user-uploaded video, and Google bought it that October for US$1.65 billion in stock, closing on 13 November. The advertising was the hard part. Chad Hurley said in August 2006 that there were "better ways for people to engage with brands than forcing them to watch a commercial before seeing content." The site had experimented with participatory ads in February 2006; in-video ads began in August 2007.

The constraint was on the supply side. The uploaders whose videos drew the traffic were unpaid, and a competitor was already paying them. **Revver**, launched on 29 October 2005, attached a clickable "RevTag" ad to the end of each video and split the fee with the creator. Its best-known clip, the Diet Coke and Mentos experiment, reportedly earned its makers $50,000. A platform that could not pay its most-watched uploaders was renting its audience from people who had an alternative.

## What Got Built

A contract, not a feature. The Partner Program is an agreement under which YouTube places advertising against a channel's videos and pays the channel a share of what that advertising earns.

It began invite-only and opened to a broader set of uploaders in December 2007. The terms that matter today are the **eligibility gate**. In April 2017 YouTube set a threshold of 10,000 lifetime views; on 16 January 2018 it replaced that with **1,000 subscribers and 4,000 hours of watch time in the previous 12 months**. The current help page adds a Shorts route — 1,000 subscribers and 10 million Shorts views in 90 days — and still requires a linked AdSense account.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

YouTube, because it was the only party that controlled both the ad inventory and the audience.

Revver proved creators would move for money, but it had the payout without the traffic. The broadcast networks and labels had catalogue, but no pipe for amateur video. Only YouTube had millions of uploads and a parent, Google, that already ran an ad-selling machine — AdSense — into which the uploads could be plugged. Paying creators was cheaper than losing them, and the revenue share cost YouTube nothing it was not already failing to earn.

The contract's shape follows that business case. **The program is YouTube's policy, amended by YouTube, unilaterally.** The 2018 post, signed by Neal Mohan and Robert Kyncl, justified the higher bar as a way to "identify creators who contribute positively to the community" and steer ad revenue "away from bad actors." The threshold exists to protect advertisers, and the creator is the party it filters.

## What It Cost

The creator got paid and gave up the terms.

Income became a function of two things the creator does not control: whether a channel is admitted and kept, and whether a given video is shown to anyone. Each tightening of the gate is justified by advertiser protection, and each is paid for by small channels: the January 2018 post applied the new bar to channels already in the program, not only to new applicants.

A second cost is less visible. A revenue share tied to ad views makes **reach the unit of pay**, so the recommendation system that allocates reach becomes the creator's real employer — one that reports outcomes and never reasons.

## What You Still Touch

Every creator with a "1,000 subscribers" milestone is working toward a gate set in a 2018 blog post. Every sponsorship deal, membership tier and owned newsletter a creator builds is, in part, insurance against the contract's unilateral terms.
