## The Problem That Came First

On a commercial job, the painter does not choose the paint. The architect does, in a specification section written before the painter is hired.

Architects are not coatings specialists. A spec might name a product that does not suit the substrate, omit the primer, call for a sheen nobody makes, or simply say "two coats, best quality." The painting contractor then bids against that text. **If the finish fails — peeling on galvanised steel, flashing on a patched wall — the dispute is about whose fault it was**, and the contractor who followed a bad spec to the letter is the one standing in front of it.

So the painter's commercial exposure was set by someone else's document, and there was no shared definition of what a correct system for a given surface even was.

## What Got Built

A specification the painters wrote themselves.

The manual is organised **by substrate** — concrete, masonry, drywall, wood, ferrous metal, galvanised metal and so on — and for each gives numbered systems: surface preparation, primer, finish coats, and the sheen. An architect can specify a system by its number instead of drafting coatings language from scratch.

The systems do not name brands. They name **product categories**, and a product qualifies for a category by appearing on the **Approved Products List**, which tests commercially available paints against published performance standards. Specification, product and workmanship were meant to lock together: the MPDA also ran an **Inspection & Guarantee** programme, so a job specified from the manual could be inspected against it.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

A painting contractors' association in Vancouver, because the contractors were the ones absorbing the failures.

The Master Painters and Decorators Association was founded in 1899, regularised in 1911 and incorporated as a society in 1927, with offices in the Vancouver area throughout. It describes itself as a non-profit association of "quality paint and coating contractors." It produced **the first Architectural and Repainting Specification Manuals in the mid-1960s**, and by **1967** had built the Inspection & Guarantee programme around them.

**Why them:** a contractor cannot win an argument with an architect alone, and a paint company's specification will name its own products. A contractors' body could write a brand-neutral standard that architects would accept, and then offer inspection and a guarantee behind it — which turned the spec from advice into something a building owner would pay for.

The manual reached the US through its successor. The **Master Painters Institute** was founded in **1995** to provide services on the MPDA's behalf and published the Architectural and Maintenance Repainting manuals under the MPI name at the end of the 1990s. MPI's own timeline records that in **2000 the GSA replaced US federal paint specifications** with MPI-based guide specifications. In 2017 MPI was acquired by NACE, now AMPP.

## What It Cost

**It standardised the system, not the surface.** The manual assumes a substrate that matches its category. The paint failures that cost most — moisture in masonry, incompatible old coatings, inadequate preparation — begin with a surface condition the specification can name but not see.

The other price was reach. A numbered spec is powerful on specified commercial and government work and nearly invisible on residential repaint, where the homeowner chooses a colour, the painter chooses the product, and nobody writes a specification at all.

## What You Still Touch

A federal or institutional repaint still arrives with MPI system numbers in the spec section. The unsolved half is the step before it: reading the actual surface well enough to know which system it needs.
