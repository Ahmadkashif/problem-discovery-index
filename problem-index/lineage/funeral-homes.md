## The Problem That Came First

A funeral is bought once, in a hurry, by someone who is grieving and has never bought one before.

The family usually arrives within a day or two of the death, has no price to compare against, and is not in a state to shop. The funeral home, by contrast, sells the same thing every week and knows exactly which parts of it carry the margin.

The selling format followed from that asymmetry: **the package**. One price for "the funeral", keyed to the casket, with embalming, viewing, the hearse and the director's time folded in. A family could not see what any element cost, decline one it did not want, or compare three homes, because nothing comparable existed to quote.

Jessica Mitford's *The American Way of Death* (Simon & Schuster, 1963) made the practice a public issue and led to Congressional hearings on the industry. What it did not produce was a document.

## What Got Built

A price sheet with a fixed shape.

The **General Price List** must carry the words "General Price List", the home's name, address and telephone number, and an effective date. It must itemise up to sixteen specified goods and services the home offers — from direct cremation, immediate burial and the basic services fee through embalming, viewing facilities, hearse and limousine to caskets and outer burial containers.

It must also carry six mandated disclosures, among them that the buyer may select only the items wanted, that embalming is not required by law except in special cases, and that alternative containers are available for direct cremation.

And it has a trigger. The home must offer the list **"when you begin to discuss"** the type of funeral, the goods and services, or their prices — before the conversation that used to end in a package. Prices must also be given by telephone on request, and the family gets an itemised statement of what it actually chose.

The rule took effect on April 30 1984 and was amended in 1994 (59 FR 1611). Packages remain legal; they must sit beside the itemised list, not instead of it.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The Federal Trade Commission, and the reason is that nobody inside the transaction had a motive to build it.

The home earns its margin from the bundle; itemising exposes what is optional. The family has no leverage and will not be back. Casket makers sell through the home. Only a regulator with authority over unfair and deceptive trade practices sat outside the sale and could impose one format on nineteen thousand-odd independent sellers at once.

That is why the artefact is a *document standard*, not a price control. The FTC did not set what a funeral costs; it fixed the shape of the disclosure — which lines, what caption, handed over when. The Commission styles it a Trade Regulation Rule, and has reopened it for review since, with notices in 1999 and 2008.

## What It Cost

**The rule made prices comparable on paper, not in practice.** The list is due when the family is already sitting in the arrangement room, so the moment of comparison still falls after the choice of home. Telephone disclosure exists but depends on a caller knowing to ask.

The industry absorbed itemisation by moving margin into the **basic services fee**, the one line the rule allows to be non-declinable. And the list was written for a paper handout: it says nothing about how, or whether, prices appear online, which is where families now start.

## What You Still Touch

Every price sheet handed across a funeral-home desk today carries the caption and lines this rule dictated in 1984. Its compliance burden — keeping 80–150 lines current, reconciling packages against items, proving every inquirer got the list — is now its own small market.
