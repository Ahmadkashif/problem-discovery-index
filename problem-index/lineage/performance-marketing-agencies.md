## The Problem That Came First

Web advertising in the mid-1990s was sold like magazine space: by the thousand impressions. An advertiser paid for a banner to be shown and then had to guess whether anyone who saw it wanted what was for sale.

Search engines had the opposite problem. A person typing "used car" was declaring intent more precisely than any magazine reader ever had, but the engines monetised that page with the same banners, while their ranked results were being polluted by pages stuffed with keywords to game relevance. The most valuable signal on the web was being sold at the price of the least valuable one.

## What Got Built

A marketplace in which the result list itself was the advertising.

Unveiled at the TED8 conference in **February 1998**, GoTo let advertisers bid on how much they would pay to appear at the top of results for a specific search term. Ranking followed the bid. The bid was charged **per click**, not per display — the advertiser paid when a searcher came through to its site, and nothing when they did not. Bids started at a cent; by July 1998 advertisers were paying up to about a dollar a click, and roughly five months after launch more than a thousand sites were paying for placement.

Every element a performance marketer works with is present: a keyword, a bid, a position, a cost per click, and an account the advertiser could change themselves.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**GoTo.com**, founded in 1997 inside **Idealab**, Bill Gross's incubator in Pasadena.

The earlier attempt shows why it had to be a newcomer. Open Text had tried favoured placement in its search index in 1996, drew complaints, and dropped it within weeks; it had a search reputation to lose. Gross's argument, as recorded in accounts of GoTo's founding, was that a new engine had no reputation to taint with paid listings — and that an open bid market would filter spam by price, since a spammer would not pay to be clicked on by people who did not want the page. The TED audience reportedly hissed.

The per-click shape followed from GoTo's own economics. It had little traffic of its own and bought visitors elsewhere by the click, so pricing its inventory by the click was the only way to make the arbitrage legible. That metering became the product. GoTo later syndicated its paid results to larger portals, renamed itself **Overture Services** on 8 October 2001, sued Google in April 2002 over AdWords on a patent filed in May 1999, and was bought by Yahoo on 7 October 2003 for $1.63 billion. The Google dispute settled with 2.7 million shares for a perpetual licence.

## What It Cost

**The party selling the click became the party counting it.**

Pay-per-click promised that an advertiser paid for results rather than exposure. But the result being metered was defined, measured and invoiced by the seller. As later platforms extended the model from the click to the conversion, each one kept that position: it sold the outcome and reported the outcome. Nothing in the design reconciles one seller's count against another's.

The second cost was control. An auction priced per keyword made the bid the advertiser's lever. Once platforms automated bidding, the lever moved back inside the seller.

## What You Still Touch

Every agency dashboard that sums conversions from several ad platforms and gets more sales than the client made is adding up sellers' own invoices for outcomes — GoTo's per-click meter, extended.
