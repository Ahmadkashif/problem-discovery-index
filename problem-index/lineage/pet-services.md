## The Problem That Came First

A pet business holds other people's dogs, and sooner or later one of them bites.

What happens next depends on a description. An owner says the dog "just nipped." A staff member says it "attacked." Size and breed bend the account further: a terrier's lunge is shrugged off where a large dog's would be reported to animal control. Every consequential decision — whether the dog stays in group play, whether it can be groomed without a muzzle, whether to refer to a behaviourist, whether the facility can keep insuring the risk — rests on a word nobody agrees on.

## What Got Built

A scale that ignores intent and measures damage.

The Dunbar Dog Bite Scale sets out, in its author's words, to evaluate "the severity of a dog's bite based on an impartial evaluation of the amount of injury caused." Its levels are:

- **Level 1** — the dog snapped or lunged but its teeth did not touch skin.
- **Level 2** — teeth touched skin but did not puncture it.
- **Level 3** — punctures, from a single bite, shallower than half the length of the canine teeth.
- **Level 4** — punctures from a single bite, at least one deeper than half the canine length; deep bruising or slashing.
- **Level 5** — a multiple-bite attack, or several attacks, at Level 4 or above.
- **Level 6** — the victim died.

The load-bearing line is the one between 2 and 3. Below it, the dog has shown that it can control the force of its jaws — **bite inhibition** — and Dunbar describes such dogs as generally safe and readily rehabilitated with training. At Level 3 the skin is broken, the inhibition is shown to be unreliable, and he calls for professional help immediately.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Ian Dunbar — a veterinarian who researched dog aggression and built a business around preventing bites.

Dunbar qualified in veterinary medicine at the Royal Veterinary College, London, then took a doctorate in ethology at UC Berkeley, where he studied the development of social hierarchies and **aggression in domestic dogs**. In **1982** he founded **SIRIUS Puppy Training**, off-leash socialisation classes for puppies, on the premise that many dog "failures" are really failures of early training — above all, of teaching a puppy to soften its bite. In **1993** he founded the **Association of Pet Dog Trainers**.

**That background explains the scale's shape.** A clinician would classify by wound; a trainer by behaviour. Dunbar's scale does both, using the wound as evidence about a behaviour — bite inhibition — that his own training programme set out to install. It is a severity measure built to answer a trainer's question: *can this dog be fixed?* That is why it tracks puncture depth against canine length rather than, say, stitches or hospital days.

## What It Cost

**It is retrospective.** The scale grades a bite that has already happened; it says nothing about the dog that has not yet bitten, which is the dog a daycare is actually deciding about at intake.

It is also **operator-scored**. The reference measure is half the length of *this* dog's canine teeth, judged after the fact from a wound, often by the person who was bitten or by a manager who was not there. Objectivity improved on "it attacked me" without becoming measurement.

And its authority travelled: a scale built for training prognosis is now used by medical and animal-control personnel, where a level can decide a dog's fate.

## What You Still Touch

A daycare incident report that asks "bite level (1–6)", a boarding facility that expels any dog with a Level 3, a groomer's note that a dog is "Level 2, muzzle for nails" — all speak Dunbar's vocabulary.
