## The Problem That Came First

A veterinarian treats a dozen species with a pharmacy mostly designed for one.

Relatively few drugs are approved for animals, and far fewer for any particular species. So the working pharmacy of a small-animal clinic is full of human drugs, and of animal drugs used outside their label — a dog product given to a cat, an antibiotic at a different dose, a sedative in a ferret. The label and the package insert, which a physician can lean on, often say nothing about the patient on the table.

That makes dosing a knowledge problem, not a lookup. The right amount depends on species, weight, sometimes breed, and on metabolic quirks — cats handle some drug classes very differently from dogs — that are recorded in scattered journal papers rather than on any box. The law for most of the period also made the practice legally awkward: extralabel use was not clearly permitted until the **Animal Medicinal Drug Use Clarification Act of 1994**, which FDA describes as allowing veterinarians to prescribe extralabel uses of approved animal drugs *and approved human drugs* under defined conditions.

## What Got Built

A handbook organised the way a clinician asks the question: by drug, then by species.

Each monograph gathers what the label does not — indications, contraindications, adverse effects, interactions, and then **doses listed species by species**, dog, cat, horse, cattle, and onward, typically as milligrams per kilogram with route and interval, each tied to the literature it came from. It is the veterinary analogue of a pharmacist's formulary, written for a profession that had to borrow its drugs.

The earliest edition this session could confirm is *Veterinary Drug Handbook*, **1991**, 687 pages, published by **PharmaVet Publishing** of White Bear Lake, Minnesota. Later editions were distributed by Iowa State University Press (2002), then Blackwell (2008, from Stockholm, Wisconsin), and the ninth by Wiley-Blackwell in 2018. The content has since moved online as Plumb's, a digital subscription reference for veterinarians and pharmacists.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

A pharmacist, which is the point.

Donald C. Plumb was **Director of Pharmacy Services and Hospital Director at the University of Minnesota's Veterinary Medical Center**. That position sat exactly at the gap: a veterinary teaching hospital runs a real pharmacy, stocked with human and animal products, and its pharmacist fields the dosing questions clinicians cannot answer from a label. The handbook is what that job produces when it is written down.

**That is why it was not a drug company or a veterinary association.** A manufacturer can only promote its own labelled uses, and extralabel doses are precisely what it may not advertise. A professional body writes guidelines for conditions, not monographs for molecules. What the problem needed was someone paid to know every drug in the building across every species that walked in, with an academic hospital's literature access behind him. The builder key is Plumb himself: the book carries his name, and whether PharmaVet was his own firm was not established.

## What It Cost

**A book is a snapshot, and dosing literature moves.** Every edition was out of date on some drug by the time it shipped; moving online answers that.

The deeper trade-off is authority by compilation. Many species-specific doses rest on small studies, extrapolation or clinical experience, and a clean table can make a thin evidence base look settled. The handbook cites; a busy clinician reading a mg/kg figure at 7pm often does not follow the citation.

And it is a reference, not a check. It tells you the dose; it does not stop you mistyping the weight.

## What You Still Touch

A technician calculating a feline dose from a kilogram weight is doing Plumb's arithmetic, often from Plumb's own tables, and the error that costs a patient is still a decimal point or a species slip.
