## The Problem That Came First

An assistant is useful in proportion to what they can touch: inbox, calendar, travel accounts, vendor portals. An assistant down the corridor touched them through the paper diary; an assistant in Manila touches them through credentials.

**That is the constraint, and it is an access problem before it is a labour problem.** Delegation to a remote person requires handing them some version of the principal's identity, and the vault's own hub describes what the industry settled on: "a person and a set of shared logins, coordinated over messaging." A shared password grants everything, logs nothing about who acted, and has to be changed — everywhere — every time the assistant does.

## What Got Built

Nothing built for this industry. What exists is a set of artefacts built for adjacent problems and adopted by it:

| Year | Tool | Builder | Place | What it was for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2003 | oDesk and its Work Diary | oDesk | US and Greece | Proof of hours for hourly remote contracts — see [[lineage/freelance-marketplaces|Lineage: Freelance Marketplaces]] |
| 2007 | *The 4-Hour Workweek* | Tim Ferriss, published by Crown | US | The demand artefact: a bestseller whose method included "outsourcing small daily tasks to virtual assistants" |
| not established | Gmail mail delegation | Google | US | A delegate can "read, send, and delete emails in your account for you," and their address appears on what they send |
| 2011 | Zirtual | Maren Kate Donovan | not established | An assistant assigned to a client for email, scheduling, research and travel |
| 2019 | Deel | Alex Bouaziz, Shuo Wang, Ofer Simon | not established — Y Combinator 2019 batch | Paying workers across borders — see [[lineage/remote-work-infrastructure|Lineage: Remote Work Infrastructure]] |

**Delegation is the row that addresses the access problem directly,** and its exclusions show it. Per Google's current help page, a delegate "can't chat with anyone or change your password,". A personal account can add up to 10 delegates; a work account up to 1,000. The principal keeps the identity; the assistant gets a signed, attributable slice of it.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

No single party — and the reason is structural. The industry's buyers are individual executives and small firms; its sellers are agencies whose margin is the gap between what an assistant is billed and what they are paid. Neither side has had the scale or the motive to build infrastructure.

Google built delegation, but why it did — and whether for executive–assistant pairs, shared role mailboxes or something else — could not be established this session. Nor could its launch date.

The demand side is dated. Ferriss's 2007 book spent four years on the *New York Times* best-seller list and sold around 2.1 million copies; Wikipedia records that he used services such as AskSunday, "paying them meagrely." Zirtual's history shows the supply side's fragility: on 10 August 2015 Donovan announced it was ceasing operations over "market circumstances and financial constraints"; Startups.com acquired it in an all-stock deal and it resumed by 17 August.

## What It Cost

Borrowed tools carry their builders' priorities. Delegation covers mail; the calendar, the airline account and the vendor portal each need their own arrangement or a shared password, so the shared login survives next to the proper mechanism. And none of the table measures the thing sold. The Work Diary proves hours; delegation attributes actions; neither says whether the executive got time back.
