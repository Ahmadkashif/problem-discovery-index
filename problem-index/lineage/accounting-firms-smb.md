**Builder:** AICPA
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

A small firm's bread-and-butter job had no way to say what it wasn't.

In 1963 Max Rothenberg & Co. agreed, orally and with no engagement letter, to keep the books for 1136 Tenants' Corp., a New York cooperative apartment building, for **$600 a year**. The firm called it "write-up" work: take the client's records, produce the statements.

The building's managing agent was embezzling. He later pleaded guilty to taking about $130,000. The accountants' own file held a worksheet headed **"Missing Invoices 1/1/63–12/31/63"**, listing more than $44,000 of invoices that were supposedly paid but could not be found. Nobody told the client.

Judgment was entered in 1970 and affirmed by the Appellate Division in 1971: **$237,278.83 with interest**. The majority held that even if the firm had been hired only for write-up work, it knew the invoices were missing and "had a duty to at least inform plaintiff of this."

**The constraint was that a CPA's name on a set of numbers carried an audit's liability, whatever had been agreed over the phone.** The product had no way to state what had *not* been done.

## What Got Built

A paragraph that says what wasn't done.

**SSARS No. 1, *Compilation and Review of Financial Statements*, issued December 1978**, set out two levels of service below an audit. A **compilation** gives no assurance. A **review** — inquiry and analytical procedures — gives limited assurance. Whenever an accountant *submitted* statements to a client, at least a compilation was required, and **statements with no report attached, called "plain paper", were no longer allowed**. The report had to state what procedures had been applied and what assurance was given: "limited or none."

Pared down since, the compilation report is now "just one paragraph with no headings."

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The AICPA wrote it, through its Accounting and Review Services Committee, which was **made a senior technical committee in 1977** with authority over unaudited statements of nonpublic entities.

It had to be the Institute because the problem was the reach of liability, not arithmetic. An informal agreement about scope had failed in court. The fix was a *published standard* defining a service that promises nothing, in wording lawyers and lenders would recognise, and only the body that sets what a CPA is supposed to do could write it.

Accounts differ on how directly the case caused the standard. One says SSARS 1 was issued "in part" to address plain-paper liability. Another calls the link indirect, running through a 1975 AICPA guide, and names two nearer pressures: lenders wanted something from small companies they could rely on, and practitioners did not want to perform analytical procedures without being able to report limited assurance. **That second pressure is why the review tier exists.**

## What It Cost

**The trigger was written for paper.** "Submission" meant typed statements handed across a desk. Then desktop ledgers arrived; a 2014 practitioner explainer notes that clients and bankers "have grown accustomed to software products such as Quick Books and Peachtree that permit a business owner to provide financial statements absent any reports." Once the client's PC printed the statements, who had submitted what?

**SSARS No. 21, issued October 2014**, dropped the trigger — compilation now applies only when a firm is engaged for one — and added a tier below it, **preparation**: no attestation, just a statement on each page that "no assurance is provided." Thirty-six years after banning the plain-paper statement, the profession wrote it back in, with a legend on every page.

## What You Still Touch

A loan covenant asking for "reviewed" statements is asking for the 1978 middle tier. A firm hired to produce monthly statements out of QuickBooks Online is doing preparation work — the tier defined by the absence of assurance.
