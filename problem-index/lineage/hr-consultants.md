**Builder:** Hay Group
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**

## The Problem That Came First

A company can price a job it can see in the labour market. It cannot price the jobs it invented.

A welder or a typist has a going rate; you can phone around and find it. A regional sales manager at one particular food company does not. By the 1940s large American employers had hundreds of management positions with no outside market price, and every salary among them was a negotiation — which meant every salary was also a grievance waiting for someone to compare it with the office next door.

What an employer needed was not a salary survey. It was a **defensible internal ordering**: a way to say that this job is worth more than that one, for reasons that could be written down and repeated, before any money was attached.

## What Got Built

A set of printed charts and a scoring procedure.

Each job is read against three factors — **Know-How** (what you must know), **Problem Solving** (how much thinking the job demands of that knowledge) and **Accountability** (what the job answers for). A trained evaluator locates the job on each chart and reads off a points value. The points are summed; the total sorts the job into a grade; the grade is then priced against the market.

The "profile" half checks whether a job's points lean toward thinking or toward answerability.

The artefact that survives is the **number**. A job's Hay-point total is directly comparable with any other scored job in any other client — which is what turned a consulting engagement into a data set.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Edward N. Hay (1891–1958), a personnel officer who ran the personnel function at First Pennsylvania Bank in Philadelphia and set up his own consultancy in **1943**. His first large engagement, in **1945**, was from **General Foods**, to evaluate **450 management positions** — large enough that he left the bank and ran the firm full time. The Guide Chart-Profile Method itself is dated by Steinberg (1992) to **1951**, developed with **Dale Purves**; a later source places a refinement with Purves in **1954**.

**Why a consultant and not the employer?** Because the method's value depends on being applied the same way across many companies. General Foods could have built an internal points scheme; it could not have made its points mean anything at another company. An outside firm that carried the same charts into every client could — and every client scored added to a cross-company database the firm alone owned. The shape of the tool follows from that: factors abstract enough to fit any management job, so that every job anywhere could land on the same scale.

The firm's name at the time of building was not established; "Hay Group" is the name it traded under until Korn Ferry acquired it in **2015** and retired the brand in **2018**.

## What It Cost

**The factors encode the organisation chart they were drawn from.** Steinberg's 1992 study of the Hay system argues that it "perpetuates gender-based occupational assumptions stemming from its origins in the 1940s and 1950s" and "consistently values male-dominated management functions over nonmanagement functions more likely to be performed by women." To the extent Accountability is scaled by the money a job answers for, it rewards the jobs that controlled budgets in 1951.

The other trade-off was access. The method is proprietary; the charts and their calibration live with the consultancy, so the defensibility a client buys is partly the consultant's authority rather than a method anyone can inspect.

## What You Still Touch

Your salary band. The grade letter on an offer letter, the "job family and level" field in an HR system, and the pay-equity analysis that compares people "in comparable roles" all rely on someone having ranked the jobs first — and points-factor schemes of this kind are one of the standard ways that ranking is still done.
