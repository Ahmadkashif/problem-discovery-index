# Lineage: Small Law Firms

**Industry:** [[industries/small-law-firms|Small Law Firms (Solo and 2-10 Attorney Practices)]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-03-pc-spreadsheet|3 — PC & the Spreadsheet]]
**The tool:** the daily Time Record — the single-sheet form printed in the ABA's 1958 pamphlet *The 1958 Lawyer and His 1938 Dollar*, posted to a client ledger and priced against a 1,300-hour year
**Builder:** American Bar Association
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

A lawyer's income was falling behind, and no small office knew what its work cost.

Over the previous quarter-century, the pamphlet says, incomes of dentists rose 83 percent and physicians 157 percent, while "the income of lawyers in private practice has risen a mere 58%." By its 1954 figure, more than half of US lawyers netted less than $7,382, so "the average lawyer in this country does not receive a living wage." Its verdict: "lawyers, for all their training, are the worst managers of money."

**The constraint was the absence of a record.** Nothing connected the hours a matter consumed to the fee it earned, so a practitioner could not tell which work paid and which lost money.

## What Got Built

A pad of paper, and arithmetic.

From 365 days the pamphlet subtracts 105 — Sundays, half of Saturdays, holidays, vacation, sickness and meetings — leaving 260 fee-earning days. At five chargeable hours a day that is **"Total net fee earning hours per year per lawyer … 1300."** Divided into a $20,800 target income, that means $16.00 an hour.

Then it prints a **TIME RECORD**, "a simple form which may be utilized as a pad of single sheets or in a loose-leaf or bound ledger": date, lawyer, file number, client and matter, services described "in sufficient detail." Its instruction: "At the end of the day, total time spent on each item should be summarized. This task may be entrusted to the secretary." The totals were posted to each client's ledger as hours and dollar value, then compared with the fee actually charged "to ascertain loss or gain" — by matter, by type of practice and by client.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The American Bar Association, through its Special Committee on Economics of Law Practice — and the audience says why.

The pamphlet is "directed to the individual practitioner, and partnerships of two or three," which it says covered the great majority of independent lawyers. A large firm had bookkeepers; a solo had nobody whose job was cost. **The one body with standing to tell every small practitioner to run his office as a business was the bar itself**, and it did so apologetically: lawyers "hesitate to utilize techniques that smack of commercial enterprise." That reading is this note's inference.

Note what the form was *for*. The same pamphlet calls on bar associations to adopt "minimum fee schedules" and encourage "compliance therewith", and lists the ABA's "Minimum Fee Kits" among its resources. **The time record was cost accounting built to sit beside a fee schedule, not replace it.** Then the Supreme Court struck the schedules down as price-fixing in *Goldfarb v. Virginia State Bar* (1975), and hourly billing, by one law-review account, "really took hold." The record that measured the fee became the fee.

## What It Cost

**The pamphlet warned against its own descendant.** "Care must be taken, however, not to over-itemize the bill or show fees on an hourly basis since this affords too much latitude for client dissatisfaction over small details." The ledger's time columns, it says, "are for cost-analysis only and do not become part of the financial records." An internal instrument became the external invoice, and the dissatisfaction it predicted became an industry of line-item bill review.

And it fixed the capture method: write it down, total it at day's end. The pamphlet's own case for the form — it "catches those small and sundry items, such as telephone calls, which might otherwise escape his notice" — is the failure the vault still measures.
