## The Problem That Came First

A health club sells a membership once and has to collect it every month.

The economics of a gym depend on that asymmetry. The sale happens on a single visit, often under pressure at the front desk; the revenue arrives in small monthly amounts over a year or more, from members who mostly stop coming long before they stop owing. Every month the club must chase hundreds of small receivables from people who have lost interest in the product.

A club owner is good at selling memberships and running a floor. Running a receivables department is a different business, and doing it inside each club — at the same desk that sells the memberships — is where the money leaked.

## What Got Built

A billing company whose only customer, at first, was a gym chain.

**American Billing Company** took over the collection of members' dues from the clubs themselves: it held the member accounts, billed them monthly and managed the receivables. In its founder's words, it would "not only bill the members, we had the software to keep up with the usage", and it added point-of-sale and inventory. Renamed **ABC Financial Services**, it later extended into front-desk check-in, class scheduling, trainer appointments and online registration.

The shape is the point: **the billing account came first and the club software was built around it**, not the other way round. By the 2018 Arkansas Business Hall of Fame citation ABC served over 7,000 clubs, including Anytime Fitness, Planet Fitness and Gold's Gym, and collected over $400 million a month.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Jim Bottin, because he was the customer.

Bottin started by teaching taekwondo near Jacksonville Air Force Base in Arkansas, buying into a martial-arts centre with $1,200 borrowed from his parents. He put fitness equipment into one of his studios, "and it seemed to really take off." At his peak he ran **13 clubs across Arkansas and Texas**.

The pain was collection. "We'd sell hundreds of memberships, and trying to collect money at the club and the companies out there doing that just weren't adequate." So **in 1981** he started American Billing Company, whose "only job was to serve as receivables management for my health clubs."

Why him and not a bank or a software house: a bank sees a gym's member dues as tiny, high-default consumer receivables not worth servicing individually, and a software house would sell the club a ledger and leave the chasing to the club. Bottin owned the losses. Building the collector was cheaper for him than tolerating them, and once it worked for 13 clubs it worked for other people's clubs. He sold the company to Thoma Bravo in early 2018.

## What It Cost

Outsourcing the collection moved the gym's attention from whether a member comes to whether a member pays.

A club that is paid monthly by a billing company regardless of attendance has, on the ledger, no reason to notice the member who has stopped coming — and a quiet reason not to. The same contract language that makes dues reliable made cancellation hard, and cancellation became the industry's consumer-protection problem.

## What You Still Touch

If your gym membership is billed by a company whose name is not the gym's, and cancelling means a letter or a phone line rather than a button, you are dealing with the 1981 separation of selling from collecting.
