# Lineage: Digital Audio Platforms

**Industry:** [[industries/digital-audio-platforms|Digital Audio Platforms]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-05-commercial-web|5 — The Commercial Web]]
**The tool:** the pro-rata ("streamshare") royalty pool — the monthly formula that divides a subscription service's rights-holder payout by each owner's share of all streams on the service
**Builder:** unknown — searched, not established
**Builder in vault:** n/a
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

A flat monthly fee had to be turned into payments for millions of recordings.

A download store never faced this: each sale was one price for one track, and the label's share followed the sale. An all-you-can-eat subscription broke that link. The listener paid the same amount whether they played three songs or three thousand, so there was no per-track price to split. Something had to convert one pile of subscription money into a payment per rights holder, every month, across an entire licensed catalogue — and it had to be computable by the service and auditable by the labels.

## What Got Built

A pool and a ratio.

Each month the service sets aside the rights holders' share of its revenue. It counts every qualifying stream on the platform — on Spotify, a play of at least 30 seconds — and pays each rights holder the pool multiplied by that holder's fraction of total streams. The "per-stream rate" artists quote is a result of that division, not a price anyone agreed in advance.

The formula does not sit alone. As Tim Quirk, who helped launch Rhapsody in 2001, has described label deals, they typically contain several ways to calculate what is owed — revenue share, per-subscriber minimums and others — and the service pays whichever is highest. The pro-rata pool is the revenue-share leg of that "greater-of" contract.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**Not established.** The first on-demand subscription with unlimited access to a large licensed catalogue is usually credited to **Rhapsody**, from Listen.com, launched in 2001. A search-result summary attributes to a secondary article the claim that the pro-rata model has served streaming since Rhapsody got its licence in December 2001, but I could not open the page to confirm the wording, and no documentary source was given. Quirk's own account is that Rhapsody paid **a penny per play** at launch — a fixed rate, which is not a pool. Both cannot describe the same mechanism unless the penny was a floor inside a greater-of deal, and I could not find the licence terms that would settle it.

What the record does support is the *reason* the pool suits the parties. Each major label owns a large share of total listening. Paying by share of all streams lets a label check its cheque against a single number — its market share — without auditing individual users. It also resembles the pooled distribution that performing-rights organisations had long used for broadcast income. Both are motives, not a design authorship; the negotiating record is private.

So the artefact is specific and universal, and its designer is unknown. That is recorded here as a finding.

## What It Cost

**A listener's money does not follow that listener's listening.**

Because the pool is divided by total streams, a subscriber who plays one niche artist all month funds mostly the artists that *other* subscribers play heavily. Heavy listeners' tastes set everyone's payouts.

It also made streams, not listeners, the unit of value — and therefore the thing to manufacture. In March 2014 the band Vulfpeck released *Sleepify*, ten silent tracks just over 30 seconds long, and asked fans to loop it overnight; it earned roughly $19,655 from about 5.5 million streams before Spotify removed it on 26 April 2014. Stream-farming fraud is the industrial version. The remedies arrive as patches on the pool rather than replacements of it: SoundCloud moved some payouts to a user-centric "fan-powered" model on 1 April 2021, and from 2024 Spotify excluded tracks with fewer than 1,000 streams in the prior year from the pool entirely.

## What You Still Touch

Every royalty statement showing a fraction of a cent per stream is the pool divided by the platform's total, not a price for the song.
