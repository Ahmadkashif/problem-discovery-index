## The Problem That Came First

A captive agent fills in one company's paperwork. An independent agent fills in everybody's.

That is the whole difference, and by the late 1960s it was the independent agency system's largest operating cost. An agency representing a dozen carriers held a dozen application forms for one class of risk, each asking the same forty facts — square footage, payroll, prior losses, protection class — in a different order, under different headings, with different definitions of what counted as a loss. Quoting one risk to four markets meant writing the facts out four times, by hand, with four chances to transcribe a number wrong.

**And the friction was not accidental.** A carrier's proprietary forms were a switching cost paid by the agency's staff. Whichever company's paperwork a CSR knew cold got the submission first — so the form was quietly doing the work of a distribution strategy. Standardisation was worth a great deal to agents and rather less to the companies whose forms were winning.

## What Got Built

A common form library, maintained centrally, that carriers agree to accept in place of their own.

**ACORD was founded in 1970**, originally as Agent Company Operations Research and Development, and its first job was exactly that: collapse the carriers' proprietary new-business and claims forms into one set of numbered documents. They are still in daily use: **ACORD 125**, the commercial application, carrying the applicant information every market needs, with line-specific supplements behind it (126 general liability, 140 property); and **ACORD 25**, the certificate of liability insurance, summarising coverage on one page for a third party who is not the insured. 

From the late 1970s ACORD extended the idea from paper to wire, publishing electronic standards to move policy and claims data between carriers and agency systems.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

A neutral body jointly constituted by agents *and* carriers — the only structure that could have worked.

A carrier standardising alone hands away its own switching cost and gains nothing until everyone follows. An agents' association publishing a form on its own authority has published a document no underwriter is obliged to read, and a submission on an unaccepted form is not one. **The artefact needed buy-in from the party suffering the cost and the party imposing it, at once** — which requires a standing organisation both sides sit inside and fund, with authority to version the forms rather than freeze them.

That the carriers agreed is the interesting part. An insurer writing through independent agencies competes for *shelf space* in an office it does not own. Making itself easy to quote was worth more than making itself hard to leave.

## What It Cost

The certificate became a legally hollow document that generates unbounded work.

ACORD 25 states on its own face that it is issued as a matter of information only, confers no rights on the certificate holder, and does not amend the policy. Everyone knows this. They demand it anyway, because it is the only universally recognised artefact that looks like proof — and because it proves nothing, they demand a fresh one at every renewal, project and change of holder. A standard built to reduce paperwork produced the largest source of unpaid servicing volume in a modern agency.

The electronic standards carried a second asymmetry. Download moved policy data one way, carrier to agency system; nothing standardised the path back, so quoting and submission stayed manual. An agency with fully automated download still has staff rekeying the same risk into carrier portals.

## What You Still Touch

Sign a commercial lease, a subcontract or a vendor agreement and you will be asked for a certificate of insurance. It will be an ACORD 25, and somewhere a CSR is producing it unpaid.
