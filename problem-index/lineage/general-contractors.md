## The Problem That Came First

A general contractor pays for a building long before the owner owns one.

Subcontractors and suppliers bill monthly. The owner wants a finished structure. Between them sits the progress payment: each month the contractor is paid for the part that exists. That needs a number nobody can count directly — how much of a half-built building is built — and someone the owner trusts to vouch for it.

The American Institute of Architects had been writing owner–contractor paper since the **Uniform Contract of 1888**, produced with the National Association of Builders, and published its first standard General Conditions in **1911**. The current General Conditions, A201, describe the loop. The contractor submits a **schedule of values** "allocating the entire Contract Sum to the various portions of the Work." Before each progress payment it submits "an itemized Application for Payment prepared in accordance with the schedule of values." The architect then has seven days to certify it in full, in part, or not at all.

## What Got Built

Two sheets of paper that are a spreadsheet.

**G703, the Continuation Sheet**, is a grid with columns A to I: item number, description, scheduled value, work completed from the previous application and this period, materials presently stored, total completed and stored to date, percent, balance to finish, retainage. The headers print their own formulas — **(D + E)**, **(D+E+F)**, **(G ÷ C)**, **(C – G)**. Its copyright line begins in **1963**, sixteen years before VisiCalc; only the 1992 edition was read, so when the formulas first appeared is unknown.

**G702, the Application and Certificate for Payment**, rolls the grid into nine lines, from original contract sum and net change by change orders down to retainage, current payment due and balance to finish. The contractor signs a notarised statement on the left; the architect certifies on the right. Its copyright line begins in **1953** and lists seven editions, the last in 1992.

This vault's history note left both dates unverified; the copyright lines settle the edition years, though not who drafted the forms.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The American Institute of Architects, because half the form is the architect's.

The title is *Application **and** Certificate*. Under the AIA's General Conditions, the architect turns a contractor's claim into an owner's obligation: issuing a certificate, A201 says, is a representation "that the Work has progressed to the point indicated" and that its quality conforms. The G702's right-hand block does that in the architect's words, "based on on-site observations and the data comprising this application."

A contractors' association could have printed an invoice. **Only the body that wrote the architect's duty to certify could write the form the certificate is issued on** — and by 1953 it had been writing the surrounding contract for sixty-five years. That is this note's reading of the documents, not a recorded AIA rationale.

It is also a product: the G702 still sells as a paper 50-pack from the AIA's shop, and inside the AIA's ACD5 software.

## What It Cost

**Progress became a percentage.** The form does not measure installed quantity. It measures dollars — column G over column C — against line items the contractor proposed at the start. Ninety percent billed against seventy inspected, the vault's example, is a monthly argument, and the architect certifies only "to the best of the Architect's knowledge, information and belief."

**And the form counts only what has been agreed.** Its change-order summary has two lines: changes "approved in previous months by Owner" and "approved this month". A change still in dispute has no line on it.

## What You Still Touch

The pay app is still due every month, and contractors' websites host their own G703 as an .xls file.
