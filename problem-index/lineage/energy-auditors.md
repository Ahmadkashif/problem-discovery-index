## The Problem That Came First

The houses were losing heat through places nobody could see.

In **1977** Princeton's Center for Environmental Studies was retrofitting recently built townhouses at Twin Rivers, New Jersey, and the heat-loss arithmetic did not close. The cause turned out to be the party walls: **hollow cinder blocks linking basement, living floors and attic**, carrying warm air straight past the attic insulation. The published finding was that these "unexpected heat-transfer mechanisms bypass the attic insulation," and predicting retrofit savings required a three-zone model.

Finding it took infrared cameras, smoke tests and **tracer gas** — sulphur hexafluoride released and sampled. That yields an air-change rate for a research house, not a way for a contractor to find and fix leaks in the next house on the street.

## What Got Built

A fan in a door frame. Blow air out and the house pulls air in through every crack at once, where a hand, smoke pencil or infrared camera can find it; the airflow needed to hold the pressure difference is the leakage.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**Read down the builder column: researchers first, then three small instrument makers — one of them co-founded by a working auditor.**

Princeton had the reason: its models had missed the bypass, and it needed a field test faster than tracer gas. Team member Ken Gadsby reportedly set the lower panel's height by his own inseam.

The Energy Conservatory began over lunches between Gary Anderson, an auditor in St. Paul, and Gary Nelson, an engineer at the Minnesota Energy Agency. They turned a two-storey garage into a calibration chamber and went after weight and cost. Nelson later described the fix for sealing the fan into any doorway, four slotted L-shaped plywood pieces, as "simple, light, cheap and worked really well." **The research instrument became a trade tool when an auditor and a state energy engineer designed it for someone else's van.**

## What It Cost

**The door measures the house at a pressure it never sees.** Energy models want leakage under real weather.

The bridge is a rule of thumb: divide air changes at 50 Pa by 20. It is credited to Kronvall in 1978 and to Persily, and it came from tracer-gas results on a limited set of Swedish and American houses. Researchers note it lacks rigorous validation and ignores building type and climate.

And it gives one number for the whole house. The model is fed the total; where the leak is, and whether it bypasses insulation, stays in the auditor's head.

## What You Still Touch

Every audit report quotes a CFM50 figure. Behind it sits a 1978 rule of thumb and a plywood frame from a Minnesota garage.
