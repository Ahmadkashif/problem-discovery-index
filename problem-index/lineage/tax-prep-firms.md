## The Problem That Came First

The preparer already had a computer. The IRS still wanted paper.

In the IRS's own 2011 account, "efficient tax collection was becoming more difficult" in the 1980s "because of the complex, time-consuming and error-prone process of converting paper returns and information documents into a form that could be processed by machine." Storing the paper was itself becoming costly. And "even as tax practitioners were starting to use computers to prepare clients' taxes, the IRS still forced them to print and mail returns."

**A return computed on a PC was printed, mailed and turned back into machine form at a service centre** — the conversion paid for twice. For the preparer's client the cost was time: before e-filing, refunds took "on average two to three months", per Wikipedia's account.

## What Got Built

A modem, a tape drive and a telephone.

The idea came from the IRS Research Division, which tested an "Electronic Filing System (EFS)". The 1986 pilot enlisted **five tax preparers in three metropolitan areas — Cincinnati, Raleigh-Durham and Phoenix** — and took 25,000 returns. It "could only process returns that were due refunds."

The mechanics were manual. The preparer phoned the Cincinnati Service Center, where an IRS employee plugged the phone into a Mitron — "a modem with a tape drive." The tape went to a "Zylog, a super mini-computer," which turned the data into files the IRS's Unisys system could process. The acknowledgement came back the same way, by a return call.

It scaled quickly: 78,000 returns from 66 preparers in seven cities in 1987, with Direct Deposit added; 583,000 in 1988, on an IBM Series/1 that no longer needed a hand on the phone; nationwide in 1990, with 4.2 million.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The IRS, because the paper cost was the IRS's to pay.

Conversion, keying and storage sat inside its service centres. Preparers were not asking for relief: "Tax preparers were not buying any of it," a former IRS systems chief recalled. "Most people figured it was a plot to capture additional information for audits." The e-file project office was conscripted into marketing, with a promotional video built on the *Mission Impossible* theme.

**It ran through preparers because preparers already held the return in machine form, in volume** — the one party that could transmit what the IRS wanted to stop keying. That is this note's inference; the IRS's own account credits the "tremendous boost" to H&R Block and Jackson Hewitt agreeing to try it. An industry profile says Block filed 22,000 returns electronically from Cincinnati and Phoenix in 1986 — most of the pilot, if both figures hold.

## What It Cost

**The IRS did not end transcription. It moved it.** What the service centres stopped doing — turning "information documents into a form that could be processed by machine" — is what this vault's preparers now do in season: keying Box 1 wages and 1099 payers from photographed W-2s and emailed PDFs, at 30–40% of their time. The return became electronic; the documents feeding it did not.

The refund-only start also made the refund the product. Refund anticipation loans grew alongside e-file. Wikipedia credits their start to Ronald Smith, a Virginia Beach accountant, in 1985, and cites a 1989 report that Block doubled its business across 4,000-plus offices through the loan. In August 2010 the IRS announced it would stop giving preparers and lenders the "debt indicator", which flagged refunds likely to be offset, from the 2011 season; by 2013 major banks had stopped offering the loans.
