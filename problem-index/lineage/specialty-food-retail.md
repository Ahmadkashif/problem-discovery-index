## The Problem That Came First

A counter that sells by the pound does arithmetic on every sale.

A can has a price. A slab of cheese has a weight, known only when it lands on the pan, and a rate per pound. The clerk multiplies while the customer waits, and every slip comes out of the margin or the customer's goodwill.

**The unit of sale is decided at the counter, not printed at the factory.**

## What Got Built

**A scale that reads out money instead of pounds.**

Julius E. Pitrat of Gallipolis, Ohio patented his "Weighing and Price Scale" on **March 31 1885** — "a scale in which the total price of merchandise in quantity may be quickly and accurately determined, the price per pound being given." A second patent, in 1886, fitted the mechanism to ordinary platform scales so the value could be read "without having recourse to arithmetical computation."

Ninety years later the machine learned to print a barcode. A weighed package has no manufacturer's code, so the UPC reserved the leading digit **2** for variable-weight goods: an item number *assigned by the store*, plus weight or price. Hobart's engineers at the old Dayton Scale plant built the weigh-wrap-label system that put that code on a meat tray, and in June 1974 Hobart printed the first in-store UPC label at the Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio — site of the first UPC scan that month.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**Pitrat designed for butchers; two Dayton investors made it a product.** Builder is keyed to their company.

An Endicott History and Heritage Center exhibit on IBM says Pitrat "designed them specifically for the meat industry." In **1891** Edward Canby and Orange O. Ozias, Dayton businessmen, bought his patents and incorporated the Computing Scale Company. Why these two, I could not find.

The case held. A 1922 pamphlet from its successor names grocery stores, meat markets and delicatessens as customers and claims the scales "protected the merchant's profit" — the promise Dayton's other counter machine made about the till ([[lineage/independent-retailers|Lineage: Independent Retailers]]).

**The key is not IBM.** The company merged into the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company in 1911 — IBM from 1924 — but IBM inherited the scale rather than building it, and by Wikipedia's account it interested Thomas Watson least. In **June 1934** IBM's stockholders voted to sell the domestic Dayton Scale Division — 350 employees, about $2.5 million in annual sales — to Hobart Manufacturing of Troy, Ohio.

Hobart began by motorising meat grinders and coffee mills — equipment for the same counters. So when the scanner arrived, a meat-room supplier, not a scanner maker, worked out how to barcode goods with no fixed size.

## What It Cost

**The scale computes a price, not an identity.**

Weight times rate equals money; what the item *is* stays in the clerk's head. The prefix-2 label kept the split. A can of soup scans as the same product in every store; a wedge of cheese scans as a number meaningful only inside one shop, attached to a dollar figure.

That is the mechanism under this vault's origin-layer finding that fresh, variable-weight goods "produced worse data": store-assigned records cannot be pooled. Chains patched commodity meat in **1984**, when the National Live Stock and Meat Board, the Food Marketing Institute and the American Meat Institute created weight-item UPCs matched to standard cut names. Trade associations now administer such numbers for other fresh categories; I could not establish that any reaches one small producer's cheese.

## What You Still Touch

The deli label on a wrapped wedge — net weight, price per pound, total, barcode — is Pitrat's multiplication, printed. Behind it, the shop's sales history for that wedge sits under a number the shop made up — a thin base to forecast fresh stock from.
