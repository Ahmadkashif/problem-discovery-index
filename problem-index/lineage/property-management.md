## The Problem That Came First

Before the 1970s, a residential lease was treated in law largely as a transfer of land, not a contract for a place to live.

That had two consequences for anyone managing rentals. The landlord owed little about the condition of the unit, and the tenant's promise to pay rent stood on its own whatever state the apartment was in. And the security deposit — the money a manager actually handles at move-out — sat in the landlord's hands under almost no rule at all: no cap, no deadline, no requirement to say what had been kept or why.

The courts started to move first. On 7 May 1970, in *Javins v. First National Realty Corp.*, the D.C. Circuit, in an opinion by Judge J. Skelly Wright, held that a warranty of habitability was implied by law into urban residential leases. But a federal appeals court in Washington binds nobody in Kansas. **Landlord–tenant law is state law, and fifty legislatures would have to change it one at a time.**

## What Got Built

A model statute a legislature could enact whole.

The Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, promulgated in 1972, rewrote the residential tenancy as a contract with obligations on both sides. The part a property manager runs into every week is the security-deposit section, and its mechanism survives nearly word for word in the states that took it up. In Kansas, which enacted its version in 1975, a landlord may not take more than **one month's periodic rent** for an unfurnished unit; must return the balance within **14 days** of determining the deductions and never more than 30 days after the tenancy ends; and a landlord who fails to comply owes **1½ times the amount wrongfully withheld**. Arizona's version requires an **itemized list of all deductions** within fourteen business days and doubles the damages.

The structure is the same everywhere it landed: a cap, an itemization, a deadline, a multiplier.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws — now styled the Uniform Law Commission — is a body of lawyers appointed by each state to draft model acts for areas where varying state law causes trouble. It had co-sponsored the Uniform Commercial Code with the American Law Institute.

**Why them:** the reform had momentum in the courts and among tenant advocates, but no single body could legislate it. The Conference was the only institution whose product was, by design, a bill any state could introduce without redrafting. Its commissioners are appointed by the states themselves, which gave a model act credibility with legislatures that an advocacy draft would not have had.

The Conference's own catalogue says the 1972 Act was adopted in 21 states. In 2015 it approved a Revised Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, which adds provisions on security deposits, tenants' abandoned property, and ending a lease after domestic violence or sexual assault.

## What It Cost

**It turned the end of every tenancy into an accounting deadline with a penalty attached.** The itemized list protects tenants from silent keeping of deposits; it also means every move-out must produce a written, defensible, line-by-line damage ledger within two weeks, or the landlord risks paying out a multiple of what was withheld.

Uniformity was also partial. With 21 adopters and every other state writing its own rules, a manager operating across state lines faces different caps, clocks and multipliers — the opposite of what a uniform act is for.
