## The Problem That Came First

Every brokerage did the same research, separately.

To price a lease or a sale, a commercial broker needed to know what was vacant and what had recently traded nearby. Nobody published it. In one company history's words, brokers "compiled their own information, at great cost, essentially calling one another to share information." Every firm large enough ran a research staff to make those calls, and every firm's staff was ringing the same people for the same facts.

So market knowledge was a cost each brokerage paid in full, and a small broker's coverage stopped wherever their contacts did.

## What Got Built

A database of buildings with someone employed to keep it current.

The design was to call every broker in the market once, centrally, and sell the result to all of them. CoStar's first core product covered office buildings and was **distributed on floppy disks**. Florance wrote software to take in data from building owners and brokerages. Revenue was **$30,000 in 1989** and passed $500,000 in 1990.

It shipped alongside a printed quarterly leasing guide, *Cornerstone*, first published in 1989. **In 1993 the company sold the print guide to its rival Black's** and bet everything on electronic delivery. In **2000** it moved the whole database to the web and stopped shipping CDs. Field researchers photographing buildings came later.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Andrew Florance, and the reason is that he held three qualifications at once.

He grew up in the real-estate business, took an economics degree at Princeton in the mid-1980s, got a real-estate licence, and "made money writing software." After graduating he "quickly grew frustrated with the quality of real estate information that was available." He started in his parents' basement laundry room; his first venture sold ten laser-printed copies of compiled records at $800 each.

The business case was not a better valuation formula. It was **removing a duplicated cost**. If one party did the calling, every brokerage could cut its research staff and still get better data for less. Presumably no brokerage could have played that role, since its rivals would have been feeding a competitor. Florance, independent and backed by Washington lawyer Michael Klein, could.

He also knew what would kill it: if he stayed in Washington–Baltimore, "his success would breed his own destruction." So he skipped secondary markets and went for the largest ones, where later entrants would find the cost of entry prohibitive. **In 1994 he entered New York by buying Space Data Graphics.** The company, Realty Information Group, took the CoStar Group name after its July 1998 IPO.

## What It Cost

**The brokers traded a shared phone habit for a subscription.** The information they once swapped for nothing, they now supply to one company and rent back. They share it, in one account's phrase, "in hopes of generating more activity as their limited networks weren't sufficient."

The shape that won — a proprietary corpus built market by market — is defended in court. CoStar has sued LoopNet (2004, over photographs, where the court found LoopNet not directly liable), Xceligent (filed 2016, ending in a **$500 million judgment and permanent injunction**) and CREXi (2020). It bought LoopNet in 2012 for $860 million. The duplicated research cost of 1987 was removed by concentrating it in one place, and that place charges for access.

## What You Still Touch

A small broker who cannot justify the subscription does what everyone did before 1989: pulls deed records, remembers their own deals, and phones other brokers. The vault prices the subscription at $8,000–$25,000 per user a year and records that the rural broker "rel[ies] on personal networks."
