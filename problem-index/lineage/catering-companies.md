## The Problem That Came First

A caterer cooks in one place and serves in another. Food leaves the kitchen hot, travels, waits for a room to fill and then has to stay safe and edible for as long as the guests keep coming back. A restaurant never faces this: the pass is ten feet from the stove. A caterer's stove is across town.

The venue — a hall, a tent, an office atrium, a backyard — usually has no kitchen line to lend, and often no gas or spare circuits where the buffet will stand. Whatever holds the food hot has to arrive with the food, work with no utilities, and be cheap enough to leave behind.

## What Got Built

A can of fuel that is its own burner.

Sterno is jellied denatured alcohol — ethanol denatured with methanol, water and a gelling agent, with a pink safety dye added in recent decades — sold in a can and lit in place. A standard 7-ounce can burns for about two hours. Set under a chafing dish's water pan, it turns a steel frame and a rectangle of hot water into a holding station anywhere a table can stand.

The institutional chafing dish it heats is, in Wikipedia's description, large covered rectangular pans, sometimes disposable, in a rack over water heated by an alcohol burner — "for buffet service", not for cooking. That is the whole catering buffet line: a gel can, a water bath and a pan.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

S. Sternau & Co. of Brooklyn, founded in 1893 — and it was not built for caterers.

Sternau made chafing dishes, coffee percolators and similar small appliances. The brand name is its own: the company applied it to its "Sterno-Inferno" alcohol burner. This was the era of the home chafing-dish supper — fashionable enough in the 1880s and 1890s that Fannie Farmer published *Chafing Dish Possibilities* in Boston in 1898, when hosts dazzled guests with flambéing at the table.

**So the business case is the appliance-maker's, not the caterer's.** A company selling dishes that need a flame has every reason to sell the flame, too, in a form a household could buy by the can. By 1918 it was marketing a Sterno Stove as a gift for a soldier going overseas — a portable flame for anyone without a kitchen. Caterers were one more customer with no kitchen at the point of service.

I could not establish when the jellied can itself first went on sale, nor when or why commercial caterers standardised on it; neither date is in any source I reached.

## What It Cost

Canned heat solved holding by giving up control.

The can has no thermostat. It burns at one rate until it is spent, roughly two hours, so a long reception means someone watching flames and swapping cans mid-service. The food sits over a water bath whose temperature depends on the fuel, the room and how often the lid is lifted — which is why the menu for an off-site event is designed around dishes that survive a water pan, not around what the kitchen does best.

And it put fire on the buffet table, in rooms that were never designed as kitchens.

The deeper trade is that the flame is the only part of the holding problem the can solves. The weight still has to be moved: hot pans into carriers, carriers into vans, vans to the venue and back again.

## What You Still Touch

Every wedding buffet, conference lunch and office party with a row of steel chafers and a small blue flame under each runs on the Sterno model. The labour it leaves — loading, hauling, swapping, breaking down — lands on the crew at the end of a long day.
