## The Problem That Came First

Singing a song in church was legal. Showing the congregation its words was not.

US copyright law carves worship out explicitly: 17 U.S.C. § 110(3) says performance of a religious musical work "in the course of services at a place of worship or other religious assembly" is not an infringement. So a church never needed permission to sing. But the exemption covers performance and display in the service, not **copies** — and the practical act of worship involves copies: a lyric typed into the Sunday bulletin, a photocopied songsheet, an overhead transparency, a songbook compiled for the youth group.

Hymnals had solved this for the old repertoire, because a publisher cleared the rights once and sold the book. Contemporary worship songs broke that model. They changed weekly, came from dozens of small publishers, and were reproduced locally by volunteers. To do it legally a church would have had to write to every rights-holder for every song, and no volunteer was going to. Howard Rachinski, looking at his own and neighbouring churches, concluded most of them were simply out of compliance.

## What Got Built

A single licence that replaced thousands of permission letters.

The Church Copyright License covers reproduction of the **words** of songs in the licensed catalogue for congregational use. The church pays one annual fee on a sliding scale by attendance — as of February 2017 from $59 for fewer than 25 people to $5,266 for more than 200,000 — and prints its licence number with each reproduced lyric. It deliberately excludes photocopying sheet music, choral octavos, musicals and instrumental parts; separate products later covered those, along with SongSelect (lyric and lead-sheet downloads) and a video licence run jointly with MPLC.

The other half of the machine is reporting. Churches record which songs they reproduce, and CCLI distributes most of the fee to publishers and songwriters as royalties in proportion, holding an annual owners' meeting in each region to account for it.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Howard Rachinski, and the reason is that he stood on both sides of the transaction.

He was a music minister at a large church in Portland, Oregon — the licensee's side — and built the prototype, **Starpraise Ministries, from May 1985**, spending about three and a half years on it before launching CCLI as a privately owned company in 1988. A publisher could not credibly offer a blanket licence covering its competitors' songs, and a denomination had no incentive to price reproduction for churches outside it. What the problem needed was a neutral clearing-house that publishers trusted to count and churches trusted to be cheap — the ASCAP model applied to copying rather than performance, sized for a volunteer to administer.

The later owner confirms the fit: CCLI is now owned by SESAC, the performing-rights organisation that moved its headquarters to Nashville in 1985 on the strength of its Christian-music business.

## What It Cost

**Reporting is the weak joint.** Royalties follow what churches report, and the reporting is done by the same volunteers who once ignored permission letters. A song that is sung but under-reported earns its writer less than it should.

The licence also fixes the unit of worship as the **word on a screen** — it prices lyrics, not arrangements, which is why a church's band still needs different permissions from its projector.

## What You Still Touch

Every projected lyric in a contemporary service carries a small "CCLI License #" line at the bottom of the slide. Worship-planning and church-management software carry the licence number as a field and export usage reports, so the licence is also one reason a church's administrative database exists in the shape it does.
