# Lineage: Language Schools

**Industry:** [[industries/language-schools|Language Schools]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-05-commercial-web|5 — The Commercial Web]]
**The tool:** SEVIS — the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System, the federal database through which every certified school issues the Form I-20 and reports each F-1 and M-1 student's enrollment, mandatory from 1 August 2003
**Builder:** Immigration and Naturalization Service
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

A student visa was a promise to attend a school, and nobody checked whether the promise was kept.

Before 2003 the paperwork was paper. A school approved to enrol foreign students issued a **Form I-20**, the certificate of eligibility for student status. Whether that student then turned up for class, dropped out after a week, or never arrived at all was known to the school and to almost no one else.

For a language school this gap was sharper than for a university. Intensive English programmes are short, start often and are the natural first stop for a student whose English is not yet good enough for a degree programme. The federal statute itself lists "language training schools" alongside flight and vocational schools as institutions whose students it wanted tracked.

## What Got Built

A central database that the school, not the student, has to keep current.

Congress mandated electronic collection of foreign-student information in **Section 641 of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996.** The INS answered with a pilot, **CIPRIS** — the Coordinated Interagency Partnership Regulating International Students — run from June 1997 to October 1999 at 21 schools across four southeastern states.

After September 2001 the pilot was renamed SEVIS and put on a deadline by the USA PATRIOT Act and the Enhanced Border Security and Visa Reform Act of 2002. It became operational for all incoming students on 15 February 2003; from 1 August 2003 no paper I-20 was valid. Schools must now issue every I-20 out of SEVIS and report enrollment within 30 days of the term's start, plus every status change and every no-show. From 1 September 2004 each prospective student has paid a $100 SEVIS fee.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The INS — built by its Immigration Services Division — because the thing being fixed was the government's inability to see its own visas, and only the government could compel schools to report.

The shape followed the enforcement problem, not the education one. The INS did not have staff at thousands of schools; the schools did. So SEVIS made each school's **Designated School Official** the data-entry point for a federal immigration record, and made the school's certification to enrol foreign students conditional on keeping that record accurate.

Language schools were squarely in the frame that finally forced the deadline. Hani Hanjour, one of the September 11 hijackers, first came to the United States in 1991 to study at the Center for English as a Second Language at the University of Arizona. Federal investigators concluded he re-entered in December 2000 on a student visa, on the strength of enrolment at an ELS Language Centers programme on the Holy Names College campus in Oakland. That case was cited in the push to put SEVIS into service.

## What It Cost

**The monitoring cost was moved onto the monitored.** The school pays in staff time for every report, and its eligibility to take foreign students at all rides on the accuracy of that work. A late report is a compliance failure for the school and a status problem for the student.

The launch cost was real too: schools reported system access failures and data-integrity problems in the first year, while the 1 August 2003 deadline for moving every continuing student stood.

For a small intensive English programme with frequent start dates, each student generates more reportable events per year than a four-year degree does — this note's inference from the reporting rules, not a published count.

## What You Still Touch

Every international student's I-20 is printed out of SEVIS, and a school official somewhere is updating it this week. The advisor in the vault's problem notes is that official.
