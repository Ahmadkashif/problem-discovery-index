## The Problem That Came First

A chiropractor treats with the hands, and the thing the hands treat — the vertebral subluxation — was, to the medical profession of the 1960s, not a real diagnosis.

That was the whole commercial problem. The American Medical Association's Committee on Quackery, formed in 1963, set out explicitly to keep Medicare from covering chiropractic. A profession whose central concept was disputed by the gatekeepers of health insurance had no route onto the claim form. Patients paid cash, and the largest payer in the country treated a chiropractic visit as outside medicine.

## What Got Built

Not a code set. A definition with a narrow door in it.

The 1972 amendments admitted chiropractors as "physicians" under Medicare Part B — **but only for treatment by means of manual manipulation of the spine to correct a subluxation.** Everything else a chiropractor orders or furnishes is excluded: examinations, therapies, and the X-rays themselves.

The door had a lock, and the key was a film. Until the end of 1999, the subluxation had to be **demonstrated by X-ray**. The chiropractor could take the X-ray and use it as documentation, but Medicare would not pay for it.

Effective for services on or after **January 1 2000**, the X-ray stopped being required and a physical examination could stand in. The manual now spells out what that examination must find — the test the profession calls **P.A.R.T.**: **P**ain or tenderness, **A**symmetry or misalignment, **R**ange-of-motion abnormality, **T**issue or tone change. Two of the four are required, and one of the two must be asymmetry or range of motion.

The last piece is a two-letter flag. A chiropractor must put the **AT modifier** — active/corrective treatment — on every claim for manipulation meant to improve the patient. Claims without it are treated as maintenance therapy and denied.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Congress, over the objection of organised medicine — which is why the benefit is a compromise rather than a design.

The statute had to reconcile two things at once: political pressure to include a licensed profession that millions of patients used, and a medical establishment that rejected its premise. The resulting shape can be read directly from the text. Coverage was granted, but for **one procedure only**; the procedure was tied to **one diagnosis**, the subluxation; and the diagnosis had to be **proven with an objective image** that medicine itself used. The X-ray was the price of admission — the concession that made a disputed condition auditable. *(This reading is inferred from the statute's structure; the legislative bargaining behind it was not traced this session.)*

The manual's uniform minimum standards date the practical rollout: they distinguish chiropractors trained before and after **July 1 1974**, adding a four-year, 4,000-hour course and training in X-ray for those who started later.

## What It Cost

**Maintenance care became unbillable by definition.** Medicare holds that once further clinical improvement cannot be expected and treatment becomes supportive rather than corrective, it is maintenance therapy and not medically necessary. A large share of what chiropractors actually do for chronic patients falls on the wrong side of that line.

And the benefit made documentation, not treatment, the thing that gets paid. Every visit must show a subluxation, an improvement trajectory and an AT modifier. When the X-ray went in 2000, the proof burden did not disappear — it moved into the note.

## What You Still Touch

Every chiropractic SOAP note written today carries four letters it rarely names: pain, asymmetry, range of motion, tissue. The note exists to prove that this visit is corrective, not maintenance.
