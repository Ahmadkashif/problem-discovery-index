# Lineage: Rideshare Fleet Operators

**The tool:** Xchange Leasing — Uber's wholly owned US subsidiary that leased cars to people so they could drive for Uber, financed through a dedicated $1.0 billion asset-backed credit facility from May 2016 and shut down from August 2017
**Builder:** Uber
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

A ride-hailing platform needs drivers, and a driver needs a car.

Uber's model moved the capital cost of a taxi fleet onto the workers. Anyone with a suitable car could drive, but that excluded many of the people most eager for the work: drivers with no car, an old car, or credit too weak to finance a new one. **Every would-be driver without a qualifying vehicle was lost supply** at a time when supply decided who won each city.

Taxi fleets had long rented cabs to drivers by the shift or week. What was new was a platform that could see exactly how much each renting driver earned, week by week.

## What Got Built

A leasing company inside the platform.

Uber's IPO registration statement (S-1, April 2019) describes Xchange Leasing as "our vehicle leasing business in the United States." It was financed off Uber's own balance sheet. **In May 2016 a wholly owned subsidiary took on a $1.0 billion secured asset-based revolving credit facility** (the "2016 XCL Revolving Credit Facility") at LIBOR plus 3%, secured on the leased cars. Uber was not a guarantor and the lenders had no recourse to Uber itself.

The filings put Xchange in a wider "Vehicle Solutions" line: "we lease or rent vehicles to third parties who could potentially use these vehicles to provide Ridesharing or Uber Eats services through our platform." Singapore had a sister business, Lion City Rentals, funded by its own SGD 590 million facility from October 2016.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Uber, because the platform was the one party that could see the driver's income and take payment from it.

A bank or rental firm leasing to a low-credit driver cannot see whether the car earns money. The platform can, and it pays the driver. **That was Uber's advantage as a lessor, and the product's structure depended on it.** A lessor who can see and draw on the lessee's income can lend to people others reject.

The reason for owning a lessor was supply, not leasing profit. Every leased car was a car on the Uber app. The S-1 shows how the business was treated: its results were reported inside Uber's "Core Platform" segment, not as a separate finance business.

## What It Cost

It failed, and Uber's own filing says why: "we discontinued certain products, such as Xchange Leasing … because we failed to operate it efficiently."

The timeline is in the financial statements. The facility was reduced from $1.0 billion to $750 million in January 2017 and to $500 million in June 2017. **In August 2017 Uber began reassessing Xchange and planned to exit.** It recorded a **$166 million impairment** on the leased vehicles. In January 2018 it sold the trust holding the cars and leases for about $104 million in cash and receivables, a $5 million convertible note and up to $20 million contingent on how the leases performed. It then repaid the $75 million left on the facility, and sold the remaining vehicles during 2018.

The lesson for the industry is the one the vault's problem notes describe. Lending against a driver's platform income only works if that income covers the weekly payment, and when it does not, the lessor holds a depreciating car and a lessee who is behind.

## What You Still Touch

Uber left the business. The independent fleet operators who stayed in it still rent at a fixed weekly rate, but without the one thing that made Uber's version work: sight of the driver's earnings. The data moat that let the platform lend is the same wall that now blocks everyone else from pricing a rental.
