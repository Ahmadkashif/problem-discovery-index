## The Problem That Came First

An esports organisation sells attention, and until 2018 it could not promise a sponsor it would still have any next season.

The League of Legends Championship Series began its first season on 7 February 2013 with eight teams, split across North American and European regional competitions. Places were earned and lost: a bad split sent a team into promotion and relegation, and a team that dropped out of the LCS dropped off the main broadcast — the only surface where its logo reached the whole audience.

So the organisation's one sellable asset — airtime in front of the league's viewers — could vanish on a best-of-five series. A sponsor could not sign a multi-year deal against it, an investor could not value it, and a salary set against expected sponsorship was a bet on the next match. Riot's own 2016 open letter, per Wikipedia, followed public argument over whether players and teams should get a bigger share of Riot's esports revenue.

## What Got Built

A seat instead of a place. In June 2017 Riot announced that the North American LCS would move "from a promotion and relegation system to franchised league with ten permanent teams beginning in 2018."

The slot bundled three things. **Permanence**: no relegation, so the team is in the broadcast every season it holds the slot. **A price**: a $10 million flat buy-in for existing League teams, plus $3 million more for new organisations. **Obligations**: a minimum player salary raised to US$75,000, a players' association, and a mandated academy squad feeding from the Challenger Series. Blizzard's Overwatch League, announced in November 2016, opened the same year on the same form, with twelve franchises paying $20 million each.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Riot Games, because in esports the league is not a coalition of clubs. It is a product owned by the game's publisher.

In traditional sports the teams formed the league and could sell it seats. Here Riot owned *League of Legends*, its broadcast rights and the competition itself, so only Riot could promise a team permanent broadcast presence — nobody else had anything to promise. The investor money it wanted to attract could only come in against a guarantee, and the guarantee was Riot's to issue.

That explains the shape. The fee is paid to the publisher; the salary floor and academy requirement are set by the publisher; the slot is a licence to appear in the publisher's product, not ownership of a share in it. Wikipedia summarises the rationale as stability for invested ownership groups rather than relegation-based competition.

## What It Cost

The slot converted a sporting risk into a financial one, and the licensor kept the power to change the terms.

In May 2023 Riot dropped the requirement for LCS teams to fund North American Challenger League academy rosters. Most franchises disbanded their development teams, affecting roughly 70 players; the LCS Players Association threatened a walkout on 31 May, the Summer Split was delayed two weeks, and an agreement on 8 June added severance and notice minimums. The Overwatch League showed the end state: a $6 million termination fee per team offered in July 2023, and the league folded on 23 January 2024.

The deeper trade-off: a permanent seat guaranteed airtime but not audience, and an organisation that bought certainty of appearance still could not prove to a sponsor what that appearance was worth.

## What You Still Touch

Every esports sponsorship deck still sells presence in a publisher's broadcast and still cannot say what it produced, and every roster is still priced against a slot whose terms someone else writes.
