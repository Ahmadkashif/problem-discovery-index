## The Problem That Came First

A private school sets one sticker price and then sells most seats below it. Deciding how far below, family by family, is the hardest pricing job in the building.

Every grant is a bet on a household's finances that the school cannot see: two incomes or one, a business, a house, siblings already in tuition elsewhere. Done alone, each school would need its own forms, its own verification and its own formula — and it would be negotiating blind against other schools courting the same child. The family, meanwhile, would fill out the same disclosures five times for five applications and could play one school's offer against another's.

For boarding schools drawing applicants nationally, both costs were acute. Aid was the lever that made a national applicant pool possible, and nobody had a common yardstick for it.

## What Got Built

A shared form and a shared formula.

The family completes **one Parents' Financial Statement** and sends it, with tax documents, to the service rather than to each school. The service applies a single need-analysis methodology — income, assets, household size, other children's tuition — and reports to each school the family designates what the family can be expected to pay. Each school then decides its own award, but from the same starting number.

The design choice that matters is that the **methodology is common and the award is local**. Schools keep their discretion; what they give up is the right to disagree about the arithmetic. That takes price competition out of need assessment and puts it back into the award decision, where schools can see it.

The service still runs. Its current operator describes a single application families use across multiple schools, direct IRS data authorisation, and a methodology NAIS continues to maintain.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The originator could not be named this session. The operator's own account says the service and its methodology **"began in the mid-1950's with a coalition of independent boarding schools that later entrusted NAIS to operate the service in 1968"** — and does not name the coalition or its members.

The why is clearer than the who. The schools that most needed a common yardstick were boarding schools: national applicant pools, overlapping admissions, and aid budgets large enough that uncoordinated bidding was expensive. A coalition was the only party that could build it, because a common formula is worthless unless the competitors themselves agree to it. No single school could impose one, and no vendor had the standing.

The National Association of Independent Schools, organised in 1962 from the merger of the Independent Schools Education Board and the National Council of Independent Schools, took over operation in 1968. That makes it the inheritor, not the originator, and under this sweep's keying rule the builder field records the coalition as unknown rather than crediting NAIS. In 2016 NAIS entered a collaboration with VenturEd Solutions, which now runs the platform as Ravenna Financial Aid while NAIS keeps stewardship of the methodology.

## What It Cost

**Schools traded pricing freedom for a common measure.** A formula that treats every family identically cannot see what a school most wants to know — how likely this family is to enrol, and at what price — so the need number and the yield decision drifted apart.

The form also made aid **a paperwork gate**. A family that will not disclose its finances, or cannot assemble the documents, receives nothing, however strong the case.

## What You Still Touch

Every tuition-and-aid office runs two numbers side by side: the family contribution the methodology computes, and the award the school actually makes. Reconciling them — across siblings, merit grants and payment plans — is the billing problem.
