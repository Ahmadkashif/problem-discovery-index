# Lineage: Electrical Contractors

**Industry:** [[industries/electrical-contractors|Electrical Contractors]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-06-cloud-saas|6 — Cloud & SaaS]]
**The tool:** the NECA Manual of Labor Units — a book assigning every item of electrical material a standard installation time, in Normal, Difficult and Very Difficult columns
**Builder:** National Electrical Contractors Association
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

An electrical bid is mostly a guess about hours.

Material can be priced from a supplier's sheet. Labour cannot: it depends on how much conduit, how many terminations, at what height, through what walls. On a competitively bid job the contractor who guesses low wins the work and loses money on it; the one who guesses high loses the work. Both outcomes were routine, and each contractor carried his hours in his own head or his own ledger.

That made the whole trade fragile in a specific way. **Every firm was pricing against the worst estimator in town**, because whoever underbid set the market rate for the job.

## What Got Built

A published table of labour units.

The **Manual of Labor Units** takes the installation of electrical material and breaks it into items — a length of conduit, a device box, a fitting — and gives each a time. NECA's own description is that "virtually every piece of electrical material has been analyzed and assigned a time frame for its installation." Each unit comes in three columns, **Normal, Difficult and Very Difficult**, so the estimator's judgement is reduced to choosing a column rather than inventing a number.

What the unit covers is spelled out: normal material handling, drawing study, measurement and layout, installation, and normal non-productive time. What it excludes is spelled out too — **supervision is not in the units**, and the contractor adds it separately.

The estimate becomes arithmetic: count, multiply, sum, apply a rate.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The contractors' own trade association, and the reason is that the problem was collective.

NECA was formed in 1901 at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, with a charter to foster trade among electrical contractors and "reform abuses." Its first Manual of Labor Units followed in **1923**. I could not establish who compiled that first edition or how its units were measured.

**Why an association rather than a firm:** a single contractor's labour data is a competitive secret and statistically thin. A shared book is neither. It gives every member the same defensible baseline, which narrows the spread between the careful bidder and the reckless one — exactly the spread that was destroying margins.

It also mattered that NECA sits across the table from the IBEW. Each local chapter negotiates the labour agreement with its union local. An association that bargains over the price of an hour has an obvious interest in owning the standard for how many hours a job takes.

The Wave 6 part came later and is not NECA's. The manual's units were carried into estimating software as its labour database, which is where most estimators now meet them. I did not establish which vendor first licensed or keyed in the MLU, and do not assert one.

## What It Cost

**A labour unit is an average, and an average is nobody's crew.**

The book knows the material, not the building: the occupied hospital, the ceiling full of existing work, the apprentice-heavy crew. Three columns are a coarse answer to that, and choosing among them is still judgement — the same judgement the manual was meant to replace, now hidden inside a column letter.

And because the units are shared, they flatten the bid. A contractor whose crews genuinely beat the book cannot easily prove it, and one whose crews lag it discovers so only at job close. The manual standardised the estimate; it never connected the estimate to what happened on site.

## What You Still Touch

Every commercial electrical bid you receive is still built from counted material times a unit time. The gap between the unit and the crew is the unsolved part, and it is where the vault's pain sits.
