## The Problem That Came First

A glasshouse is a heated box made of the material that loses heat fastest.

The grower must hold the air at the temperature and humidity the crop wants while the weather changes by the minute: vents open when sun loads the glass, shut when cloud passes, pipes up at dusk. By hand, that is someone walking the house turning valves, every correction late.

Heat is the bill. In 1973, by one supplier's account, greenhouses burned oil, coal or wood, and "large amounts of energy were lost through roofs and walls of the glass construction." This vault's own high-impact note puts energy at 20–30% of production cost for a US operation today.

## What Got Built

A box on the wall that reads sensors and moves actuators — temperature, humidity, light and wind in; heating valves, vents and screens out. It holds each variable to a **setpoint** the grower types in, and shifts setpoints by rule.

Not one invention — it came from European suppliers who already sold growers heat or materials:

| Year | Tool | Builder | Place | What it was for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1959** | Imported greenhouse heaters | Valk en Prins, renamed Priva in 1965 | De Lier, Netherlands | Safer, cleaner heat |
| **after 1973** | Isocryl — combined shading and energy screen | Reimann | Germany | Keeping heat in after the oil crisis |
| **1974** | TUCO climate computer — 150 built | Hoogendoorn | Netherlands — town not established | Claimed by its maker as the first greenhouse climate computer |
| **1977** | Priva's first climate or process computer | Priva | De Lier | Climate, energy and water from one controller |
| **1984** | Computer-integrated climate and irrigation control | Argus Control Systems | Surrey, British Columbia | Among the first to join the two |

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**The company that sold the grower heat learned to control it.** Priva began in 1959 as Valk en Prins, founded by Jan Prins and his uncle Cor Valk to import greenhouse heaters. Eighteen years later it shipped a computer to run them.

It happened in the Netherlands because that is where the glass was. In the Westland, south-west of The Hague, greenhouses spread on a large scale after 1900 until they covered about three-quarters of the horticultural area. Hoogendoorn's TUCO and Priva's computer arrived within three years of each other; North America's Argus came a decade later.

**The reason is inference, not a claim any source makes:** a heating supplier already knew the valves, the boiler and the grower, and a controller was the next thing to sell into the same boiler house. Heat first, control second, crop last — which is what the machine still does.

Priority is contested: Hoogendoorn claims TUCO was first; Priva claims the first to control climate, energy and water. Both are vendors' claims, unadjudicated here.

## What It Cost

**The climate computer holds the air to a number, and never learned what the number is for.**

It controls what its builders understood — pipes, vents, humidity. The crop is not in the loop. Whether a setpoint grew a better tomato or only burned more gas stays in the head grower's memory, and walks out when the grower retires.

Meanwhile it logs everything, minute by minute, in its vendor's format — what the air did, unjoined to what the plants did.

## What You Still Touch

A head grower still nudges setpoints several times a day on a vendor's screen, on judgement the controller records but cannot learn from.
