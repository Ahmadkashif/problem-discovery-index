## The Problem That Came First

A shop owner cannot stand at the till all day, and whoever stands there instead handles the owner's money unwatched.

Receipts went into an open drawer. What leaked out of it — through theft, honest error or scrambled bookkeeping — was invisible, because nothing recorded what should have gone in. The owner met it only as a loss in the books.

John H. Patterson met it that way. With his brother Frank he ran a miners' supply store at Coalton, Ohio, which lost money even though, he said, it had almost no competition and its prices were high. By one account, instead of an expected $12,000 profit it was losing $6,000 a year. His clerks were shortchanging the till.

## What Got Built

**A machine that makes every sale public.**

James Ritty, a Dayton saloonkeeper who suspected his staff of embezzlement, watched a counter record a steamship propeller's revolutions on a trip to Europe in 1878. With his brother John, a skilled mechanic, he built a machine worked by keys, each standing for a sum of money. It showed the amount on a dial facing the customer and rang a bell. They patented it on **November 4 1879**.

Under Patterson it gained what turned it into a shop's record: **a paper roll logging each transaction** — a journal for the owner's books, a receipt for the customer. In NCR's machines the clerk had to ring the sale to open the drawer. One history sums up its promise as "publicity, protection, and compulsory morality."

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**The inventor was a saloonkeeper; the builder of the shop register was a shopkeeper.** Builder is keyed to NCR for that reason.

Ritty found few buyers — about a dozen machines were in use when Patterson heard of them — and in 1881 sold out to investors including Jacob H. Eckert of Cincinnati, who formed the National Manufacturing Company.

Patterson bought a pair of registers at $50 each, sight unseen, from an advertisement. **In the next six months, by one account, the Coalton store turned a $5,000 profit.** He bought into the maker in the early 1880s, and in **1884 paid $6,500 for control**, renaming it the National Cash Register Company.

That business case dictated the artefact. Patterson had not bought a counting machine; he had bought proof that his clerks were stealing. So NCR's salesmen opened with "I have called to interest you in a way to increase your profits", and the machine was built to police the person at the counter — a public dial, a bell, a drawer that stays shut, a journal the owner can audit.

## What It Cost

**The register counts money, not goods.**

It answers one question — did the cash reach the drawer? — per transaction, in dollars. It records nothing about the shelf. A shop could ring every sale honestly and still not know what it had left, what to reorder, or what walked out unrung.

Counting goods at the till came later, through the scanner — the first UPC scan, in 1974, crossed an NCR scanner at a Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio — and the analytics that made item-level data pay were priced for chains with hundreds of stores, as this vault's origin layer records.

It also aimed the machine at the clerk. By one account NCR's "thief catcher" aroused fury among bartenders and café cashiers. A century later, the owner still does the reconciling.

Cashing up at close — counting the drawer against the day's total — is the question Patterson bought the register to answer, still asked nightly.
