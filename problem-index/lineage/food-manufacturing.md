## The Problem That Came First

A food plant cannot prove a batch safe by testing it.

Pathogens are rare and patchy. Confidence that a lot is clean takes heavy sampling, and every sample is destroyed by the test — the more certainty, the less food left to sell. Quality control nevertheless meant exactly that.

Spaceflight made that untenable. From the early 1960s Pillsbury was a NASA contractor making food for crewed missions — pieces that would not crumble into the electronics, and could not make an astronaut ill in orbit. NASA's Paul Lachance recalled that his team began by testing end products thoroughly, **"destroying one batch after another when irregularities were found."** Under the programme's strict pathogen limits, little of what was produced passed.

## What Got Built

**A way of proving safety from the process instead of the product.**

Take the process apart step by step and name the hazard at each. Find the few points where control decides the outcome — a cook temperature, a metal detector, a pH — and set a measurable limit there. Monitor it, write down every reading, decide in advance what happens when one fails, and check the records.

That is HACCP. It was presented to the food industry for the first time at the **first National Conference on Food Protection, in Denver, in April 1971.** In **1992** the National Advisory Committee on Microbiological Criteria for Foods set out the seven principles every plan still follows. What a plant actually holds is the written plan and, behind each critical control point, its log.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Pillsbury — a food manufacturer standing between two engineering cultures.

The work was shared. **Howard Bauman, a Pillsbury microbiologist, led the company's team;** Lachance led flight-food safety at NASA's Manned Spacecraft Center in Houston; and the system was built, with modification, on methods from the US Army's laboratories at Natick, Massachusetts. NASA and Natick brought reliability thinking; the plant operator knew where a line goes wrong.

What made it Pillsbury's rather than NASA's is what happened next. **After glass was found in Pillsbury's farina, the company put HACCP into its own plants, and had it in all of them by 1974.** When botulism in Bon Vivant's canned vichyssoise proved fatal in summer 1971 — and it emerged the FDA had not had an inspector in that plant in four years — the FDA asked Pillsbury to train its inspectors. A 21-day course, *Food Safety through the Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point System*, ran in **September 1972**, and in **1973 low-acid canners became the first to be regulated on HACCP lines.**

Bauman's summary: quality control "had been based primarily on testing the end product," and "we found we could establish a better system."

Mandates followed outbreak by outbreak: meat and poultry after Jack in the Box (**July 25 1996**), seafood (in force **December 18 1997**), juice (2001). The Food Safety Modernization Act of 2011 carried the logic, renamed preventive controls, to most other FDA-registered plants.

## What It Cost

**HACCP moved the proof from the food to the paperwork.**

Once safety is shown by the process staying within limits, the record *is* the evidence. An inspector audits the log, not the lot. A plant can make safe food and still be out of compliance because the 3am reading was never written down — and a clean log proves only that the chosen points were watched, not that they were the right ones.

The cost lands on whoever holds the pen. Microbiologists designed the system; shift supervisors and sanitation crews operate it, entry by entry.
