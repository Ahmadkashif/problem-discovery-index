## The Problem That Came First

Before 1891 a tattoo in New York was put in by hand. The artist dipped needles in ink and drove them into the skin one stroke at a time, and every line on the customer's arm was a line of the artist's own wrist motion.

A larger piece meant a longer sitting and fewer customers per day, in Bowery shops that sold to people who wanted to walk out finished. The binding constraint was not design or demand. It was punctures per minute.

## What Got Built

A tattooing machine built around a tube. O'Reilly's patent describes a hollow tubular handle carrying a rod "geared with an electromotor" mounted on the handle, driving a bar of needles up and down — five in the drawing, though the text says it could be one or any number "to suit circumstances". An ink reservoir sits along the lower end of the handle, so the needles pick up ink on every stroke, and an extension of that reservoir "forms a gage to regulate the depth" the points go in.

Those three pieces — powered reciprocation, a feed of ink at the point, and a fixed depth — are the tattoo machine. Everything since is a change of motor.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Samuel O'Reilly, a working tattooer at 5 Chatham Square on the Bowery, and the reason is that he was holding both halves of the problem.

The mechanism already existed and had nothing to do with skin. Thomas Edison's US Patent 180,857, *Improvement in Autographic Printing*, granted 8 August 1876, was an office duplicator: a hand-held pen whose motor drove a needle fast enough — Wikipedia gives 50 punctures a second — to perforate a paper stencil as you wrote, which a press then inked through to make copies. By around 1880 it was being displaced by typewriters.

Edison's market was clerks copying letters. Nobody selling to clerks had a reason to put ink behind the needle or limit how deep it went. A tattooer did: a pen that punched holes in paper was already most of a machine that punched holes in skin, and the missing parts — reservoir and gauge — were exactly what his trade needed.

That is why it was a Bowery shop and not Edison's company. The patent is O'Reilly's alone; no manufacturer is named on it.

## What It Cost

The patent did not decide what a tattoo machine would become. Per Wikipedia, twenty days after O'Reilly filed, Thomas Riley in London built a single-coil machine from a modified doorbell assembly in a brass box, and Alfred Charles South later patented the two-coil layout. The electromagnetic coil machine, not O'Reilly's motor, became the standard for most of the following century; a new rotary design did not reappear until Manfred Kohrs in 1978.

What O'Reilly's shape did fix was weight and posture. Early machines were heavy enough that, per the same source, springs hung from the ceiling to take the load off the artist's arm. The trade accepted a machine the artist held against gravity for hours, because it multiplied what the hand could do.

The second trade-off is structural rather than documented: once the needle was a powered instrument feeding ink into punctured skin, customer after customer, the instrument itself became the thing that had to be kept clean.

## What You Still Touch

Every tattoo sitting today is still a hand-held reciprocating needle bar with ink at the point and a set depth — the three claims in an 1891 patent. The artist still holds it for hours, and the health inspector still asks how the parts that touched skin were handled.
