## The Problem That Came First

A buyer paying for an opinion on a house could not tell what the opinion covered.

In the mid-1970s there was no such job title. Ron Passaro, who started inspecting in 1975, has said that *"the term 'home inspection' did not seem to exist. I was a home builder and inspected my homes and other homes if asked."* Whoever walked the house — a builder, an engineer, a handyman — decided how far to look, and wrote whatever they liked.

That made the product hard to compare and dangerous to sell. **An inspector who missed a cracked heat exchanger could be told he should have found it, and nothing written down said otherwise.** A buyer could not tell a thorough inspection from a walk-through. And real estate agents, who stood to lose deals, were not keen to recommend anyone.

## What Got Built

A scope document: a written definition of the inspection itself.

The ASHI Standard of Practice lists the house as systems — structure, exterior, roofing, plumbing, electrical, heating, air conditioning, interiors, insulation and ventilation, fireplaces — and for each says what the inspector *shall* inspect and describe and what they are *not required* to do. The current edition keeps the founding logic in two phrases: the inspection covers **"readily accessible," visually observable installed systems and components**, and it is **"not technically exhaustive."** The report must name systems that are "not functioning properly, significantly deficient, unsafe, or are near the end of their service lives," with a recommendation to correct or monitor.

That is the shape every home inspection report still takes: a tour of systems, a list of deficiencies, and a boundary around what was not looked at.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**The inspectors themselves, because they needed a defensible boundary before anyone else needed a product.**

Passaro gathered six to ten inspectors in his Stamford, Connecticut office, then in White Plains, and in 1976 a first national meeting at Rutgers University's School of Engineering drew about a hundred people — inspectors meeting each other for the first time. ASHI was formed that year; Passaro holds member number 1 and was its first president.

The association's first order of business was the standard. **John Heyn, charter member number 5, chaired a Standards Committee of about a dozen that met monthly in New York City**; ASHI's own account says it took a year, and the Standard of Practice and Code of Ethics were presented to members in 1977.

Why a trade body and not a regulator, lender or realtor? Nobody else carried the liability. Lenders had appraisers; agents had an interest in clean deals; no state licensed the work until Texas began regulating inspectors in 1985, by secondary accounts. For inspectors, a published scope did two jobs at once: it made the service a named, comparable product a buyer could ask for, and it drew the line a court could hold them to — **and not past.**

## What It Cost

**The standard defines the inspection by what it excludes.** "Visual," "readily accessible," "not technically exhaustive": the language that made the job insurable also made the report hedged. Buyers read a long list of disclaimers and a longer list of findings, most minor, with little to say which matter.

It also fixed the report's unit as a finding per system, not a judgement about the house — severity and cost were left to the reader.

## What You Still Touch

Every inspection report — the system headings, the "recommend evaluation by a qualified contractor," the paragraph of limitations — is the 1977 scope, rendered in software. Inspection apps template it; E&O insurers underwrite against it.
