## The Problem That Came First

A house is collateral, and termites eat collateral where nobody can see it.

A lender making a mortgage on an existing home is betting on the value of the frame. Subterranean termites eat that frame from the inside. They leave a sound-looking wall and a hollow sill. The buyer cannot see the damage, the appraiser is not trained to find it, and the seller has every reason not to look.

So the question "is there wood-destroying insect activity or damage here?" came up at every closing where the lender or the local custom demanded it. The only people equipped to answer were pest control operators. Each operator could write the answer however it liked. A lender had no way to know what each report had actually covered.

## What Got Built

One form, with one wording, owned by the industry.

The **NPMA-33 Wood Destroying Insect Inspection Report** records activity or damage from exactly four groups: termites, carpenter ants, carpenter bees and reinfesting wood-boring beetles. It is "a copyrighted document that cannot be reproduced or distributed, in whole or in part, without prior written consent." The inspector fills in fixed boxes rather than writing a letter.

The federal side of the lender's question is documented. In **March 1995** the VA sought OMB clearance for **VA Form 26-8850**, *Wood Destroying Insect Information — Existing Construction*. It was "completed by pest control operators to report the presence, if any, of wood destroying insects and any resulting damage to properties proposed for guaranteed financing." The VA estimated about **262,953** responses a year at 20 minutes each.

Today HUD requires the NPMA-33 on an existing property when an inspection is customary in the area, required by the state or locality, prompted by evident infestation, or asked for by the lender. A set of states use their own forms instead. For new construction, the matching **NPMA-99a** (builder's guarantee) and **NPMA-99b** (service record) have been through OMB review.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The trade association, because the form's wording is where its members' liability lives.

NPMA was established in **1933** and is headquartered in Fairfax, Virginia. It represents the firms that do the inspecting. A lender or agency could, and the VA did, write its own form. But a form written by a lender tells the inspector what to certify. A form written by the operators' association limits what an inspection covers to terms its members can defend: four named insect groups and a fixed set of findings. Keeping it copyrighted means the association, not each lender, controls those terms.

The association also gains something no single firm could get alone. One report, accepted by HUD and lenders nationally, turns a stream of letters that each carried their own liability into a standard product that any licensed firm can sell.

I could not establish when the NPMA-33 was first issued, whether it directly replaced VA 26-8850, or whether the association was still named the National Pest *Control* Association at the time. The builder is therefore keyed under the name printed on the form.

## What It Cost

**The report is sold by the firm that sells the cure.** The inspector who ticks "evidence of active infestation" works for a company that treats termites. The form makes findings comparable. It does nothing about the incentive behind them.

It also limits what the inspector is asked. The form says whether activity or damage was observed. It does not say what the damage costs to repair or whether the structure is sound.

And the form changes on the association's schedule. After the **2019** revision, every earlier version became obsolete on **31 December 2019**, and every firm's reporting had to change with it.

## What You Still Touch

Every termite letter at a closing is an NPMA-33, or a state form written for the same purpose. The pressure on the person filling it in is the one the form left untouched.
