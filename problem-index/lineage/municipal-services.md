## The Problem That Came First

A city cannot put its money in one pile, because almost none of it is its own to spend.

A bond floated for a bridge may be spent on that bridge. A gas-tax allocation may be spent on roads. Water revenue belongs to the water system. Moving a dollar from one to another is not a poor managerial decision — **it is a breach of the instrument that produced the dollar**, and in the early twentieth century it was also the commonest form of municipal graft.

The American reform movement formed around exactly that: the National Municipal League was organised in 1894 in response to publicised corruption in city finances, and by 1901 had a committee on uniform municipal accounting. The problem it attacked was not profitability. A city has no profit. **It was proving that money went where it had been told to go.**

## What Got Built

The fund: a self-balancing set of books — its own assets, liabilities and fund balance — opened for each legal restriction the money arrives with.

A city therefore does not keep accounts. It keeps *many sets*, and closes all of them. The structure that hardened into GAAP names **eight fund types**: general, special revenue, capital projects, debt service, enterprise, internal service, trust and agency. Governmental funds run on **modified accrual** — what is available to spend now; proprietary funds such as water run on full accrual, like a business.

**The Municipal Finance Officers Association — GFOA's name from 1932 — created the National Committee on Municipal Accounting in 1934.** Its **1936 Bulletin No. 6, *Municipal Accounting Statements***, is regarded as the prototype Blue Book; Bulletin No. 14 followed in **1951**; and the **1968 GAAFR** became the primary authoritative statement of GAAP for state and local government. **NCGA Statement 1 (1979)** restated it with minor revision, and the GASB inherited the principles from 1984.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The finance officers wrote it themselves, and the reason is that the standard's purpose is **exoneration rather than measurement**.

A commercial standard-setter optimises for comparability to an investor. A city comptroller needs something narrower: a form of accounts in which "we spent the sewer bond on the sewer" is a statement the books themselves make, auditable by someone hostile. Only the people held to that proof knew which proofs were actually demanded of them.

GFOA has been that constituency since **1906**, when it was founded as the National Association of Comptrollers and Accounting Officers. Neither the accounting profession nor the federal government built this. The people who had to survive the audit did.

## What It Cost

**Fund accounting can tell you where every dollar went and cannot tell you what you own.**

General infrastructure — roads, bridges, culverts, sewer mains — sat outside the reported balance sheet entirely. **GASB Statement No. 34, issued in 1999, required general infrastructure assets to be reported for the first time**, either depreciated or under a "modified approach" that lets a government expense preservation instead, provided it performs a **documented, replicable condition assessment at least every three years**. Few take that option: it presumes asset data most cities never had a reason to collect.

Six decades of standard-setting had never once required a city to know the state of its own pipes. So it didn't — and the knowledge stayed in the head of the crew foreman who remembers which main breaks.

## What You Still Touch

Your water bill arrives from a different department, in a different system, from your property tax bill. That is not disorganisation. It is an enterprise fund and a general fund, kept apart on purpose since 1936.
