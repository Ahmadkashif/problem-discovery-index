## The Problem That Came First

A car in a ditch took half a dozen men to get out.

In the 1910s motor cars were spreading faster than roads that could hold them. When one ran off the road there was no machine to recover it. There were ropes, blocks and as many people as the garage could send. **Recovery was labour-intensive, slow and risky to the wreck**, since hauling an overturned car by hand could damage it further.

## What Got Built

A lifting crane that could be driven to the wreck.

Wikipedia and the Chattanooga towing museum tell the story like this. In **1916** Ernest Holmes Sr., a Chattanooga garage worker, needed blocks, ropes and six men to pull a car out of a creek, and decided to mount the job on a vehicle.

The patent record is firmer than the story. Holmes filed on **November 16 1917**, and US Patent 1,254,804, *Hoisting Apparatus for Automobiles*, was granted on **January 29 1918**. It describes a hoist mounted on an automobile, braced across the vehicle "doing away with an oscillatory movement even though the cranes … project at right angles to the side." He applied for a reissue in December 1918. **Reissue 14,720, *Derrick-Truck*, was granted September 2 1919.** His 1920 follow-up patents define the type as "a pair of booms located in juxtaposition at opposite sides of the truck and each coöperating with a hoisting cable."

The twin booms were the key idea. **One boom could lift the wreck while the other was set against the ground as a brace**, or both could swing out over a ditch. His 1920 patent describes inverting a boom "for use as ground-engaging braces" and bringing "such automobile into position for towing." One truck and one operator could now do what the six-man crew had done.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

A man who repaired cars and had to fetch them first.

Every filing lists Holmes as an individual inventor "residing at Chattanooga … Tennessee," with no company assignee on the patents checked. That fits the key used here: the design came from one mechanic, not an equipment firm. **He had the problem as a customer before he solved it as a maker.** Every car stuck in a creek was work for his garage, if he could get it back.

Manufacturing followed. Holmes improved the design and sold the rigs commercially. The **Ernest Holmes Company** became the leading maker in the field, and Chattanooga is now known as the home of the US towing industry. The Holmes brand was one of the first bought by William G. Miller when he founded Miller Industries in 1990. Holmes's grandson Jerry founded Century Wrecker Corp. in 1974, which Wikipedia credits as the first wrecker maker to fit hydraulic towing equipment on every unit. Century also went into Miller Industries, which calls itself the largest maker of towing equipment in the world.

## What It Cost

**It lifted the car by its body.** A boom-and-cable rig hooks and chains the wreck and drags it on its other wheels, which tends to damage bumpers and bodywork. The industry has spent a century developing gentler methods: the wheel-lift, credited to Arthur W. Nelson of Weld Built Body Co. in 1967, and the flatbed rollback, which carries the car instead of towing it.

The subtler cost is the tow truck's dual role. The same truck handles a stranded motorist, a police impound and a motor-club call, and each is priced by a different party. Holmes solved the lifting. He did not solve who pays for it.

## What You Still Touch

The truck-type decision a tow dispatcher makes on every call (boom, wheel-lift, flatbed, medium- or heavy-duty) is a choice among descendants of Holmes's derrick. Heavy recovery still uses the boom-and-cable principle for the jobs a flatbed cannot reach.
