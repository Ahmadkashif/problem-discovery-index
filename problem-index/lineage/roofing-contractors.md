## The Problem That Came First

A roof quote begins with a ladder and a tape measure.

The roofer climbs up, measures each plane, estimates the pitch, walks the ridges, hips, valleys and eaves, and comes down with a sketch from which squares of shingle, underlayment, drip edge and waste are worked out. It takes time on every lead, including the leads that never buy. It is dangerous on steep or wet roofs. And after a hailstorm, when a restoration contractor may have hundreds of houses to quote in a fortnight, **the climb is the bottleneck on how much work the firm can bid at all.**

Insurance made it worse. On a storm claim the contractor's measurements and the adjuster's are compared line by line, and every disagreement about square footage becomes a negotiation.

## What Got Built

A measurement report produced from photographs rather than a climb.

The contractor orders by street address. The service locates the house in aerial imagery taken at oblique angles, reconstructs the roof as a three-dimensional model, and returns a report: total area, each facet's area and pitch, and the lengths of ridges, hips, valleys, rakes and eaves — the inputs a roofing takeoff and its waste calculation need.

**The measurement moved off the roof and into a back office.** A quote could now be prepared before anyone visited, and both contractor and adjuster could work from the same third-party number.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Two brothers-in-law: one who had the problem and one who could write the software.

**Dave Carlson was a roofing contractor; Chris Pershing was a software engineer.** They launched EagleView in 2008 in Bothell, Washington; the company describes the February 2008 launch as the first remote aerial roof measurement service. Pershing refined the software on Carlson's feedback from the field, reportedly using his wife's ornamental birdhouses as test models, and the pair patented their method of building 3D roof models from imagery. Chris Barrow joined as CEO some months later, bringing angel investors and, in his words, taking over "a really fun, really small, underfunded company."

**Why them rather than an imagery firm or an estimating vendor:** the imagery companies sold pictures to governments and did not know what a roofer needed measured; the estimating vendors priced line items but assumed someone had already measured. The report was the join between the two, and it took someone who had climbed roofs to know that the join was the product.

The fastest-growing customer turned out to be the insurer. EagleView merged with Pictometry International of Rochester, New York, in 2013. In January 2014 Verisk — owner of Xactware, maker of the estimating program behind [[lineage/public-adjusters|Lineage: Public Adjusters]] — agreed to buy it for $650 million; on **16 December 2014 the FTC challenged the deal** as creating a near-monopoly in rooftop aerial measurement for insurance, and the parties abandoned it. EagleView then sued Xactware and Verisk, and in **September 2019 a jury found willful infringement of five EagleView patents** and awarded $125 million.

## What It Cost

**The roofer stopped owning the measurement.** A number once taken with a tape became a per-report purchase from a supplier that also sells to the insurer on the other side of the claim.

The report also measures what the camera can see. Pitch estimates, tree cover, recent additions and imagery age all introduce error, and a report's number carries an authority that a roofer's sketch never did — which makes it harder, not easier, to dispute when it is wrong.

## What You Still Touch

Most storm-restoration quotes now start from an ordered report rather than a climb, and the waste factor applied to it is still largely the contractor's guess.
