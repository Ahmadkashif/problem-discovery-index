## The Problem That Came First

In 2009 Jim McKelvey, a St. Louis glass artist, lost a $2,000 sale of glass faucets and fittings because he could not take a credit card.

The card networks were not the obstacle. The stripe on the card and the authorisation behind it had been built decades earlier — see [[lineage/payment-processors|Lineage: Payment Processors]]. The obstacle was admission. As this vault's own history of the category records, accepting cards in person meant a merchant account of one's own, with an underwriting relationship, a monthly minimum, often a multi-year contract and a leased terminal. For a seller with a handful of card sales a month, none of that paid.

## What Got Built

**A read head and a headphone plug.** The first Square Reader was a magnetic read head wired directly to a 3.5 mm audio jack; the phone's headphone socket carried the stripe's signal to an app.

Jack Dorsey announced Square in December 2009. The iPhone app reached the App Store in May 2010, and Square mailed the readers **free** to anyone who signed up. By October 2011 it said it was processing about $2 billion a year, at 2.75% per swipe. The Museum of Modern Art added the reader to its collection in 2011.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Square, founded in 2009 by Dorsey, McKelvey and Tristan O'Tierney. Dorsey, a Twitter co-founder, worked on the software.

**Who designed the hardware is contested, which is why the key is the company.** McKelvey turned to Robert Morley, a Washington University engineering professor with patents on magnetic card reading, to develop a prototype. In December 2010 Square sued Morley's company, REM Holdings 3, alleging McKelvey had been wrongly left off a patent for the reader; in early 2014 Morley sued, claiming he alone had invented it. Square recorded a $50.0 million charge in the first quarter of 2016 on settling.

**Why Square:** the reader's shape is its business model. A company paid a percentage on every swipe needs as many merchants swiping as possible, so the hardware had to cost the merchant nothing and plug into a device already in their pocket. Merchants signed up under Square's own master account — an aggregator, in the trade's term — rather than obtaining their own, so a seller with no processing history could start at once. The incumbents sold terminals, and a terminal business needs the terminal to cost money. Square's needed it to be free.

## What It Cost

**It inherited the stripe's weakness and added one.** The first reader passed card data to the phone unencrypted. On March 9 2011 VeriFone's chief executive, Doug Bergeron, published an open letter calling for a recall, saying his staff had written a skimming app for the Square reader in under an hour. Dorsey called the claim not "fair or accurate"; Square later added encryption, and announced a chip and contactless reader in July 2014.

The second cost is underwriting. Admitting merchants in minutes means judging them afterwards; frozen accounts and held funds are the complaint trade sources most associate with the aggregator model.

The third is depth. A free reader funded by a per-swipe fee is paid on transaction volume, not on the quality of a merchant's decisions — which is why, as this vault's hub records, markdown and buying decisions at independents still run in spreadsheets beside the till.

## What You Still Touch

A card tapped on a phone at a market stall or a boutique counter is running the bargain of 2010: the hardware is nearly free because the fee is not.
