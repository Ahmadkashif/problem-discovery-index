**Builder:** IHRSA
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**

## The Problem That Came First

Anyone could call themselves a personal trainer, and a gym manager had no way to tell which certificate meant anything.

There was no licence. There were instead dozens of certifications, from bodies such as the American College of Sports Medicine, whose *Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription* first appeared in 1975, to the National Academy of Sports Medicine, founded in 1987 by Robert M. Goldman to create "standardized educational programs for the personal fitness training industry" — and a long tail of weekend courses selling a wall certificate.

The distance between them was measured. A 2002 study using the Fitness Instructors Knowledge Assessment, as reported by Wikipedia, found that 70% of the trainers surveyed held no degree in any field related to exercise science, while holders of ACSM or NSCA certifications answered 83% of its questions correctly on average.

The club carried the consequence. A trainer on the floor works under the club's name, with the club's members. Choosing whom to let in meant judging certificates nobody on the hiring side was equipped to judge.

## What Got Built

A rule that outsourced the judgement. In January 2006 the International Health, Racquet & Sportsclub Association implemented a recommendation that its facilities accept only trainers whose certifications were recognised by the NCCA.

The NCCA did not examine trainers. It accredited the *certifying bodies* — checking that an exam program met standards for fairness, validity and reliability. So the recommendation created a two-step test a hiring manager could apply without knowing any exercise science: is this certificate from a program on the NCCA's accredited list?

## Who Built It, And Why Them

IHRSA, because it spoke for the employers, and the employers were the ones exposed.

A trade association of clubs could not credibly run its own trainer exam — it would be certifying its own members' staff. Nor could it rank competing certifiers without a fight. What it could do was point to a neutral accreditor that already existed. The NCCA's parent had been set up in 1977 as the National Commission for Health Certifying Agencies "in cooperation with the federal government" to set standards for voluntary certification; it became the National Organization for Competency Assurance in 1989 and the Institute for Credentialing Excellence in 2009. It was built for health occupations, not gyms. IHRSA borrowed it.

The certifiers moved ahead of the rule. NASM received NCCA accreditation in 2005, the year before the recommendation took effect.

## What It Cost

The gate measured the exam, not the trainer. NCCA accreditation says a certification's test is psychometrically sound; it says nothing about whether the person who passed it can watch a client squat and see what is wrong. That judgement stayed tacit.

It also made a private recommendation do the work of a licence. The profession stayed unregulated almost everywhere; per Wikipedia, the District of Columbia became the first jurisdiction to require personal trainers to register, in February 2014. Credential inflation followed the obvious path: certifiers compete on being accredited, and trainers pay to hold one.

## What You Still Touch

Every job ad for a gym trainer still asks for "an NCCA-accredited certification", and every trainer still works inside a scope of practice defined by what that certificate did not cover.
