## The Problem That Came First

Two strangers had to trade, and the site in the middle had no way to make either of them behave.

A shop controls its own service: it picks the stock, checks the goods, handles the complaint. An auction site that only lists other people's items controls none of that. One party has to send first to someone they have never met. If the item is not as described, or never arrives, the site has nothing to take back.

By early 1996 that was a practical problem for one person. AuctionWeb, a few months old, was still being run in spare time by its founder, and as the community grew into several hundred members he was the one fielding complaints between buyers and sellers. Every dispute that reached him was a cost the business could not scale.

## What Got Built

**The Feedback Forum**, announced on the site on 26 February 1996.

Members left comments on each other after dealing — praise or complaint — and those comments stayed attached to the member's identity. Over time the record became a count, displayed next to a user ID wherever it appeared, so that anyone deciding whether to bid or to ship could see how everyone before them had fared.

The mechanism moved dispute resolution from the operator to the crowd. The site did not investigate who was right. It published what people said and let the next trader decide.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**eBay** — at the time AuctionWeb, a site Pierre Omidyar launched in September 1995 and renamed eBay in 1997.

Omidyar's founder's letter states the reasoning directly. He had been "developing this system single-handedly, in my spare time" and had "dealt with complaints among participants." His premise: "Most people are honest. And they mean well." But some are "dishonest. Or deceptive." The instruction that followed was the product specification: "Use our feedback forum. Give praise where it is due; make complaints where appropriate." Of the dishonest minority: "Here, those people can't hide. We'll drive them away."

**That is why it was a platform operator and not a bank, a payment company or a regulator.** Those parties had ways to guarantee a trade, but none of them had the transaction history. The only party that saw every exchange between thousands of strangers was the site listing them — and a lone founder with no staff had the strongest possible reason to make the users do the policing. The cheapest enforcement available was memory.

## What It Cost

**A public score invites the parties to bargain with it.** If both sides rate each other, each can threaten the other's number. Buyers who had been sent bad goods held back negatives for fear of a retaliatory one — a pattern sellers themselves complained of as "feedback hostage". In 2008 eBay stopped letting sellers leave buyers anything but positive feedback, and said neutral ratings from buyers fell afterward, which it read as buyers now being willing to be honest. Fixing the incentive meant making the record one-sided.

**It also made the platform the keeper of every trader's reputation.** A score earned on eBay does not move with the seller. The record that protects buyers is also the asset a seller loses on suspension, which is why account decisions carry so much weight.

And it measured the counterparty, not the listing. A perfect score says a seller ships; it says nothing about whether a one-of-a-kind item will find the buyer who wants it.
