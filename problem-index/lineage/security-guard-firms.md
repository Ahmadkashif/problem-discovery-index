## The Problem That Came First

A night watchman is paid for being somewhere nobody else is.

The owner of a mill, a warehouse or a pier hires someone to walk the building through the night, and by definition the owner is not there to see it. Nothing the watchman says in the morning can be checked. "I made my rounds" and "I slept in the boiler room" leave the same trace.

So the product a guard sells cannot be inspected. A guard firm that wants to charge for rounds needs some way to show the rounds happened, and it has to be something the guard cannot fake.

## What Got Built

A clock that the guard carries but cannot open.

The watchclock is a portable, sealed timepiece holding a paper disc or tape printed with the hours. At each checkpoint on the route a **numbered key is mounted, usually chained in place**. The guard puts the key into the clock and turns it, and the key stamps its number onto the paper at the current time. In the morning, whoever opens the clock can read the night's route: which stations, in what order, at what times, and where the gaps were. Disc models ran 24 hours and tape models 96.

The clock also records whether it has been tampered with. If a disc has more than the expected number of perforations, the case was opened, and the record may have been changed.

The idea is older than the company. The German Clock Museum holds a watchman's clock by **Valentin Kammerer from 1806**. Stationary, electrical and portable designs existed side by side from the middle of the 19th century.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

A clockmaker, because the problem is making a record that cannot be forged, not anything to do with guarding.

**Abraham A. Newman founded a watchclock company in 1878.** On **4 June 1923** it merged with the Watchman's Clock and Supply Company, the Eco Clock Company and the watch-clock division of Hardinge Brothers. The result was the Newman Watchclock Corporation, later renamed Detex Watchclock Corporation. The **Detex Newman** was in production for about 130 years, until Detex stopped making mechanical watchclocks on **31 December 2011**.

The shape comes from who pays. The guard carries the clock, but the clock is built for the person who is not there. It is sealed so the guard cannot change the record. The keys are fixed at the stations, so the guard has to physically go there. The paper comes out only when the case is opened, and opening it shows. A guard firm did not have to build any of this. It bought the clock and sold its record: rounds a client can check.

Detex moved into exit and access-control hardware in **1963**. It moved its operations from Chicago and New York to New Braunfels, Texas, in **1980–81**.

## What It Cost

**The clock proves presence, not attention.** A key turned at 2:14 shows that someone stood at station 7 at 2:14. It says nothing about what that person saw, whether a door was open, or what happened between stations. The record measures the route, so the route becomes the job.

That leaves a cost in two places. The client gets proof of the walk rather than intelligence about the building. The guard is judged on hitting marks rather than on noticing things. Computerised systems replaced the mechanical clock, first in Europe in the early 1980s and in North America from **1986**. They swapped the key for a scanned checkpoint and the paper for a download. The model underneath stayed the same.

## What You Still Touch

Every guard-tour app that makes an officer scan a tag on a post is the chained key with a network behind it. Its reports are still mostly lists of times and places. The pattern across them is the part nobody reads.
