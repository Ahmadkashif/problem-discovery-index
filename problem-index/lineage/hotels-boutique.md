## The Problem That Came First

A hotel is not one business. It is a building containing six or seven.

Rooms, restaurant, bar, banqueting, laundry, telephone, valet — each with its own staff, revenue and costs, and in the 1920s each owner allocated between them however he liked. The engineer's wages sat against rooms at one house and against general overhead at the next. The linen bill might be split by weight, by occupancy, or by whim.

**The consequence was that nobody could read a hotel's profit, including its owner.** He could not tell whether the dining room was carrying the rooms or bleeding them. A lender comparing two properties was comparing two private conventions, and a buyer had no defensible basis for a price.

## What Got Built

A published chart of accounts and a fixed reporting format: the *Uniform System of Accounts for Hotels*, 1926, ninety-one pages, five dollars.

Three statements: a balance sheet, a profit and loss statement, and — the invention that mattered — a **departmental statement of income**. Revenue and its directly attributable expenses are assigned to the department that generated them, producing departmental profit. Costs belonging to no department (administrative, marketing, utilities, maintenance) are pooled as **undistributed operating expenses** and subtracted to reach **gross operating profit**, which sits above fixed charges — rent, insurance, property taxes, depreciation.

That stack is why hotel people argue about GOP rather than net income. It isolates what the operator controls from what the owner's balance sheet imposes, identically at every property that follows the book. The schema is still maintained, and is now in its twelfth revised edition, published February 2025.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

A committee of the Hotel Association of New York City, chaired by **E. M. Statler**. The proprietors' committee seated the owners of the Waldorf Astoria, the Astor, the Willard and the Commodore; the accountants' committee the comptrollers of the Pennsylvania, the Biltmore, the Plaza and Statler's own company.

**Read that roster as a statement of who had the problem.** A single innkeeper does not need comparability — he has one hotel. Comparability is worth money to whoever must compare: an owner running several houses, and the lender underwriting them. Statler was operating a chain and financing new construction. That table was precisely the constituency for whom a common chart of accounts converted into borrowing capacity.

Which is why the standard has the shape it has. A schema written by full-service, chain-owned city hotels encodes one as the default case — and becomes the format everyone else reports in.

## What It Cost

The boutique does not have the departments.

A thirty-eight-room property with a leased-out café, no banqueting and no valet still files a departmental statement designed for a house with six operated departments. Its overheads cannot be spread across them, so its undistributed operating expenses run high as a share of revenue and its GOP margin reads as mismanagement rather than a different shape of business.

The subtler cost is the unit. Because the schema makes the room-night the object of account, the metrics that grow out of it — occupancy, average daily rate, RevPAR as their product — measure rate and volume and nothing else. An independent optimising what USALI can see is optimising price, the one dimension on which it cannot beat a chain.

## What You Still Touch

Every hotel P&L a general manager opens is this document. So is the lender's feasibility study, the appraiser's income approach, the management contract's incentive fee, and the benchmarking report ranking a property against its comp set.

The twelfth edition's 1 January 2026 adoption date means a forty-room independent is restating its accounts this year to a schedule set by a committee it has no seat on — the same relationship 1926 established.
