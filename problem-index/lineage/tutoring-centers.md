## The Problem That Came First

A classroom moves at the pace of the grade. A child who missed one step falls behind on every step built on it, and the textbook keeps going.

In 1954 Toru Kumon, a high-school mathematics teacher in Osaka, found the problem at his own kitchen table: his son Takeshi, a second grader, did badly on a mathematics test. Prompted by his wife Teiko, Kumon went through the boy's textbooks and concluded they gave a child too little chance to practise one thing until it was mastered before moving on.

That is still the tutoring industry's core problem. A tutor meets a child who is "behind in fifth-grade maths" and has to find the missing rung — which may sit two or three years below the grade on the report card.

## What Got Built

Daily sheets of calculation problems, written by hand on loose-leaf paper for one boy.

The design has three rules, all visible in what the company still publishes. **Small steps:** each sheet is only slightly harder than the last, so difficulty rises "little by little". **Start low:** every student begins "from a point that they can do comfortably, regardless of their age or grade at school" — Kumon's own examples include fifth, sixth or seventh graders starting with addition. **No ceiling:** a student advances by mastery, not age, toward a fixed destination — the maths sequence is built around the skills needed for high-school differential and integral calculus.

The company's term for the target is the student's **"just-right" level**. The worksheet itself is the diagnostic: rather than a tutor inferring the gap, the child works down the sequence until the sheets become easy, and the gap is wherever that happens.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Toru Kumon — a mathematics teacher who was also the parent. No firm existed; the worksheets came first and the organisation grew around them.

He had both halves of the problem. As a high-school teacher he knew the destination: what a student needed on arrival at calculus. As a father he saw the failure at its origin, in second-grade arithmetic. A curriculum publisher writes to a grade; a school teaches to a grade. Kumon wrote backwards from the end point for one child, which is why the sequence ignores grade entirely.

Kumon's own history dates the rest. By Takeshi's sixth year he was solving calculus. Other parents asked for the sheets, and in **1955 the first maths centre using Kumon worksheets opened in Moriguchi, Osaka**. An office, the Osaka Institute of Mathematics, followed in 1958; the name Kumon Institute of Education dates from 1983. The first centre outside Japan opened in **New York in 1974**, initially serving mostly children of Japanese families, with a US affiliate in Los Angeles in 1982.

## What It Cost

**Repetition in place of explanation.** The sheets make practice cheap and instruction thin; the student is expected to work out much of the method from the worked progression. Critics call it rote, and psychologist Kathy Hirsh-Pasek has said such drilling gives pre-kindergartners no lasting advantage.

The franchise model paid for its own reach with **depth of diagnosis**. "Start where it is easy" finds *where* a child breaks down, not *why* — a misconception and a gap in fluency both look like a sheet the child cannot finish.

## What You Still Touch

A tutoring intake that starts a child below grade level, and a lesson built as a worksheet set chosen for one child, are both Kumon's shape. The unresolved part is Kumon's missing half: explaining the error, not just locating it.
