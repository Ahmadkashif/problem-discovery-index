**Builder:** US Congress

## The Problem That Came First

A youth league is run almost entirely by people who are not paid: the coach, the team parent, the treasurer, the league president. Every one of them is a potential defendant.

A child is hurt at practice. A coach drives a carpool. A board skips a step in its screening. In the ordinary law of negligence, the volunteer who made the call can be sued personally, and a volunteer's house is worth more to a plaintiff than a league's bank account. The rational response for a parent weighing whether to join the board is to decline.

Congress wrote that reasoning into the statute itself. Its findings state that "the willingness of volunteers to offer their services is deterred by the potential for liability actions against them," and that nonprofits and civic programs had been "adversely affected by the withdrawal of volunteers from boards of directors and service in other capacities," leaving "fewer and higher cost programs."

## What Got Built

A federal immunity with a checklist attached.

Section 14503(a) says no volunteer of a nonprofit or governmental entity "shall be liable for harm caused by an act or omission" on the organization's behalf, provided four things hold: the volunteer was **acting within the scope** of their responsibilities; they were **properly licensed or certified** where that was required; the harm was not caused by **willful or criminal misconduct, gross negligence, reckless misconduct**, or "conscious, flagrant indifference"; and it did not involve **operating a vehicle** for which the state requires a licence or insurance.

The definitions decide who counts. A "volunteer" is someone receiving no compensation beyond expenses and no more than **$500 a year** in other value — and the term expressly "includes a volunteer serving as a director, officer, trustee, or direct service volunteer." So the league president and the U-8 coach are covered by the same sentence.

Signed June 18, 1997, it took effect 90 days later.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

US Congress, through S. 543, passed by the Senate on May 1, 1997 and by the House on May 21 in lieu of its companion H.R. 911. The sponsor was Senator Paul Coverdell of Georgia, a former Peace Corps director — an organisation whose whole model depends on unpaid service.

Why Congress rather than the states: states already had a patchwork of volunteer-immunity statutes, and a national youth or charitable programme could not promise a volunteer the same protection in every state. The act's findings argue the federal hook explicitly — insurance bought "through interstate insurance markets," programmes "national in scope," federal funds that "depend heavily on volunteer participation." A uniform floor was the thing only Congress could supply. The act still defers to states: it preserves state laws that condition immunity on risk-management procedures, "including mandatory training of volunteers."

## What It Cost

**The organization kept all its liability.** Section 14503(d) is blunt: nothing in the section affects "the liability of any nonprofit organization or governmental entity." Immunity moved risk off the volunteer and onto the league — which is why the league's insurance policy, not the statute, is what a board actually relies on.

It also **excludes the dangerous cases**. Driving is carved out; gross negligence is carved out; and the organization can still sue its own volunteer. Critics argued immunity for careless volunteers clashes with the charitable purpose, and that adequate general liability insurance would make the act unnecessary.

## What You Still Touch

Every registration form with a signed waiver, every coach certification and background check a league demands before handing over a whistle, exists because immunity is conditional: the volunteer must be acting within scope and, where required, properly certified. The paperwork is how a board keeps its volunteers inside the shield.
