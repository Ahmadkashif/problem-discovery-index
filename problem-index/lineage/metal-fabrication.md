## The Problem That Came First

A weld hides its own quality.

The fused metal inside a joint cannot be seen from outside without cutting the joint open or imaging it. A fabricator replacing rivets with welds had nothing to show an engineer but the finished surface — and a building official had no rulebook defining an acceptable weld.

The push came from wartime shipbuilding: during the First World War the Emergency Fleet Corporation ran a welding committee chaired by Comfort A. Adams, a Harvard professor. In the 1920s the first all-welded steel buildings went up in the United States, built by General Boiler Co., by one account "to the exclusion of rivets".

## What Got Built

**A code that substitutes proof of process for proof of the joint.**

In 1928 the American Welding Society issued the *Code for Fusion Welding and Gas Cutting in Building Construction*, "Code 1, Part A". It was revised in 1930 and 1937, renamed D1.0 in 1941, and in **1972** merged with D2.0, the highway and railway bridge specification, to become **D1.1, the Structural Welding Code**. Bridges left again in 1988, as D1.5.

The code never asks to see inside the weld. It asks instead for a written welding procedure specification qualified by test, a welder qualified by test, and inspection against stated acceptance criteria. For material under one inch thick, for instance, undercut may not exceed 1/32 inch, with 1/16 inch tolerated for up to 2 inches in any 12-inch length of weld.

Then the concession that made it usable by a small shop: **prequalified procedures.** Use a listed process, base metal, filler metal, joint detail and preheat, and the procedure is exempt from qualification testing. The code has, in effect, run the tests on the shop's behalf.

In **1976** AWS began certifying the person who applies the table — the Certified Welding Inspector.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

AWS was founded on March 28 1919 by merging the Emergency Fleet Corporation's welding committee with the National Welding Council, an existing industry body; Adams was its first president. Its stated aim was "to provide dependable and objective information on the developing technology of welding."

**That hybrid is why it could write the rulebook.** The likeliest reading — no source read gives a rationale for the 1928 code — is that a rule written by one equipment maker would have been sales literature, by one fabricator would have bound nobody, and by a city building department would have lacked welding engineers. A society holding both could publish something others would cite.

The code's grammar shows who it was written for. D1.1 addresses "the Engineer" — the owner or owner's engineer — who "shall specify" the code, any nondestructive testing, and any verification inspection. It is a contract document, shaped so a designer can buy welded steel from a shop they have never visited.

## What It Cost

**The code judges the surface and trusts the process for the rest.**

A structural-welding presentation by Wes Oliphant puts it bluntly: "AWS D1.1 has very liberal visual inspection acceptance criteria!" The same deck notes that prequalified procedures carry no notch-toughness testing and no control of maximum interpass temperature.

On January 17 1994 the Northridge earthquake exposed that bet: welded beam-to-column connections in steel moment frames fractured in a brittle manner. Investigations of the damage led AWS to add D1.8, a seismic supplement, first published in 2005.

The subtler cost is the inspector. A code built on a visual table needs a certified eye at the weld, and that eye does not scale.

## What You Still Touch

A certified inspector with a fillet gauge and a light, checking undercut against 1/32 inch, is applying the 1928 bargain: what you can see, plus a qualified process, stands in for what you cannot.
