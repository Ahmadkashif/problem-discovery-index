## The Problem That Came First

A radio station's product is airtime, and an advertiser without cash cannot buy it.

In 1977 **Bud Paxson** owned **WWQT (1470 AM) in Clearwater, Florida**. An advertiser had inventory but not the money to pay for spots, and settled in merchandise: a stock of can openers. Paxson told his talk-show host **Bob Circosta** to sell them live over the air. On **28 August 1977**, **all 118 can openers sold within the hour**.

Read as a station owner, that hour solved two problems. Unsold airtime is perishable; once the hour passes, it is worth nothing. And a listener who buys during the broadcast turns that perishable hour into margin on goods, rather than a fee from an advertiser who might not pay. Paxson ran with it as the **Suncoast International Bargainers Club**.

## What Got Built

A broadcast whose content was the catalogue.

In **1982** Paxson and **Roy Speer** launched a local cable channel — channel 52 on Vision Cable, also carried by Group W Cable in Pinellas County — selling merchandise directly on air as the **Home Shopping Club**, with Circosta as its first host. On **1 July 1985** it went national by satellite.

The mechanics were three parts: a host presenting goods live; viewers calling in orders while the goods were on screen; and a back office taking and fulfilling those orders. Wikipedia records that HSN's original order-taking system was developed on a **Burroughs Large System mainframe** using the **LINC** fourth-generation language.

The company went public on **13 May 1986** and passed **$1 billion in annual sales within five years**.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

A broadcaster and a financier — not a retailer.

Paxson held the scarce input: airtime, which a retailer would have had to buy. Speer was a lawyer turned Pasco County real-estate developer and utility operator; he supplied the money and the corporate structure. Neither brought retail expertise, and neither needed to. The idea that made it work was a broadcaster's idea: **the selling is the programming**. A retailer on television buys ads that point elsewhere. A broadcaster selling its own goods on air is paid by the programme itself, so every minute of airtime becomes shelf space the channel never has to buy.

That also fixed the format. Because revenue arrived only while the goods were on screen, the show had to make viewers buy *now* — which is to say it had to create bursts of demand, on purpose.

## What It Cost

**The telephones broke first.** The national service began on a standard rotary system that concentrated calls at the front of the queue. HSN replaced it with a GTE system, then claimed that system's inability to handle high call volumes lost it business, and **sued GTE for $1.5 billion**. GTE countersued for slander and won a **$100 million judgment**; the parties settled out of court.

That is the design's cost in one dispute. A format built to produce demand spikes on command inherits a checkout that must absorb them, and on HSN's own account its 1980s telephony could not — a caller who cannot get through while the item is on air is a sale lost with the moment.

**The host became the bottleneck.** Circosta's role — one person presenting, pacing and selling live — made the channel's output depend on one voice at a time.

## What You Still Touch

A seller on a live-shopping app announces a drop, a counter runs, and the checkout either survives the next ninety seconds or it doesn't. The perishable hour, the host doing everything, and the burst the format exists to create are all 1977 Clearwater, rebuilt on a phone.
