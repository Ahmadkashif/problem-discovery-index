## The Problem That Came First

In a salon, the client belongs to the stylist, not the building.

Clients follow a person — the one who knows their curl pattern, their colour formula, their tolerance for change. When a stylist moves chairs, a good share of her book moves with her. That makes the independent stylist, and especially the **booth renter** who pays the salon a weekly rent and runs her own business from its chair, a small firm of one: she is her own marketing department, receptionist and bookkeeper.

Before 2011 her tools were the salon's phone, the salon's paper appointment book or front-desk system, and word of mouth. The salon's software, where it existed, was bought by the owner and organised around the salon: its chairs, its staff, its till. The stylist's clientele — her single real asset — lived in somebody else's system, or in her head.

## What Got Built

A page per professional, not per salon.

StyleSeat gave each stylist, barber or nail artist a public profile — photos of her work, a service menu with prices — joined to her own calendar, so that a client could find her, judge her and book a slot without phoning anybody. Behind the profile sat a small practice-management kit: appointments, a client list, reminder texts, and later in-app calling and messaging. By December 2013 TechCrunch reported 200,000 stylists in 15,000 US cities and more than 5.5 million appointments booked, on a freemium model with paid tiers from $25 a month.

The load-bearing choice is the account holder. **The booking record belongs to the stylist**, so when she leaves a salon her schedule, her client list and her reviews go with her.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

StyleSeat, founded in 2011 by **Melody McCloskey** and **Dan Levine** in San Francisco.

McCloskey came from Current TV, where she worked on digital distribution, and started from the consumer side: she had moved to San Francisco, had hair that needed someone who knew what they were doing, and found that finding, booking and trusting a new stylist meant phone calls, voicemail and salon hours. What she found on the supply side was the business: professionals "did not have a good way to market themselves." Her first recruitment was personal — 30 names, an email, and a presentation at a friend's salon.

Why an outsider and not the salon-software vendors: those vendors' customer was the salon owner, and a product that makes the stylist portable works against the person paying for it. A consumer-internet founder had no such conflict. She could sell directly to the individual professional, which is exactly the customer an owner-facing vendor could not court without undermining its own accounts.

## What It Cost

Portability for the stylist is attrition risk for the salon. Once a stylist's book is hers online, the commission salon's claim on "its" clients weakens, and the booth-rental model — the stylist as tenant, not employee — gets easier to choose.

And the marketplace charges for discovery. Today StyleSeat's New Client Connection fee takes a share of a new client's first appointment; the platform that made the stylist independent also sits between her and her next client.

## What You Still Touch

Search for a stylist on your phone, scroll her photos, and book a Tuesday slot without calling the salon: that is the 2011 decision to key the booking to the person.
