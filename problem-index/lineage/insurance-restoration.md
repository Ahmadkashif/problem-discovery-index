## The Problem That Came First

A water loss is a race against time, paid for by someone who was not there.

The restorer decides on site what to tear out and what to dry in place. The adjuster judges that call later, from paper. **Before a written standard there was no shared test for "necessary."** Did soaked carpet pad have to go? Could wet drywall be dried, or must it be cut out? Each contractor followed his own training; each adjuster second-guessed it from his.

The restorer is paid only for work the carrier accepts. So every judgement made on site was also an argument he would have to win later, with nothing written to point to.

## What Got Built

A book, and a vocabulary inside it.

The S500 was first published in **1994**, revised in **1999**, rewritten in **2006** (the edition that brought ANSI accreditation, per a 2010 commentary), and reissued in **2015** and **2021**.

Its lasting mechanism is two independent scales, as the 2006 edition sets them out:

- **Category 1, 2, 3** — how contaminated the water is. Category 1 comes from a sanitary source such as a broken supply line. Category 2 carries some contamination, like a washing-machine overflow. Category 3 is "grossly unsanitary" — sewage, or water coming in from outdoors — and in that case most porous materials are "expected to be removed and replaced."
- **Class 1 to 4** — how much water there is to evaporate. The class sizes the dehumidification, from a surface spill to materials such as hardwood or plaster that need specialty drying.

**The Category decides what gets thrown away; the Class decides how many machines run for how many days.** Together they turn a site judgement into a label an adjuster can check against a written rule.

I could not establish whether the 1994 first edition already used this Category and Class scheme, or whether it came in with a later revision.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The IICRC — a certification body that began with carpet cleaners, not with insurers or builders.

It was founded in **1972 by Ed York** as the International Institute of Carpet and Upholstery Cleaning (IICUC), by its own account. Secondary sources say it reached into water and fire damage restoration in the 1980s. Its first published standard, the IICUC S001 carpet-cleaning standard, is dated **1991**.

**Why them:** the people first called to a flooded floor were carpet cleaners who owned extraction gear. The IICRC already sold the thing that needed a written standard behind it — a technician's certificate. A standard gives the certificate its content. It also gives the certified firm a document to put in front of an adjuster. (An inference, not a stated motive.) I could not find the 1994 drafting committee or any account of why the IICRC chose that year.

By the 2006 edition, a 2010 bulletin says, the consensus body included restorers, building scientists, insurers, equipment and chemical makers, and training schools.

## What It Cost

**The standard binds the restorer and only informs the adjuster.** The 2010 bulletin says the S500 places both compliance requirements and obligations of responsibility on the restorer. The 2021 edition also states that finding and fixing the source of the water is "the responsibility of the property owner and not the restorer."

It is also, on purpose, not a rulebook. It lets a restorer depart from it if he writes down why. So the argument moved from "was that necessary?" to "is the departure documented?" — and documentation became the job.
