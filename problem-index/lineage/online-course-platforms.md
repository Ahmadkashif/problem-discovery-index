## The Problem That Came First

A free course has no price, so it has no revenue and no proof.

In fall 2011 Stanford put courses online for anyone. Sebastian Thrun and Peter Norvig's *Introduction to AI* reached **160,000** enrolments. Andrew Ng and Daphne Koller, both Stanford computer science professors, offered theirs the same term and founded **Coursera** in 2012 with Princeton, Stanford, Michigan and Penn as the first partners. By March 2013 Coursera alone had about **2.8 million** registered learners.

Two things were missing at that scale. The first was money: the courses were free and the company was venture-funded. The second was trust in the result. A certificate of completion from a free online course said a named account had submitted the work. It could not say that the named person had done it, because nobody had checked who was typing.

The two gaps turned out to have the same answer.

## What Got Built

A paid lane through a free course.

A learner on **Signature Track** paid a fee, verified their identity and received a verified certificate — a document meant to state that this person, not merely this account, took and passed the course. Martin Weller's *The Battle for Open* describes it, as of May 2013, as "a paid-for model, whereby students pay a fee to have verifiable identity, records and certification".

The fee sat, per Wikipedia's account, in the range of **$30 to $90**. In **September 2013** Coursera announced it had earned **$1 million** from verified certificates.

Note what is verified. The identity check answers *who*. The course's own quizzes and assignments answer *what*, and nothing in the sources describes Signature Track changing them. On that reading the premium certificate certified the same assessment as the free one, with a verified name attached.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Coursera, because it was the party that needed a revenue line more than it needed a pedagogy.

The universities supplied content and brand. The learners supplied attention. Coursera held the platform, the investors and the cost base, and it had given the product away. Charging for the course would have broken the thing that produced 2.8 million learners. Charging for the *credential* kept the course free and sold the one thing a free course could not provide: a document an employer could believe belonged to the holder.

That is why the tool took the shape of an identity product rather than an assessment product. Identity verification is a fixed cost per learner, independent of subject. Real assessment of ability — graded projects, proctored exams, feedback on work — is a variable cost per course and per submission. A company scaling across thousands of courses could sell the first. It could not afford the second.

There was a side effect Coursera reported: learners who paid **$30 to $90** were substantially more likely to finish. The fee raised completion. Whether it raised learning, nobody measured.

## What It Cost

**The credential certified attendance with a verified name.** The paid tier bought trust in *who*, and left *what they can do* exactly as uncertain as before.

**Completion became the product's success metric.** Paid certificates made finishing monetisable, so finishing is what got optimised. Averages were already low: MOOCs from Harvard and MIT in 2012 had completion rates averaging **22%**.

**The model moved toward the paywall.** By January 2016 Coursera charged for grades and assessments across most Specialization courses; in December 2024 it ended free auditing.

## What You Still Touch

The "verified certificate" upsell at the end of a free online course is this design: pay, confirm who you are, and receive a certificate for the same quizzes the free learner took.
