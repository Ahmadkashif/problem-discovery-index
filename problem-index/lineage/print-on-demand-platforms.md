## The Problem That Came First

Selling a T-shirt with your logo on it used to mean buying a box of them first.

Screen printing has high setup cost and low marginal cost: burn the screens, mix the inks, and the economics only work at dozens or hundreds of units. So anyone who wanted merchandise — a band, a small website, a club — paid up front for a minimum run in guessed sizes and colours, stored it, shipped it by hand, and ate whatever did not sell. For the thousands of small websites appearing in the late 1990s, each with an audience and no capital, that inventory risk made merchandise not worth attempting.

## What Got Built

A shop that held no stock.

CafePress opened in **August 1999**, according to its own published history, from a garage in the San Francisco Bay Area, with four products: a T-shirt, two mugs and a mouse pad. The first order was a white T-shirt printed with *www.killpopradio.com*. The mechanism: a seller uploaded an image once; CafePress rendered it onto every blank in its catalogue and hosted the storefront; a buyer ordered; only then was the item printed — by digital transfer on apparel and dye sublimation on mugs and mouse pads, per CafePress's shopkeeper documentation — and shipped. CafePress also took payment and handled customer service.

The pricing was the other half of the artefact. CafePress set a **base price** per product covering production and fulfilment; the seller set the retail price, and the difference was the seller's royalty. No inventory, no setup fee, no minimum.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**CafePress**, founded in 1999 by **Fred Durham and Maheesh Jain**, incorporated in October 1999 and funded that December.

Why a new web company rather than a printer: the product was not printing, it was the storefront and the catalogue. A conventional print shop sold runs to one customer at a time and could not profitably take an order for one shirt. CafePress's bet was that aggregating single-unit orders from thousands of storefronts would keep a digital production line busy enough that no individual seller needed volume. Its growth method fitted that bet — the company's history describes an outbound email campaign to 100 new websites a day, reporting a 20 percent response rate. By 2006 it hosted more than 2.6 million shops. Headquarters later moved to Louisville, Kentucky, where production already ran.

## What It Cost

**Nobody who made the design sees the product before the customer does.**

The model multiplies one upload across many substrates the seller never handles — cotton of different colours, ceramic, fabric — each reproducing the same file differently. There is no proof, no press check and no person between the file and the printer except whatever the platform chooses to insert. The same absence applies to rights: a shop anyone can open with any image means the platform, not the seller, is the last point where infringing artwork can be stopped. Zero setup cost moved both kinds of checking — will it print well, may it be printed at all — from before the order to after it.

## What You Still Touch

Every POD listing that shows a flawless mock-up of a design that arrives muddy on a dark shirt is the CafePress catalogue working as designed: the render is generated, the print is not previewed.
