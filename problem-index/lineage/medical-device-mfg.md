## The Problem That Came First

Until 1976 nobody had to ask permission to sell a medical device.

Federal law gave the FDA no premarket review of devices. It could seize a noncompliant product, seek an injunction, or recommend prosecution — all after the device was already in use. The Dalkon Shield, an intrauterine device marketed from late 1970, had by 1975 been linked to at least 16 deaths and 25 miscarriages.

A committee under Theodore Cooper of the National Heart and Lung Institute reported in September 1970 and proposed classifying devices by risk. But any law demanding proof before sale ran into the stock already on the shelf. FDA officials — chief counsel Peter Barton Hutt with commissioners Charles Edwards and Alexander Schmidt — inventoried **some 8,000 devices already on the market.**

## What Got Built

**A notification, not an approval.** Section 510(k) required a maker to tell the FDA at least 90 days before selling a new device. In Hutt's account it "was written as a reporting obligation. FDA had no authority to approve a 510(k) submission." The point was for the agency to know what was on the market so it could classify it.

Beside it sat the phrase that did the work. A new device **substantially equivalent** to one sold before May 28 1976 entered on the older device's terms; one that was not fell automatically into Class III and needed full premarket approval. After the bill's introduction in March 1973, "not significantly different" was judged "not ideal"; the enacted text said "substantially equivalent".

There is still no 510(k) form. 21 CFR 807 Subpart E lists what a submission must contain — the intended use, the device to which equivalence is claimed, and the evidence.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**Congress, and the drafting room is the business case.** By Hutt's account, four people spent two months rewriting the statute: David Meade of the House legislative counsel's office; Steve Lawton, aide to Representative Paul Rogers; Rod Munsey for the Pharmaceutical Manufacturers Association; and Hutt for the FDA. President Ford signed it on May 28 1976.

It had to be a legislature because only a statute could settle the legal status of 8,000 devices already lawfully sold. The answer was to make **the existing market the benchmark**: a new device qualified by resembling an old one.

That fitted the parties at the table. A comparison is cheap for an incumbent with a product history and expensive only for something genuinely new. Congress expected the 510(k) to be "a purely transitional tool for Class III devices," ending once premarket approval was phased in.

## What It Cost

**The yardstick became the previous product, not evidence about patients.**

The tool never retired. At a May 1987 House hearing under Henry Waxman, a memorandum reported that more than 80% of post-amendment Class III devices had entered on a 510(k); the FDA was receiving more than 5,000 510(k)s and about 100 approval applications a year. Until 1990, in Hutt's words, a manufacturer "merely submitted a 510(k) report to FDA and placed its product on the market."

Comparison also compounds. A 2023 study traced the predicate ancestry of Intuitive Surgical's da Vinci Si robot through 2,618 device instances and 50 unique devices, back to a cryosurgical system cleared on June 16 1981 — "predicate creep," each step a small difference from the last.

On July 29 2011 the Institute of Medicine concluded that the process "is not intended to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of medical devices" and recommended building a replacement rather than modifying it again. The 510(k) survives.

## What You Still Touch

Every 510(k) still has to name the device it claims to resemble — a 1976 grandfather clause made ancestry the test.
