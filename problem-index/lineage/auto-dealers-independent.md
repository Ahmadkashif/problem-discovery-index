**Builder:** Carfax

## The Problem That Came First

A used car carries one number that claims to describe its past, and the seller controls it.

Mileage is among the biggest inputs to a used car's value, and the odometer was a mechanical counter a screwdriver could reach. Rollback was organised enough that the **Motor Vehicle Information and Cost Savings Act, signed October 20 1972**, made tampering a federal offence and required a mileage disclosure at transfer. But the disclosure was a statement by the seller, and the seller was the suspect.

The party who felt this hardest was not the retail buyer. **A retail buyer buys a car every few years. A dealer buys hundreds, from strangers, at auction lanes and trade-in desks, and resells them under its own name.** For a dealer the lemons problem is not a one-off. It is a standing cost of acquiring stock.

## What Got Built

A report keyed to a car's identity rather than its dashboard.

The precondition came first. **From the 1981 model year, NHTSA's VIN rule — 49 CFR Part 565 — required a 17-character identifier in a fixed format.** Before that, manufacturers used their own schemes. After it, an Ohio title and a Missouri title could be joined on one string.

CARFAX was the join. **Founded in Columbia, Missouri, in 1984**, it started with a database of **10,000 records** and, **from 1986, sent vehicle history reports to dealers by fax**, working closely with the Missouri Automobile Dealers Association. Once the records line up, a rollback is simply a mileage lower than one captured earlier. The **Truth in Mileage Act of 1986, effective April 1989**, then required the reading printed on the title itself — exactly the record CARFAX was buying. **By the end of 1993 it drew title data from nearly all fifty states**, which mattered because the classic laundering move was retitling across a state line.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**Ewin Barnett III, a computer professional, with Robert Daniel Clark, an accountant from Pennsylvania** — not a dealer, not a manufacturer, not a state.

That combination is the business case. Each state held only its own records and had no reason to build a product for buyers in the other forty-nine. Manufacturers had no stake in a car after first sale. Dealers had the demand but not the data skill. **What was missing was not automotive knowledge. It was the ability to buy, normalise and match record sets from dozens of motor-vehicle departments on one key** — a job the 1981 VIN had only just made possible.

A state dealers' association is where repeat buyers of used cars organise, and it gave a small start-up a paying channel long before any consumer had heard of it. **The first customer was the dealer, protecting itself at purchase.**

## What It Cost

**The report records paperwork, not the car.** "No accidents reported" means no record reached the database; buyers read it as "no accidents", and an absence of evidence became a selling point. Keyed to a VIN rather than a body panel, it left judging condition a human job at the lane.

The second cost fell on the dealers who incubated it. **In December 1996 CARFAX began selling reports directly to consumers online.** A tool built to protect the dealer became something the customer brings to the dealer's desk — which is why dealer trade press was still asking in 2010 whether CARFAX was friend or foe.

## What You Still Touch

Nearly every used-car listing online carries a history-report link or a one-owner badge, usually paid for by the dealer. That is a 1986 fax to dealers, turned around to face the buyer.
