# Lineage: Stock Media Marketplaces

**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-05-commercial-web|5 — The Commercial Web]]
**The tool:** the royalty-free stock licence: one flat payment for an image, reusable across projects without a further fee, first sold on PhotoDisc's CD-ROM image packs
**Builder:** PhotoDisc
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

A stock photograph used to be priced by what the buyer would do with it.

Under the older **rights-managed** model, a licence covered one specified use. Another use meant another purchase. Each sale was a small negotiation over size, placement, run and territory, handled by an agency that had a physical print or transparency in a drawer. The photographer's share was a cut of each of those negotiated fees. The industry standard is usually given as 30 to 50 per cent, and early arrangements often split the fee evenly.

That suited a magazine buying one cover, not a desktop designer. By the early 1990s that designer was laying out brochures on a personal computer and wanted a dozen usable images by the afternoon. Negotiating each use cost more than most of the images were worth.

## What Got Built

A licence, shipped with the pictures.

In **1991**, **PhotoDisc** in Seattle began selling packs of images on CD-ROM. Customers ordered from a mailed catalogue by letter or phone and received a disc. The images came under a **royalty-free** licence: a single payment granted the right to use a picture across many projects, with no further fee per use. Unlike its competitors, PhotoDisc licensed its image packs this way.

The licence carried two structural consequences. It could not be exclusive, because the same image went to every buyer. And the price no longer tracked the use. A picture on a church newsletter and the same picture in a national campaign cost the buyer the same.

PhotoDisc's website launched in **autumn 1995** and let customers download directly. That is the moment the licence met the commercial web. In **September 1997** PhotoDisc agreed to combine with London-based Getty Communications to form Getty Images, in a deal the *Seattle Times* valued at $160.8 million. When the acquisition closed in February 1998, PhotoDisc's library stood at about 60,000 images.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**PhotoDisc**, founded in Seattle in 1991. Wikipedia names the founders as Tom Hughes, Mark Callaghan and Mark Torrance. The contemporary *Seattle Times* report adds Sally von Bargen.

Why a start-up and not an incumbent agency comes down to the distribution medium. A CD-ROM made copying free and unmonitorable once it left the building. A licence that priced each use would have needed per-use enforcement that nobody could perform on a disc sitting in a design studio. Royalty-free is the licence that matches a medium with no meter. An incumbent with a drawer of transparencies and a business built on metered use had every reason not to adopt it. A new entrant with no rights-managed revenue to cannibalise had none.

The founders' own stated reasoning was not established in this session. The argument above is inferred from the licence's terms and the medium, not quoted.

## What It Cost

**The meter came off the use, and with it the link between an image's value and its maker's pay.**

Once price is independent of use, the contributor's income stops carrying information about what the work did in the world. The web inherited that decoupling and extended it. iStockphoto, founded in May 2000, moved to micropayments in 2001. Shutterstock launched in 2003 on subscriptions. Each step pushed the price per use further down and made the contributor's share more of a formula and less of a negotiation.

A training corpus is the extreme case of the same licence. A model that has seen an image uses it in every output, and under a flat or pooled arrangement that use is priced at nothing in particular.

## What You Still Touch

A designer who downloads an image and uses it everywhere without a second thought is using the 1991 licence. So is the contributor whose monthly statement moves for no visible reason. Neither the statement nor the formula behind it has ever had to say what a specific use was worth, because the licence stopped asking.
