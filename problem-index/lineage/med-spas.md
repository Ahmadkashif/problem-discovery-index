# Lineage: Med Spas

**Industry:** [[industries/med-spas|Med Spas]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-06-cloud-saas|6 — Cloud & SaaS]]
**The tool:** BOTOX Cosmetic — onabotulinumtoxinA, a prescription injectable approved by the FDA in April 2002 for the temporary improvement of moderate to severe glabellar (frown) lines
**Builder:** Allergan
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

Before 2002, looking younger was surgery or it was a cream.

Surgery — a brow lift, a facelift — meant an operating room, a plastic surgeon, general anaesthesia and weeks of recovery. Creams and facials were retail and required no licence, and did little to a frown line that is made by a muscle pulling the skin. Between the two there was no product that was medical enough to work and small enough to fit a lunch hour.

That gap is what a med spa is. The business depends on a treatment that a prescription is needed for, that takes minutes, that needs no recovery, and that wears off — so the patient comes back.

## What Got Built

A purified botulinum toxin type A, injected in units into the muscles between the eyebrows, which weakens them for a few months.

The drug came first and the cosmetic label second. Alan B. Scott, an ophthalmologist at the Smith-Kettlewell Eye Research Institute in San Francisco, had been developing it since the 1970s as an injectable alternative to eye-muscle surgery for strabismus; he called it **Oculinum**. The FDA licensed Oculinum on **December 29 1989** for strabismus and blepharospasm. **Allergan acquired it in 1991 and renamed it Botox in 1992.** The glabellar-lines approval — the one that made it a consumer product — came in **April 2002**; Allergan and AbbVie date the anniversary to April 15.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Allergan, and the reason is that it already owned the drug for the eye.

Scott could not get industry to make it at first. In his own account, around 1984 he approached six drug companies, Allergan among them, and all refused: the market for a strabismus injection was small and the toxin frightening. Allergan kept sending a representative to research meetings, became interested in the drug's wider potential, and bought it.

The cosmetic use was then found in the clinic. From 1986 **Jean and Alastair Carruthers** in Vancouver — an oculoplastic ophthalmologist and a dermatologist — began using the toxin on glabellar frown lines, and published on it in 1992. That meant the cosmetic indication grew out of the ophthalmic one. The company holding the licence, the manufacturing, and the relationships with eye doctors was the one positioned to run the trials for a new label on the same molecule. Nobody else could file for glabellar lines without first building their own toxin.

## What It Cost

The product is a prescription drug, and that single fact built the industry's compliance burden. A med spa exists to deliver a physician-grade treatment outside a physician's office, so every state had to decide who may inject, who must examine the patient, and who must supervise — and they decided differently.

The effect also wears off, by design of the molecule rather than of the business, and the business learned to love it: the revenue model is the return visit. That same impermanence is why the industry lives on memberships, loyalty points and rebooking targets rather than one-off procedures.

## What You Still Touch

If a nurse injector in a strip-mall clinic priced your forehead in units, and asked you to come back in three months, you are touching a strabismus drug relabelled in 2002.
