**The tool:** OASIS — the Outcome and Assessment Information Set, the standard patient assessment data set every Medicare-certified home health agency must complete, encode and transmit, mandatory from 1999
**Builder:** Health Care Financing Administration
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** verified for the dated regulatory record; partial for the design history — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

Home care happens in a thousand living rooms nobody from the payer ever sees.

A home health agency has a nurse in a car and a visit note in whatever format that agency used. Every agency assessed patients its own way, so no two agencies' records described the same thing, and **there was no way to ask whether patients of agency A got better more often than patients of agency B.**

It also mattered for money: without a common description of how sick a patient was, a payer could not pay more for sicker patients. It could only pay per visit or cap costs.

## What Got Built

A fixed list of questions, asked the same way in every home.

HCFA's 1997 proposed rule describes the OASIS it intended to mandate as a **"79-item, core standard assessment data set"**, to be built into each agency's own comprehensive assessment rather than filled in separately. The items run from living arrangements and skin status to activities of daily living and medications. They are collected at start of care, at the follow-up assessment around day 57–62, and at discharge.

Two final rules of **25 January 1999** made the comprehensive assessment and the use of OASIS, and the electronic reporting of its data, conditions of participation — effective 24 February 1999, with mandatory collection, encoding and transmission dates announced that June.

Then OASIS became the price list. The **Balanced Budget Act of 1997** ordered a prospective payment system for home health from 1 October 2000. HCFA's October 1999 proposal built the case-mix model from OASIS: 19 OASIS items plus one item on therapy hours assigned each 60-day episode to one of **80 Home Health Resource Groups**. The quality instrument was now also the billing input.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

HCFA paid for it and owns it; researchers in Denver designed it.

The proposed rule says that **in 1988 HCFA contracted with the Center for Health Policy Research and the Center for Health Services Research at the University of Colorado Health Sciences Center** "to develop, test, and refine a system of outcome measures that could be used for outcome-based quality improvement in HHAs." The Robert Wood Johnson Foundation funded related tasks. Developing more than 150 outcome indicators "took most of 5 years," with expert panels and volunteer agencies, and a demonstration was running with 50 agencies by 1997. The 1999 rule dates the data set itself to 1993 and ties it to sections 1891(c)(2)(C) and 1891(d)(1) of the Social Security Act.

Why HCFA and not the agencies? An agency gains nothing from being comparable; a payer that wants to measure outcomes and adjust payment for case mix cannot function without it. The design follows the buyer: short, coded, repeatable by different clinicians, and aggregated nationally — the 1999 rule notes Colorado found it reliable even when a nurse and a therapist completed it at different time points.

## What It Cost

**A clinical assessment that sets payment is no longer only a clinical assessment.** Once 19 items decide the payment group, every answer has a dollar value, which invites both upcoding and audits of it.

The time cost was underestimated from the start. HCFA estimated about 15 extra minutes at start of care on the learning curve, falling to about 2.5 minutes after five uses. The vault's own worker-life note puts the real figure at 60–90 minutes per patient today, after several revisions of the instrument — a claim from this vault, not independently checked here.

## What You Still Touch

A home health nurse spends the first visit answering a federal questionnaire on a laptop in someone's kitchen — Medicare's 1988 outcome project and its 2000 payment formula, sharing one set of fields.
