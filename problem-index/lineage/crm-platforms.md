## The Problem That Came First

A salesperson's book of business was a card file and a diary.

The Rolodex held names and numbers. The diary held the promises — call back Tuesday, send the quote, check in after the budget meeting. The two were never joined: a card did not know it was overdue, and a diary did not know whose history lay behind the appointment. Follow-up was the unit of selling, and it was held together by one person's memory.

That was a problem for the representative before it was anyone else's. A missed call-back was lost commission. Nobody above the rep saw the file at all.

## What Got Built

A database record for each contact with the activity schedule attached to it.

ACT! put a person's details, a notes history and a list of scheduled activities — calls, meetings, to-dos — on the same record, and surfaced what was due today. The original name says what it was for: **Activity Control Technology**. The rename to **Automated Contact Tracking** kept the acronym. Wikipedia's CRM history describes it as a digital Rolodex, and dates its release to 1987.

It was not the first contact manager: Wikipedia names Exsell for DOS, from Excalibur Sources, released in 1984. What ACT! fixed in place was the pairing of contact and next action — the idea that a record is an entry in a to-do list.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Pat Sullivan and Mike Muhney founded Conductor Software in Dallas, Texas, in 1986, with two programmers, Randy Haben and John Maurer, writing the first commercial version. ACT! shipped on 1 April 1987. The company was later renamed Contact Software International and sold to Symantec in 1993.

**Why them** is the part the record does not fully support. ACT! was built for the individual representative on a personal computer — no server, no manager, no company-wide database — and sold to the person whose commission depended on follow-up. That buyer, not an IT department, is what shaped the product. Sullivan was later named in 1998 by *Sales & Marketing Management* as one of the 80 most influential people in sales and marketing history, but I could not establish the founders' careers before 1986 or their stated reason for building it.

The sequel is telling. Sullivan founded SalesLogix in 1995, a sales force automation company that bought ACT! back from Symantec in 1999. Siebel's CRM, dated by Wikipedia to 1993, turned the same record into something a company owned.

## What It Cost

**The record was designed to serve the person typing into it.** In ACT! that was coherent: the rep entered data and the rep got reminders.

When the contact-and-activity record moved onto a shared server, the typing stayed with the rep and the benefit moved upward to managers, forecasts and pipeline reviews. The data-entry burden that once paid the rep back now paid someone else. That is the root of stale close dates, optimistic stages and blank fields: the incentive built into a single-user tool did not survive becoming a shared one.

## What You Still Touch

Every CRM still opens a contact on a timeline of logged activities and a list of open tasks — ACT!'s layout. The rep still types it in, and the forecast built on it is still distrusted, because the person entering the data is not the person using it.
