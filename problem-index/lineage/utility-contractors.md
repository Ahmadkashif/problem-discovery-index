# Lineage: Utility Contractors

**Builder:** American Public Works Association
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

Nobody who digs a street owns what is under it.

A city street carries gas mains, electric duct banks, telephone and cable conduit, water and sewer — each laid by a different owner, at a different time, to its own drawings. A contractor with a backhoe cannot see any of it and has no standing to ask each owner separately. The first fix was a telephone number: in **1964** the Rochester, New York utilities formed a Utility Coordinating Committee so that an excavator could request a stake-out with **one call**, routed through Rochester Gas and Electric's customer service department to every participating owner.

That solved *asking*. It did not solve *answering*. Once five owners each sent a locator to the same intersection, the street was covered in marks from five organisations, and the excavator had to know which paint meant which pipe.

## What Got Built

A colour vocabulary for temporary marks on the ground. Each utility class gets one safety colour drawn from the ANSI safety-colour standard — **red** electric, **yellow** gas, oil and steam, **orange** communications, **blue** potable water, **purple** reclaimed water, **green** sewer and drain, **pink** temporary survey, **white** the excavator's own proposed dig area.

It is deliberately dumb. Paint, flags or chalk; no depth, no size, no owner name required. Its entire job is to let a machine operator who has never seen the drawings know, from the cab, what kind of thing is under a line — and therefore how badly a strike will go.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

The American Public Works Association, through its **Utility Location and Coordination Council (ULCC)**, and the reason is who owns the surface.

Every other party in the street is a tenant with one asset class to protect. The gas utility cares about gas; the telephone company about its conduit. **The public-works department is the only party whose asset is the right-of-way itself** — it issues the excavation permit, repairs the pavement, and sits in the room with all the tenants. A shared code among competitors and monopolies with no common owner could only come from the party none of them could route around. That is an inference from the structure, not a documented founding rationale; APWA's own materials describe the code's purpose, not its origin.

The date is the weak joint. A secondary account places the code's introduction in "the 1970s"; APWA's current guide page is dated 1999. The **June 15 1976** Culver City pipeline strike on Venice Boulevard — the rupture that led California to set up DigAlert three months later — is often cited as having catalysed systematic colour marking, but no source checked shows the APWA code post-dating it.

The federal layer came later and built on the regional one: the **Pipeline Safety Improvement Act of 2002** required a single national abbreviated dialling code, and the FCC designated **811** in **2005**. Its keying belongs to US Congress; it is the call, not the colour.

## What It Cost

The code carries **class, not position**. A yellow line says "gas, roughly here" — not how deep, not how wide, not whether the locator's electromagnetic signal bled onto the neighbouring main. Tolerance zones exist precisely because the paint is approximate, and the approximation is carried by the person holding the locating wand.

Marks are also temporary by design. They wash away, and the as-built record of what was actually found is a separate, often absent, document.

## What You Still Touch

Every painted arrow on a sidewalk is this code. And the field it never had — depth — is exactly where the industry's hardest problem lives.
