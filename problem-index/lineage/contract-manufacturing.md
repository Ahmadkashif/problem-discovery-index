## The Problem That Came First

A contract manufacturer builds someone else's product, so it must prove its competence to someone else — and it has many someone elses.

Defence procurement set the pattern. **The US Department of Defense issued MIL-Q-9858 in 1959**; Britain had Def Stan 05-21 and 05-24; **NATO adopted its AQAP procurement standards in 1968**. Each buyer wrote its own requirements and sent its own inspectors, and a supplier to several buyers complied with several overlapping regimes, contract by contract.

The UK government named the cost in its **1966** quality and reliability campaign, whose report asked for "a more co-ordinated system of vendor rating to avoid the multiplicity of assessments made by each customer." Suppliers were being assessed by any and all of their customers.

For a firm that builds only to other companies' drawings, that duplication **scales with the customer list.**

## What Got Built

**BS 5750, published by BSI in March 1979** — a generic specification for a supplier's quality system, written so that any customer could cite it instead of writing its own.

Its content was deliberately unglamorous — a quality manual, contract review, document control, traceability, inspection, corrective action, internal audit, records. It says what a supplier must *have*, not what its products must *be*.

The second half was the certificate. **The Department of Trade and Industry's 1982 white paper, *Standards, Quality and International Competitiveness*, put BSI's relationship with government on a memorandum of understanding and set out arrangements for certification schemes.** Third-party registration grew up under the National Accreditation Council for Certification Bodies, now UKAS. A supplier could be assessed once, by an accredited body, and show the result to everyone.

**In March 1987 BS 5750 became the template for ISO's 9000 series.**

## Who Built It, And Why Them

BSI, because the one thing the solution required was a party no buyer owned.

To every other customer, one customer's standard is a rival's paperwork. What suppliers needed was a document their buyers would **agree to substitute for their own** — and convening rivals to agree is precisely what a national standards body is for. John Seddon's critical history of the standard describes exactly that: through the 1970s BSI orchestrated meetings of the interested parties, and **key industry bodies that had written contractual documents for suppliers agreed to drop them and reference BS 5750 instead.**

BSI also arrived with form. It had published **BS 9000 in 1971** for the electronics industry and **BS 5179, *Guidelines for Quality Assurance*, in 1974** — "the natural home", in Seddon's phrase, for the quality-assurance debate.

The government had its own motive. By Seddon's account it employed some 17,000 inspectors, and a standard checked by third parties let it stop inspecting.

## What It Cost

**The certificate proves conformance to your own procedures, not the quality of your parts.** The assessor's question, as Seddon puts it, is "do you do as you say you do?" A well-documented bad process passes.

That was the price of generality: a standard every customer could accept had to say nothing about any product, so it said a great deal about documents. In Seddon's summary of BS 5750's elements, statistics gets a single line — statistical techniques "should be used where appropriate."

Nor did the audits entirely stop. OEMs still send auditors before awarding a programme; the certificate gets a factory onto the list, not the contract.

## What You Still Touch

A contract manufacturer's website and quote package carry an ISO 9001 certificate number, often beside ISO 13485 or AS9100. It is how a stranger's factory gets onto an approved-supplier list without the buyer visiting first.
