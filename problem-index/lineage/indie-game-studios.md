# Lineage: Indie Game Studios

**Industry:** [[industries/indie-game-studios|Indie Game Studios]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-05-commercial-web|5 — The Commercial Web]]
**The tool:** Steam Greenlight — Valve's public voting queue, opened 30 August 2012, in which players voted on which independently made games would be admitted to the Steam store, and its 2017 replacement, the per-title Steam Direct fee
**Builder:** Valve
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

By the early 2010s an indie game could be built by two people. It could not be sold by two people.

The engines had done their work; the shelf had not changed. On PC the decisive shelf was Steam, and getting onto it was a decision made inside Valve — a curated approval process, a quality filter applied by the store's owner to whatever reached it.

That filter was cheap when submissions were few. It stopped being cheap when the tools that lowered the cost of making a game multiplied the number of people making one.

**So the constraint was on both sides of the same gate.** Studios could not get a yes. Valve could not afford to keep saying no by hand.

## What Got Built

Greenlight turned the approval decision into a public page.

A developer posted a game — screenshots, a trailer, a description — and Steam users voted on whether they would buy it. Games that gathered enough support were "greenlit" and offered distribution. The launch was immediately heavier than expected: more than 600 games were submitted and some 2.3 million votes cast on the first day.

Shortly after launch Valve added a **$100 submission fee**, with proceeds going to the Child's Play charity, explicitly to cut the noise of joke and fraudulent listings.

Greenlight closed on 6 June 2017. Its replacement, **Steam Direct**, opened on 13 June 2017 and removed the vote entirely: a developer completed identity, bank and tax paperwork, paid a recoupable fee per title, and shipped. When Valve first floated the fee it said developers it consulted had suggested anything from $100 to $5,000; it settled on $100.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Valve, because it owned the only gate worth passing through — and because the gate was costing it money.

No studio, publisher or engine vendor could have built this: the decision being redesigned was Valve's own admission decision, and the audience being polled was Valve's own customer base. Nobody else held both.

The commercial logic is visible in the design. Manual curation meant Valve bore the cost of every rejection and captured nothing from games it never saw. A vote moved the screening work onto players for free and let Valve observe demand before committing shelf space. By Valve's own count, more than 100 Greenlight titles had each made at least $1 million by the time it announced the replacement — games its earlier curated model would likely not have admitted. The move to Direct finished the same trajectory: once the store was large enough, even the vote was friction, and a flat fee did the only filtering Valve still wanted.

## What It Cost

**Greenlight made admission a popularity contest before it made it a quality test.** Vote-begging campaigns, asset flips built from purchased engine-store assets, and joke submissions all worked the system; contemporary coverage records malware in some admitted titles.

Direct traded that problem for a larger one. With a $100 fee as the only barrier, the gate effectively disappeared, and the scarce thing moved from *getting onto the store* to *being seen once there*. The screening job did not vanish. It moved from Valve's staff to Valve's recommendation algorithms — which reward early sales velocity.

## What You Still Touch

Greenlight's lasting idea was that a game should prove demand from players *before* launch. That idea outlived the vote. The wishlist counter and the pre-launch demo festival are, on this note's reading, its descendants — demand measured before release, now feeding an algorithm instead of an admission panel. That lineage is interpretation, not a documented Valve design decision.
