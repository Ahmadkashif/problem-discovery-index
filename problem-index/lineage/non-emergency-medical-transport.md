## The Problem That Came First

Medicaid promises a ride to the doctor, and states have to arrange it.

Federal rules require every state Medicaid plan to "ensure necessary transportation for beneficiaries to and from providers." Every trip has to be requested, checked for eligibility, matched to a vehicle type (sedan, wheelchair van or stretcher car), and paid for.

**The difficulty was Medicaid's own rules.** Normally a beneficiary may choose any qualified provider (freedom of choice), services must be the same statewide, and benefits must be comparable across groups. A state that wanted one gatekeeper to authorise rides and control costs was breaking all three.

## What Got Built

A middleman with a contract, not a vehicle.

Before 2006, according to CMS, a state that wanted to "selectively contract with a broker" had to get a **§1915(b) waiver** of freedom of choice, comparability and statewideness, and renew it every two years. Brokers therefore existed before the statute, but each one depended on a waiver.

**Section 6083 of the Deficit Reduction Act (Pub. L. 109-171, February 8 2006) added §1902(a)(70):** at a state's option, notwithstanding those three requirements, a plan may "provide for the establishment of a non-emergency medical transportation brokerage program … under contract with a broker who … is selected through a competitive bidding process." The statute lists the vehicle types covered: "wheelchair van, taxi, stretcher car, bus passes and tickets, secured transportation."

CMS wrote the conditions into 42 CFR 440.170(a)(4) (73 FR 77520, December 19 2008, effective January 20 2009). The broker must be competitively selected, audited, and run complaint monitoring. It may not refer rides to operators it has a financial relationship with, apart from narrow rural and specialised-service exceptions. It may not withhold a necessary ride "for the purpose of financial gain." The payoff for states was federal matching at the **medical-assistance rate** without a waiver.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Congress wrote the version that states now copy. The model itself came from states running waivers, and which state or firm started it has not been established.

The statute was needed because only Congress could override Medicaid's own guarantees. No broker could contract its way around freedom of choice. CMS's 2008 preamble says states had asked for such tools "for more than a decade."

**The contract has one party running the queue and others doing the driving.** The broker takes the call, checks eligibility and picks the operator. The operators, most of them small wheelchair-van businesses, take the trip at the broker's rate. The self-referral ban exists because a broker that owned vans would send the good trips to itself.

Congress returned to NEMT in the Consolidated Appropriations Act 2021 (Pub. L. 116-260, §209). It wrote the transportation assurance into the statute itself at §1902(a)(4)(A). It also added §1902(a)(87), requiring a mechanism, possibly attestation, to confirm that NEMT providers and individual drivers, "including a transportation network company," are not excluded from federal programs.

## What It Cost

**Operators lost direct contact with the payer.** A van company under a broker is a subcontractor whose trips, rate and proof of service pass through a party earning the spread.

The quality cost is on record. Modivcare, the largest broker (known as Providence Service Corporation, with LogistiCare as its main subsidiary, until 2021), has been widely criticised for leaving patients stranded. Wikipedia cites a state audit that estimated over 40% of customers filed complaints in five months, against the company's reported rate of under 1% a month. Modivcare filed for Chapter 11 in August 2025.
