## The Problem That Came First

A lawn product only works if the homeowner applies it correctly, and most don't.

Before lawn care was a service, it was a purchase. A homeowner bought a bag of fertiliser or weed killer at a garden centre and spread it, often at the wrong time, at the wrong rate, or on the wrong problem. When the lawn didn't improve, the complaint came back to the shop that sold the bag. Nobody sold the outcome. They only sold the ingredients, and the result depended on labour the seller had no control over.

## What Got Built

A calendar, sold in advance.

ChemLawn sold lawn treatment as a programme. A technician visited on a schedule through the season and applied fertiliser and weed control, and the customer paid for the season rather than for a product. TruGreen, ChemLawn's successor, still sells weed control, fertilisation, aeration and overseeding "on a subscription basis". It does not mow. That separation dates from the start: this was chemistry delivered on a route, not yard work.

The usual account is that ChemLawn applied treatments as a liquid spray from a tank truck, which let a single technician cover many lawns a day. I could not confirm that this session, or the number of visits in the original programme.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Garden-centre owners, which is the documented part.

**Paul and Richard Duke**, father and son, ran the Duke Garden Centers. With **Thomas Grapner**, a former employee, they founded **ChemLawn in 1969 in Troy, Ohio**. It became a large service provider in the 1970s. Ecolab bought it in **1987** for **$376 million**. ServiceMaster bought it from Ecolab in **1992** for $103 million and moved its operational headquarters from Columbus, Ohio, to Memphis. It became TruGreen and, in **2016**, merged with Scotts LawnService.

Why a retailer rather than a chemical maker or a landscaper? The following is inference, not sourced. A garden centre is where a failed application ends up: the customer who used the wrong product at the wrong time comes back to the counter. A retailer is the one party that sees both the product and the homeowner's mistakes. Turning the bag into a visit takes the homeowner out of the process, and turning the visit into a subscription turns a seasonal sale into recurring revenue. A landscaper sold labour by the job. A chemical maker sold by the tonne. Only the retailer had a reason to sell the result.

## What It Cost

**The programme runs on the calendar, not on the lawn.** A scheduled visit applies what the schedule says, because the model pays for route density and repetition, not diagnosis. Whether a given lawn needs a given treatment on a given date is exactly the question a subscription is designed not to ask.

The chemistry also drew public opposition. In **1990–91** in Ottawa, ChemLawn agreed to keep a registry of people with chemical sensitivities and to warn them before spraying nearby. It did so years before the city's public health service set up a similar process. In **2001** the company was a party to *Hudson v Spraytech & ChemLawn*, the Canadian case that upheld a town's pesticide bylaw. In **2007** the brand dropped "Chem" and became simply TruGreen, to distance itself from its chemical associations.

## What You Still Touch

Every lawn-care company that sells a numbered-step annual programme and sends a technician on a fixed route is selling ChemLawn's product. The problems that remain are the ones a calendar can't answer: what is actually wrong with this lawn, and whether the standard programme suits it.
