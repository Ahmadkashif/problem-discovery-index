## The Problem That Came First

Federal procurement law said the government had to shop on price. The engineering profession said its members could not quote one.

Agencies had long got around this by choosing an architect-engineer firm on ability and only then negotiating a fee. In **December 1966** the Comptroller General examined the practice against the competitive-negotiation requirement of 10 U.S.C. 2304(g). He declined to overturn it, in words that name the conflict precisely: "the standards of professional practice do not permit the price competition contemplated by the negotiation authority." He promised Congress a review instead. **The April 1967 GAO report on architect-engineer fees** followed, and it covered agencies' handling of a statutory 6 percent cap on those fees.

So by the late 1960s the way federal design contracts were awarded rested on a practice the government's own auditor had excused rather than approved.

## What Got Built

A statute that bans price from the first round.

**The Brooks Act** requires an agency to evaluate firms on "demonstrated competence and qualification," narrow the field to the **three best qualified**, and negotiate a fair and reasonable fee with the top-ranked firm, moving to the next only if that fails. Price is not a selection factor at all.

The statute needed an input format, and that was a pair of forms. **SF 254**, the firm's general questionnaire, and **SF 255**, its questionnaire for a specific project, were unchanged from 1975 until they were retired. **SF 330**, *Architect-Engineer Qualifications*, replaced them from January 12 2004 and became mandatory from June 8 2004. Its Part I is contract-specific and Part II is general, "organized in data blocks that readily support automation."

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Congress, because only a statute could exempt a whole profession from the general rule that the government buys on price.

Representative Jack Brooks, a Texas Democrat first elected in 1952, introduced the Architect/Engineer Act in 1972. It passed the House **276–114**, and Richard Nixon signed it on **October 27 1972**. A later GAO decision says Congress's consideration of the 1967 report "culminated" in the Act, which was intended to codify the traditional selection method. The professional societies backed it: one account says QBS "has long been enthusiastically supported by every professional A/E society."

The timing matters. The profession's *private* rule against price competition did not survive. **In April 1978 the Supreme Court, in *NSPE v. United States*, struck down the National Society of Professional Engineers' ethical ban on competitive bidding** as a Sherman Act violation. The *public* version was already law six years earlier, with Congress as its author, and it stands.

## What It Cost

**If a firm cannot compete on price, it competes on paperwork.**

The approved burden for the old forms was one hour for SF 254 and 1.2 hours for SF 255. The 2001 rule that proposed SF 330 said "experience has shown that these hours are substantially underestimated." It re-estimated the new form at **29 hours per response**, for about 20,000 responses a year, **580,000 hours**. It also conceded that "because of the tailoring required by the form for each project submittal, there are virtually no savings in burden hours by repeat submittals."

That last sentence is the design choice. Selection by qualification means every submission has to be rewritten to fit the project. The data blocks made the form machine-readable. They did not make it reusable.

## What You Still Touch

A federal RFQ asks for an SF 330, with Part I tailored afresh every time. States such as North Carolina run their own "mini-Brooks" acts for public design work.
