# Lineage: Moving Companies

**Industry:** [[industries/moving-companies|Moving Companies]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-05-commercial-web|5 — The Commercial Web]]
**The tool:** Tariff 400N — the Household Goods Carriers' Bureau's collective interstate moving tariff, pricing a move by the shipment's weight and the distance hauled
**Builder:** Household Goods Carriers' Bureau
**Builder in vault:** **ABSENT**
**Verification:** partial — see Sources

## The Problem That Came First

A household move is a product nobody can see until it is loaded.

A freight carrier pricing a pallet knows what it is carrying. A mover pricing a family's belongings is quoting on furniture that is still in the rooms, packed by people who have not packed yet, going to a house whose stairs the driver has never climbed. The price has to be named before the thing being priced exists as a shipment.

The interstate answer, under federal regulation, was to stop trying. Price the move by what the loaded truck weighs and how far it goes, read the rate from a published table, and settle the bill when the weigh ticket comes back. An estimate given in the living room was just a forecast of that later number.

## What Got Built

**A collective tariff: one book of rates for the regulated interstate household-goods industry**, filed with federal regulators and distributed to carriers by a rate bureau in Washington, D.C. Its final incarnation was **Tariff 400N**.

The mechanics were weight and distance. A shipment's charge came from its weight bracket and the mileage between origin and destination, plus listed charges for packing and other services. An economist who priced his own move from the bureau's tariffs in the 1970s found that a 7,000-pound shipment would be cheaper billed at 8,000 pounds — the kind of artefact a bracketed weight table produces.

The estimate therefore did not set the price; the scale did. It was Congress, in the Household Goods Transportation Act of 1980, that first let interstate movers base the charge on a written binding estimate — a price fixed before loading. The weight-based non-binding estimate survived alongside it.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**The Household Goods Carriers' Bureau**, the moving industry's own rate bureau, later operating under the American Moving and Storage Association.

The reason is structural. Interstate movers were regulated common carriers whose rates had to be published and filed, and the industry filed together, through a bureau whose collective ratemaking enjoyed antitrust immunity. The bureau was a party in its own name to litigation against the Interstate Commerce Commission over the 1980 rules, which shows how directly it spoke for the carriers on rates.

**That is why the price book came from the carriers and not from a regulator or a customer.** Van lines and their agents had every incentive to agree a common schedule, and immunity made agreeing lawful. An economist writing for the American Enterprise Institute in 1978 found the collectively made interstate rates 39.5 to 67.2 percent higher than competitive intrastate rates in Maryland.

The collective tariff ended by regulation, not competition. The Surface Transportation Board declined to continue the immunity, and on January 1, 2008 the bureau discontinued 400N.

## What It Cost

**The customer could not know the price until after the truck was loaded.** Weight-based billing made the final number something the carrier measured, at a scale the customer did not attend, after the belongings were already in its possession. That is the soil the hostage-load scam grows in.

It also taught the industry to estimate by weight rather than by work. A cube sheet converts furniture to cubic feet and then to pounds because the tariff charged in pounds — not because pounds predict the crew hours, stairs and carries that drive the actual cost of a move.

And the book outlived its authority. Software vendors now let a mover generate its own tariff from the 2007 rates with percentage adjustments; many carriers publish something closely resembling 400N.

## What You Still Touch

Ask a long-distance mover for a quote and you will still be offered binding, not-to-exceed or non-binding — three answers to the question of whether the scale or the estimator sets the price.
