## The Problem That Came First

Congress created a defence and never spelled out what it took to claim it.

CERCLA, the 1980 Superfund law, made owners of contaminated land liable for cleanup whether or not they caused it. The **Superfund Amendments and Reauthorization Act of 1986** added a way out, the **innocent landowner defence**. A buyer who "did not know and had no reason to know" of contamination could escape liability, but only after making "all appropriate inquiry into the previous ownership and uses of the property, consistent with good commercial or customary practice."

The statute pointed at a custom that had not settled. Buyers, sellers and lenders, afraid of inheriting a previous owner's cleanup bill, began requiring assessments before closing. The name "Phase I" came into use, but the scope of what consultants delivered, one history says, "ranged from a one-page checklist survey to comprehensive research projects going back to early settlers." **A lender could not tell whether the report on its desk would hold up as a defence.**

## What Got Built

A written floor.

**ASTM E1527-93** set the minimum a Phase I had to contain: which information sources had to be reviewed and what the site inspection had to involve. Its core is a table. The **Federal NPL site list and RCRA corrective-action facilities must be searched to one mile**. Delisted NPL sites, CERCLIS sites, state landfills, leaking storage tanks, voluntary-cleanup and brownfield sites go to half a mile. Registered tanks and RCRA generators cover the property and the properties next to it. Records count only if "reasonably ascertainable": public, "obtainable from its source within reasonable time and cost constraints," and practically reviewable.

The standard's stated first objective is "to synthesize and put in writing good commercial and customary practice." It later became law: the 2002 Brownfields Amendments pointed to **E1527-97** for purchases after May 31 1997, EPA's All Appropriate Inquiries rule of November 1 2005 recognised **E1527-05**, and EPA recognised **E1527-21** in December 2022.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

ASTM, through **Committee E50 on Environmental Assessment**, and the reason is in the statute's wording.

SARA did not ask for a scientific standard. It asked for **"good commercial or customary practice"**, which by definition is whatever the people doing the transactions do. A regulator writing it would have made it regulation; a single firm, one firm's method. **A voluntary consensus body could put consultants, lenders and lawyers in one room and publish what they agreed on**, in a form a court could read as the custom the statute meant.

The defence dates from 1986 and the standard from 1993. A federal rule came only after Congress ordered EPA to write one in 2002, and it was published in 2005.

## What It Cost

**A Phase I is judged by its process, not by what is under the ground.** An inquiry is not deficient "merely because" it missed contamination, and assessments are judged on "the reasonableness of judgments made at the time." The product is a legal shield, measured by whether the steps were followed.

**And it goes stale.** A report is presumed valid for 180 days. After that, for up to a year, it can be used only if the interviews, lien search, site visit and **government records review** are redone within 180 days of the purchase. So the same parcel's records search is rerun for every transaction, on the same rings.

## What You Still Touch

A radius report: a map with circles at half a mile and one mile, and a list of every tank, generator and spill inside them. The rings come from the standard's table. The vault records that every Phase I buys one.
