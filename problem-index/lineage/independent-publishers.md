## The Problem That Came First

A bookseller orders by title, and a title is a bad key.

In 1965 WHSmith — bookseller, stationer and publisher — announced plans for a standard numbering system for its books. Its building programme shows the scale involved: its new distribution centre at Swindon, constructed in **1966–67**, held 45,000 feet of shelving "for half a million books" and a sorting conveyor "controlled by a 'Memory Logic' system."

A conveyor cannot read a title page. Titles repeat, authors share names, and the same text comes out in different bindings from different houses. A clerk resolves that by reading. **A computer needed each saleable book to have one unambiguous code** — and no such code existed across publishers.

## What Got Built

Nine digits.

In 1965 WHSmith commissioned Gordon Foster, a statistician then at the London School of Economics, to develop "a computerised filing system as part of its drive to modernise its growing company." He devised a nine-digit code and named it the **Standard Book Numbering System (SBN)**. It "was rapidly adopted by all UK publishers and booksellers."

Then it went international. David Whitaker conceived the ISBN format in the UK in 1967 and Emery Koltay in the US in 1968, and ISO published the ten-digit version as **ISO 2108 in 1970**. Any SBN became an ISBN by putting a zero in front. Since **1 January 2007** ISBNs have had thirteen digits, compatible with the "Bookland" EAN barcode.

One rule matters more than the digits: **a different ISBN is assigned to each separate edition and variation**. The ebook, the paperback and the hardcover each get their own.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

WHSmith, because its machine was the thing that could not work without one.

A publisher knows its own list, and a single shop can order by title and author. The party that needed a key valid across every publisher was the one buying from all of them and moving their books through one computer-controlled warehouse. For WHSmith, the ambiguity of titles was a cost at scale, and the computer project made it a blocker.

It also made sense to let the number escape. A key works only if every supplier prints it, so WHSmith's interest was served by a national standard, and then an international one, that it did not own. Both readings are this note's inference from the sequence — commission, rapid UK adoption, ISO — and not a documented statement by the company.

## What It Cost

**The number identifies a product, not a work.** Because every format takes its own ISBN, one book becomes a hardcover, a paperback, an ebook and an audiobook with unrelated numbers. That is right for a warehouse, which ships objects. It is wrong for everything a publisher does with the work across those objects — rights, royalties, reporting, discoverability.

**And the key is sold by the block.** In the US the sole agency is R. R. Bowker. By a search summary of its current prices, one ISBN costs $125, ten cost $295, a hundred $575 and a thousand $1,500 — $125 a number at the bottom, $1.50 at the top. A code designed for a wholesaler's volume costs most per title to the publisher with the fewest titles, and each new format is another number to buy.
