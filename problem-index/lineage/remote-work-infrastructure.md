# Lineage: Remote Work Infrastructure

**Industry:** [[industries/remote-work-infrastructure|Remote Work Infrastructure]]
**Wave:** [[series/eras/wave-11-covid-dislocation|11 — The COVID Dislocation]]
**The tool:** the global employer-of-record arrangement — a provider's own subsidiary in the worker's country becomes the worker's sole legal employer, runs local payroll, benefits and termination, and invoices the foreign company the worker actually works for
**Builder:** unknown — searched, not established
**Builder in vault:** n/a
**Verification:** partial — ancestors and category entrants dated; originator of the cross-border form not established

## The Problem That Came First

A company in one country wants one person in another. Before this arrangement there were three options, all bad at small headcount: incorporate a local entity, for one salary; call the person a contractor and hope the label survives that country's legal test; or pay informally, with no compliance layer at all.

**The constraint was fixed cost per country.** An entity costs roughly the same whether it employs one person or fifty, so a company hiring one engineer in Portugal and one designer in Brazil faced two full setup costs for two salaries.

## What Got Built

A contract structure that amortises the entity across strangers. The provider's legal employer in each country signs the local contract, withholds tax, enrols the worker in statutory benefits and handles termination; the client directs the work and pays an invoice. Wikipedia's article draws the line against the older domestic model precisely: an EOR "acts as the sole legal employer," whereas a PEO shares responsibility under co-employment.

There is no single inventor. What can be dated is the lineage:

| Year | Artefact | Builder | Place | What it was for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| late 1960s | Employee leasing | Eugene Boffa, Louis Calmare, Joseph Martinez | US | Moving a small employer's staff onto another firm's payroll |
| 1974 · 1982 | ERISA; TEFRA | US Congress | US | Pension rules whose loopholes made leasing a tax shelter; the 1986 Tax Reform Act "eliminated most of the TEFRA incentive" |
| 2016 | Papaya Global global payroll and EOR | Eynat Guez, Ruben Drong, Ofer Herman | Israel | Paying employees and contractors across countries from one platform |
| 2019 | Deel | Alex Bouaziz, Shuo Wang, Ofer Simon | not established — Y Combinator 2019 batch | Hiring, paying and managing remote workers; EOR through its own local entities |

Globalization Partners (G-P), widely described as the category's early specialist, says on its own about page only that it has "more than a decade of global employment experience"; its founding date and founders could not be confirmed and it makes no claim there to have originated the model.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

**Unknown — and that is the finding.** The domestic ancestor has named founders and a tax motive. The cross-border sole-employer form does not have an identified originator in any source reachable this session.

What is established is why the 2019 wave built it. Deel's founders, Wikipedia records, launched the company "after encountering difficulty hiring international workers for their previous ventures." Bouaziz and Wang had met at MIT in 2013. **They were the buyer before they were the seller** — founders of distributed startups, for whom the per-country fixed cost was their own problem. Hence a product shaped as a checkout, not a legal service.

COVID did not create the category; Papaya and Deel both predate it. It poured demand into it. Wikipedia's EOR article describes "an explosion in the use of EORs in the wake of COVID," and this vault's history note records Deel's ARR rising from about $4M in 2020 to $54M in 2021.

## What It Cost

The arrangement buys employment compliance with a judgement the customer cannot see. Whether an engagement is lawful, whether it creates a permanent establishment for tax, whether a termination is valid — each is made by the provider's specialists and surfaced as a status. The provider can pay a worker correctly and still not make the underlying relationship compliant.

And the worker's legal employer becomes a company they have never dealt with.
