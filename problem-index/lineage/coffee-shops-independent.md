## The Problem That Came First

A coffee shop sells a drink made one at a time, by hand, to a queue that arrives all at once.

Grind, dose, tamp, lock the handle into the machine, extract, steam milk. This vault's own worker note puts a busy barista at 250–400 espresso drinks a shift, bunched into a morning peak. The constraint is how many cups one pair of hands can finish before the line gives up.

That is the Italian bar's problem, inherited, with a different cup: not a short shot drunk standing, but a large milk drink carried out.

## What Got Built

No founding artefact — a counter assembled from other industries' tools:

| Year | Tool | Builder | Place | What it was for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1945** | Lever-driven piston espresso machine | Achille Gaggia | Italy | Higher brewing pressure by lever, for the bar |
| **1957 → 1963** | Flat-bottom paper filter, then pour-over commercial brewer | Bunn-O-Matic | Springfield, Illinois | Coffee for restaurant service |
| **1961** | Faema E61 — pump-driven, with heat exchanger | Faema | Italy | Continuous service at the bar |
| **1970** | La Marzocco GS — first dual-boiler machine | La Marzocco | Florence | Separating brewing from steam |
| **1978** | Licence to import La Marzocco to the US | Kent Bakke and partners | Seattle | Getting Italian machines into American cafés |
| **1991 → 1995** | Java Jacket cup sleeve, patented 1995 | Jay Sorensen | Portland, Oregon | Ending double-cupping on hot to-go cups |
| **2009 → 2010** | Square headphone-jack card reader | Jack Dorsey and Jim McKelvey | St. Louis / San Francisco | Card acceptance for tiny merchants |
| — | Paper punch card | not established | — | Rewarding the repeat visit |

The E61's pump-driven layout became "the most popular design in commercial espresso bars".

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Read down the builder column. **Italian makers built for the Italian bar; Bunn built for restaurants; Square built for anyone too small to take a card.** Only one row was invented at an American coffee counter, by a customer: Jay Sorensen burned his hand on a drive-through cup in 1991 and sold sleeves to local shops from his car.

The café owners in the table became importers, not inventors. **In the mid-1970s Kent Bakke and friends bought a defunct Pioneer Square tavern, Hibble & Hyde's, found an espresso machine in the back, and learned only six or seven commercial machines existed in Seattle.** They began importing La Marzocco in 1978. Bakke's company became Starbucks' exclusive La Marzocco supplier in 1988; in 1994 his group bought 90% of La Marzocco and opened a Seattle factory building machines for Starbucks.

**An independent shop is too small a buyer to shape any of this.** It gets what the largest buyer asked for.

## What It Cost

**The shop inherited motions designed for a short shot and ran them at American volume** — tamping, locking and knocking out the portafilter hundreds of times a shift.

The chain that could pay for a different answer took one: by Bakke's account, **Starbucks began using super-automatic machines at all its locations in 2004.** Independents that kept the manual portafilter kept the strain.

The borrowed till records the sale and nothing about the shop — not the rota, not the milk.

A portafilter knocked out against a bin, a cardboard sleeve, a tablet swivelled round for a tip — three industries' tools, none built for the counter they sit on.
