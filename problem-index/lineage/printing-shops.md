## The Problem That Came First

A colour described in words cannot be reproduced by the next printer.

In the 1950s Pantone was a small New Jersey printing company, and its printers, in the words of its company history, "employed a basic stock of about 60 different pigments and used them to mix ink colors in an inefficient, trial and error method." Every job's colour was a fresh mix, judged by eye, and the recipe lived in whoever mixed it.

The cost showed up on shelves. The company history's example is Kodak: "Confronted with film boxes with varying shades of yellow, customers tended to leave the darker shaded ones on retail shelves, thinking the film in them was not as fresh." **Batch-to-batch drift in one brand colour was a lost sale**, and designer, agency and printer had no shared term for it.

## What Got Built

A vocabulary and a recipe book, bound together.

Lawrence Herbert used his chemistry training to cut the pigment stock from about 60 to 12, and then to **ten basic inks**. In 1963 the company issued the first **Pantone Matching System Printers' Edition**: numbered colours, each printed as a swatch on strips roughly 6×2 inches bound into a fan deck, and each paired with a formula — the proportions of which base inks to mix.

The formula is the load-bearing half: a designer specifies a number, and the printer weighs out its recipe from base inks any licensed ink maker supplies. **A matching problem became a mixing problem**, which any shop could repeat.

## Who Built It, And Why Them

Pantone: a printer with the problem, and a chemist in its ink room.

Herbert, a Hofstra graduate, began part-time in 1956 at the Levine brothers' M & J Levine Advertising. By 1962 he was running the ink and printing division at a profit while the commercial-display side was $50,000 in debt; he bought the technological assets for **$50,000** and renamed them Pantone.

Then he wrote to **21 major ink producers**, offering to license them to manufacture the ten basic inks. The company history says all but one signed within two weeks and paid a royalty.

**That is why it came from a printer and not an ink maker.** One ink maker's system would have been a product its rivals refused to supply. A standard every supplier could sell had to come from a party that sold no ink and already held the recipes. That reading is this note's inference, not a documented statement by Herbert.

Unlike the ISBN in [[lineage/independent-publishers|Lineage: Independent Publishers]], this key was never let go. Between 1988 and 1990 Pantone signed licensing agreements with most of the major graphics and design software makers on Macintosh and IBM-compatible PCs, carrying the numbers into the desktop-publishing colour picker.

## What It Cost

**A Pantone number specifies a recipe and a swatch, not a result on press.** The swatch was printed on one paper; the job runs on another, with ink film and water balance still set by hand. The number ends the argument between designer and printer and moves it onto the press.

The reference also decays: Pantone says to replace its Graphics guides every 12–18 months, because pigments fade and paper yellows.

And the vocabulary is rented. After August 16 2022, Adobe phased most Pantone Color Books out of Illustrator, InDesign and Photoshop; full access required a Pantone Connect licence. The standard now sits with the instruments that measure against it: X-Rite bought Pantone in October 2007 for $180 million, and Danaher bought X-Rite in 2012.

## What You Still Touch

Brand guides still name colours by Pantone number, and pressmen still match them by eye:

- [[problems/printing-shops/worker-life-1|🟢 Pressman Color Matching During Makeready]] — 20–45 minutes per job to "match a PMS swatch or customer proof"
- [[niches/printing-shops/commercial-offset-printers/fix|Makeready Time Unpredictability]] — where "PMS custom mix = slow"
- [[niches/printing-shops/packaging-label-printers/buy|Cross-Run Brand Color Consistency Monitoring]] — the Kodak film-box problem, still open: "the label printed today must match the label printed 3 months ago"
