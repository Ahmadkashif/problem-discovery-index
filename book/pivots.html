Time Travel

This is one way of reading the book. You are a junior engineer, and you are dropped into one wave of computing at a time. You only get the tools that existed then. If there is no internet, there is no internet. You live there for a while, you face the problems people faced, and you make their decisions before you find out how they turned out.

1959Wave One · Mainframe & Batch · 1955–1975

You arrive.

You are twenty-four and you have just been hired into the new data processing department at a big California bank. Nobody in your family knows what that means. Most people at the bank don't either.

On the main floor, rows of clerks sit with ledger cards, one card per customer, posting each cheque by hand. When someone wants to know a balance, a clerk walks to a cabinet and looks. When the day's paper runs late, branches close early to catch up. Upstairs, behind glass, sits the machine you were hired to look after. It fills a room. It is so expensive that it is never allowed to sit idle: all day it is fed, and all night it runs.

Across the country, an airline is booking seats by phoning a room where people walk to a rack of cards and check if one is free. It takes about ninety minutes to book a trip. A new kind of system called SABRE is being built to fix that, and it won't run nationwide for another five years.

Outside work, a long-distance phone call is something you plan for. A brand-new bank credit card was mailed last year, unasked, to 65,000 households in Fresno. Most people pay for things with cash or a cheque, and wait days to know if the cheque was good.

What exists
  • Room-sized computers, bought only by the biggest companies
  • Magnetic ink numbers on cheques, a brand-new standard
  • Ledger cards, filing cabinets, telephones, telegrams
  • Overnight batch runs: collect the day, process it all at night
  • The first bank credit card, barely a year old
What doesn't exist yet
  • Personal computers late 1970s
  • Spreadsheets 1979
  • Electronic transfers between banks 1972
  • Barcodes at the checkout 1974
  • The web 1993
  • Anything you would call AI

The one thing everyone around you is short of: time to do the arithmetic. Every decision you are about to face comes back to that.

1950Stop 1 · San Francisco

You run operations at Bank of America, the largest bank in the world.

On your desk
  • clerks
  • ledger cards
  • mechanical bookkeeping machines
  • paper cheques
  • a research institute down the road at Stanford
The problem

Since the war, customers have been writing cheques faster than you can hire people to post them. Some branches now close in the afternoon just to finish the day's paperwork. Run the growth forward and you would one day need an absurd share of California's workforce to keep your own books.

What do you do?

You picked A

You are fighting a curve with a line. Hiring grows one clerk at a time; cheques were growing faster than that. This is the option the bank's own projection had already ruled out.

You picked B

You would be taxing the habit that makes you money, while competitors happily take the customers you push away. Rationing demand is how you lose a market politely.

You picked C, which is close

A computer alone does not help if a human still has to read every cheque to type it in. The bottleneck was never the adding. It was getting the numbers in.

You picked D, what actually won

You saw that the machine was the easy half. The bank did exactly this.

What won

The bank commissioned the Stanford Research Institute in 1950. The machine, ERMA, was unveiled in September 1955 and went to work on September 14, 1959, posting 50,000 accounts a day. But the decisive invention was the line of magnetic digits printed along the bottom of the cheque, MICR. It let a machine read a document the customer had folded, stamped and scribbled on. In 1956 the American Bankers Association made its font, E-13B, the national standard, so every bank's cheques worked in every bank's machines.

Why the others lost

  • Hiring scales linearly. The problem did not.
  • Rationing would have handed growth to rivals.
  • A computer on its own would have been fast at the part that was already fast.

How it changed what people believed

The computer belongs in the back room, working overnight on the whole pile at once. For the next generation of bankers, "the computer" meant the night run, and the night run became how money worked.

The question to keepIs the bottleneck the work itself, or getting the work into a form a machine can take?

1968Stop 2 · California

You sit on SCOPE, a committee of California clearinghouses.

On your desk
  • bank mainframes running overnight batches
  • MICR cheques
  • couriers and sorting machines
  • the Federal Reserve as a partner
  • dozens of banks that must all agree
The problem

Each bank now processes its own cheques by machine, but money still moves between banks by physically carrying paper to a clearinghouse every day. Your job is to design a paperless way to move it.

What do you build?

You picked A

A faster horse. It makes the existing process cheaper but keeps the paper, and with it the trucks, the losses and the delays.

You picked B

The right answer for the 2020s, and a very hard sell in 1968. Every bank would have to rebuild systems that were designed around one overnight run, and all of them would have to agree to do it at once.

You picked C, what actually won

The committee replaced the paper without replacing the process. That is what got built.

What won

The first Automated Clearing House went live in 1972, run by the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco with a group of California banks. Regional groups merged into a national body, NACHA, in 1974. ACH still moves most American payroll and bill payments today, and it still settles in batches.

Why the others lost

  • Faster paper kept the most expensive part of the problem.
  • Instant payments asked every bank to rebuild at once, on computers that were still costly to run continuously.
  • The electronic copy fitted every bank's existing night run, which made it the one option everyone could say yes to.

How sure we areNo record shows the committee choosing overnight settlement on purpose. That ACH inherited the clock of the cheque clearing it replaced is a strong inference, and it is presented as one here.

How it changed what people believed

"Next business day" stopped sounding like a limitation and started sounding like how money works. Generations of customers, and the engineers who built on top of ACH, learned to treat slow money as natural.

The question to keepWhen many parties have to agree, does the best design win, or the one that changes the least for each of them?

1985Stop 3 · Dallas

You run pricing at American Airlines.

On your desk
  • SABRE, a live count of every seat
  • years of booking history
  • unionised staff
  • a full-service network
  • Super Saver fares since 1975
The problem

Deregulation in 1978 let anyone fly anywhere at any price. People Express, founded in 1981, flies no-frills, packs the seats in, and sells fares you cannot profitably match. It is carrying around four million passengers a year and growing. Your costs are higher, and they are not coming down.

How do you fight back?

You picked A

You cannot out-cheap a company built from scratch to be cheap. Your costs are structural: unions, service, network.

You picked B

You would win the bargain hunters and hand a discount to every business traveller who was happy to pay full price. That is how you lose money faster than your rival.

You picked C

The price-sensitive passengers are real, they are many, and they are leaving. Ignoring them shrinks you slowly instead of quickly.

You picked D, what actually won

You stopped treating price as a number and started treating it as an allocation. American did exactly this.

What won

In 1985 American launched Ultimate Super Saver fares, priced below People Express but released in tightly limited numbers by a system called DINAMO. Early, price-sensitive travellers got cheap seats. The rest of the plane was held for late bookers paying several times more. The rule behind it, published by Ken Littlewood in 1972, fits on one line: hold a seat back while the chance of selling it at full fare, times the full fare, is worth more than the discount.

Why the others lost

  • Cutting costs was structurally impossible at the speed needed.
  • Matching every fare would have given away the business-traveller revenue.
  • Ignoring them meant a slow bleed of exactly the customers who were growing.
  • Limited cheap seats needed a live inventory and deep booking history. American had spent 25 years building both.

How sure we areThe INFORMS Edelman Prize committee credited American's yield management with $1.4 billion in extra revenue over three years. The verdicts on options A to C are reasoning from the situation, not quotes from American's planners.

How it changed what people believed

A price stopped being a number and became a decision about how many to sell. Two people in neighbouring seats paying wildly different fares went from scandal to normal. Today every hotel, concert and ride-share price works on the same idea.

The question to keepIf your rival copied your algorithm tomorrow, what would they still be missing?

1985Stop 4 · Newark

Now switch sides. You are Donald Burr, founder of People Express.

On your desk
  • the lowest costs in the industry
  • one simple fare per seat
  • a basic reservation system
  • fast growth
  • a new acquisition: Frontier Airlines
The problem

American has just launched fares below yours, but only on some seats. Your cheapest customers are being picked off while American keeps its full-fare passengers. Burr's own reaction: "This is a shot across our bow! If we don't invent a way to deal with [yield management], we're history!"

What do you do?

You picked A

The right instinct, and almost impossible in time. The algorithm was public, but the live inventory and years of booking history it needs could not be bought. American took a quarter of a century to build them.

You picked B

You are already the cheapest. Going lower only deepens losses on every seat, while American still undercuts you where it matters and earns full fare everywhere else.

You picked C

Growing larger means more seats sold at the one price American is targeting. Scale magnifies the problem instead of fixing it.

You picked D

Probably the least bad answer, and in the end it is roughly what happened, just later and for less.

What happened

People Express had already bought Frontier in 1985 with heavy debt, and it kept flying an expanded network. Burr later said the airline was profitable until American's new fares arrived, then "tipped right over into losing $50 million a month." In June 1986 it was quietly put up for sale. On February 1, 1987, it was folded into Continental Airlines.

Why there was no good option

  • The weapon that beat People Express was built from data and systems, and those took decades to build.
  • Its whole advantage was a single low price. Yield management attacked precisely that.
  • Rapid growth, the Frontier debt and falling service quality had already weakened it.

How sure we are"Software killed People Express" is too simple. Historians also cite overexpansion, the Frontier debt and service problems. Burr's quotes are real; his explanation is one participant's view.

How it changed what people believed

Being the cheapest stopped looking like a moat. For the next generation of airline founders, the lesson was that you also need to know, seat by seat, what you are selling, or a richer rival will take your best customers one fare at a time.

The question to keepIs your advantage something a rival can copy, or something they would have to spend twenty years building?

HomeWhat you bring back from Wave One

You come back.

You lived through the years when doing arithmetic over every customer stopped being impossible. You watched a bank win by changing a piece of paper, a committee build slow money because slow was what everyone already had, and an airline beat a cheaper rival by knowing exactly what it had left to sell.

Now look at your own desk. Your payment still says "pending." Your company still runs a job every night. Somewhere in your stack is a habit that was right in 1959.

Questions you bring home
  1. What are we still paying for a problem that no longer exists?
  2. Is the bottleneck the work, or getting the work into a form a machine can take?
  3. When many parties must agree, does the best design win, or the one that changes least?
  4. If a rival copied our algorithm tomorrow, what would they still be missing?
